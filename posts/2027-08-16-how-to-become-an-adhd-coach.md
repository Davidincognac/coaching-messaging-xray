To become an ADHD coach, you need proper coach training with a specialist ADHD course on top, plenty of supervised practice, insurance, and a clear rule that you never diagnose ADHD or advise on medication. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no licence to get and nobody checks your certificate. What it costs depends mainly on the training you choose, and the prices vary a lot from one provider to the next, so get current quotes yourself before you sign up for anything. The time it takes, from your first course to a steady flow of paying clients, is usually longer than people expect.

![A coach helping a young man sort sticky notes into piles on a desk](how-to-become-an-adhd-coach.jpg)

Nobody selling you a course has much reason to tell you the whole cost up front, so here it is as a budget: the money, the hours and the stages you'll go through, and what I'd pay for and what I'd skip.

## What an ADHD coach does and doesn't do

An ADHD coach helps adults with ADHD find ways of getting things done that work with how their brain works. That means starting tasks, keeping track of time, planning a week they can stick to, and dealing with the shame that builds up after years of being told they're not trying hard enough.

An ADHD coach doesn't diagnose ADHD. Coaches don't advise on medication either, whether that's starting it, stopping it, changing the dose or switching to something else. If a client thinks they might have ADHD, or wants to talk about their medicine, they go to their GP or doctor. If they're low day after day, can't cope, or talk about harming themselves, that's for their doctor or the emergency services first, not a coaching session.

In our research, one person wrote "My ADHD meds don't work anymore". Another said:

> "I'm about to start ADHD meds (modafinil) and feel ashamed, lazy, and 'like an addict.'"

You can help that second person with the shame. Whether that medicine is right for them is a question for their doctor, never for you. [What to put on a mental health coach website](/blog/mental-health-coach-website) shows how to put limits and safety on your website without scaring people off.

Be careful what you call yourself too. In the UK, titles like "clinical psychologist" and "counselling psychologist" are protected by law, and "coach" isn't. Rules differ between countries and US states, so check the rules where you live.

## The money: a rough budget for year one

Prices change and vary between providers, so check them yourself before you buy anything.

| What | How big a cost | Worth paying for? |
|---|---|---|
| General coach training with observed practice | Often one of the biggest costs | Yes, if you haven't trained already |
| Specialist ADHD coach training | Often the biggest single cost | Yes, but one good course is enough |
| Supervision | An ongoing cost, cheaper in a group | Yes |
| Professional indemnity insurance | A yearly fee | Yes |
| UK data protection fee (ICO) | A small yearly fee, if it applies to you | Check the ICO website |
| Simple website and domain | A small monthly cost | Later, keep it simple |
| Video calls and booking tools | Free to a small monthly cost | Only what you'll use |
| Brand photoshoot, logo, paid ads | Can run into thousands | Not yet |

**Training.** This is the big one, and the range is huge. Some specialist courses are short and done online, and some full certification programmes cost many times as much. If you've never trained as a coach at all, you'll need general coach training as well, or a course that covers both. Before you pay, ask how much live practice with real people is included, and who watches you coach and gives you feedback.

**Supervision.** Supervision means talking about your clients, in confidence, with an experienced coach who can spot where you're going wrong. It's worth paying for, because ADHD clients will bring you shame, workplace trouble and medication worries, and you need someone to check you're staying on the right side of the line.

**Insurance.** Coaches don't usually have to have insurance by law in the UK or US, but get professional indemnity anyway. It covers you if a client says your coaching cost them money or caused them harm. Tell the broker you work with ADHD.

**Data protection.** What clients tell you about their ADHD is health information. In the UK that's treated with extra care under data protection law. You may need to pay a small yearly fee to the ICO, and their website has a quick check that tells you. Either way, keep notes locked away or in secure software and only keep what you need. Check the rules where you live.

## The hours: training, practice and admin

Good courses include plenty of supervised practice with real people, not just videos and reading. If you might join a professional body later, check what it asks for before you pick a course.

Don't skimp on the practice. Plenty of people know the term time blindness, which means struggling to feel how much time has passed. Knowing what it means and helping someone who's an hour late for everything are two very different things, and practice is where you find that out. Coach people for free or cheaply, with a clear end date, and ask for honest feedback.

The admin is the bit nobody puts on the course brochure. Notes, invoices and tax records take time every week. With ADHD clients, rearranged and forgotten sessions come with the job, so write a cancellation policy that's kind but clear, and stick to it.

## The stages: from starting to paying clients

There's no set timetable, but this is the order things happen in, and each stage tends to take longer than you'd hope.

1. Training, general and ADHD, often done in the evenings around a job.
2. Practice clients, ideally alongside the training, with supervision.
3. Setting up the business: insurance, data protection, a way to take bookings and payments.
4. Your first paying clients, usually from people who already know you.
5. A steady flow of clients, which comes from people knowing exactly who you help.

Starting part time while you keep another income is a sensible way in. [How to start a coaching business](/blog/how-to-start-a-coaching-business) goes through setting everything up, step by step, and if you're still deciding whether coaching is right for you at all, [how to become a life coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-life-coach) helps with that first.

## Lived experience: a head start with a catch

If you've got ADHD yourself, you know the piles of half-finished jobs and the sinking feeling when someone asks "have you done that thing yet?". People with ADHD trust someone who gets it, and that's a real advantage.

There's a catch, though, and it's the mistake I see over and over in coaches who have lived through the problem they help with. They make their marketing a reflection of their own story. Their website explains how they were diagnosed, what they struggled with and what worked for them. In my opinion, they don't really understand the people they're writing for, because they think those people are them.

ADHD shows up in so many different ways in adults that this is a real problem. Take two made-up examples: a 45-year-old diagnosed last year after their child was assessed, and a 25-year-old who's been on medication since school. Their days and worries are nothing alike. A website built around the coach's own diagnosis will match one of them at best, and only by luck. A broken clock is right twice a day, and that's what you get when your marketing is your own story.

The other problem is how coaches describe things. They fall back on words like "clarity" and "abundance", and people with ADHD don't talk like that. Only coaches do. Here's how people with ADHD described it in our research, word for word:

- "Do I have adult ADD or am I just lazy?"
- "How do you deal with procrastination and time blindness caused by ADHD at work"
- "I find myself too lazy to even get out of my bed and just lay there with my thoughts"
- "How can I improve their perception of me (it is literally my boss who told me he just thought I was 'slow')?"

That last one gives you more to work with than any coaching phrase. Someone whose boss thinks they're slow will see themselves straight away in a homepage about a boss who's noticed and work piling up. Use their words, not yours. Your story still belongs on your website, just further down the page, as the reason you understand them.

## Why general productivity tips don't work for this group

Plenty of people with ADHD have tried the planners, the apps and the early mornings already. When none of it sticks, they don't think "that advice was wrong for me". They think "I'm the problem". That's why the question "Do I have ADHD, or am I convincing myself as an excuse for laziness?" turns up in our research. They've taken every failed tip as proof they're lazy.

So if your offer is a list of time management tips, you're selling them more of what's already failed. What helps is someone who explains why normal advice hasn't worked for them, and then works out with them what does. Some people with ADHD find it easier to get on with a task when someone else is there with them, which they call body doubling. A good specialist course teaches this properly, which is why it's worth the money.

## What to spend on first, depending on who you help

ADHD coaching is still a wide area, and picking one group of people makes you much easier to find and understand. It also tells you where to put your money first. We found 40 books in the ADHD and neurodiversity area, and 22 buyer quotes. One of the two biggest groups was neurodiversity at work (ADHD, autism, dyslexia and similar, and how people get on in a job), with 14 books. There were 9 on ADHD and getting things done, and 4 on women diagnosed with ADHD later in life.

- **Adults struggling at work, with a boss who's noticed.** Spend on training that covers the workplace, because employers sometimes pay. If you help people at work, look at the UK government's [Access to Work scheme](https://www.gov.uk/access-to-work). It can sometimes help pay for support for people with a disability or health condition, so check the current rules before you mention it to anyone.
- **People recently diagnosed as adults, working out what it means for them.** One wrote "Getting an ADHD Diagnosis as an Adult Explained So Much". Spend on supervision, because these clients bring medication questions you'll need to pass back to their doctor.
- **Self-employed people with ADHD who have no boss to keep them on track.** Spend on practice hours with business owners before you spend on a website.
- **Students with ADHD at university.** Work out who'd pay before you spend anything.

Whoever you choose, [our guide to coaching pricing](/blog/coaching-pricing) will help you work out what to charge once you start.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become an ADHD coach?

Not legally. There's no licence for coaches in the UK or the US. Training with real practice is still worth paying for, and some clients and employers will ask what training you've had.

### Can an ADHD coach diagnose ADHD?

No. Only qualified healthcare professionals, such as a psychiatrist or specialist, can diagnose ADHD. If a client thinks they have it, send them to their GP or doctor. The same goes for any questions about medication.

### How long does it take to become an ADHD coach?

Longer than the course brochure suggests. Practice, setting up the business and finding paying clients all take time on top of the training, and plenty of people do it part time around another job.

### How much does it cost to become an ADHD coach?

It depends mainly on the training you choose, and the range is wide. If you've already trained as a coach, you'll only need a specialist course. If you're starting from scratch, you'll need both. Insurance, supervision and a simple website are the other main costs. Get current prices yourself, because they change.
