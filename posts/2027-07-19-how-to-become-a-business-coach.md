To become a business coach, you work out which background you're coming from, you pick the kind of owner that background really fits, you're straight about the experience you haven't got, and then you go and talk to those owners. You don't need a licence, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, and a coaching course is optional. What you can't skip is a good answer to "have you ever run a business?". Plenty of owners will ask it, and the ones who don't are usually thinking it. How you answer depends on where you're coming from, and there are five common starting points.

![A business coach at a whiteboard with two business partners listening](how-to-become-a-business-coach.jpg)

## Why owners care where you've come from

Business owners are handing you time and money they've earned themselves, so they want to know you've got some idea what their week looks like.

Our buying research backs that up. Across the types of coaching buyer we mapped, authority helps the decision for nearly 9 in 10 of them. For a business owner, authority usually means "has this person been anywhere near what I'm dealing with?".

You can hear it in how owners write about their problems. One asked "How do I decompress from feeling overwhelmed by all the work involved in starting my own business, and prevent getting to that point again?". A founder in our research described what they wanted as "Getting a business coach or sounding board... with whom you can confide about your doubts and fears". You'll struggle to be that sounding board if they think you've never been anywhere near their position.

## The questions you ask are what they judge you on

In my own work, business owners don't really come to me with a list of questions to check me out. It's the questions I ask them, to understand their business, that do that job. When they can see I understand their business, that's what shows them my worth and value.

That changes how you should think about "have you run a business?". Your answer gets you through the door, but it won't keep you in the room. What keeps you there is whether your next few questions show you understand how their business makes its money and where it's losing money. Each of the five backgrounds below gives you different questions.

## Route one: you've run a business yourself

This is the background people assume you need. You owned something, and you hired people or decided not to. You chased invoices and you've probably lain awake about a tax bill. Maybe you sold it in the end, or closed it.

When an owner asks the question, you just say yes and tell them what it was.

The risk is that you coach everyone to run their business the way you ran yours. Picture a made-up example: someone who built a cleaning company with a big team could give rubbish advice to a graphic designer working alone, because what worked for them simply doesn't apply. The other trap is spending half the session telling stories about your own business, which is teaching rather than coaching.

So be clear about the size and type of business you ran, and coach owners close to it. If yours was a service business with five staff, start with service businesses of a similar size. If you sold your business, say so, because there are owners thinking about doing the same and we found 8 books just on buying and selling small businesses.

For your first clients, make a list of the people you dealt with when you ran the business, like suppliers, your accountant and other owners nearby. Ring three of them and ask who they know who's stuck where you once were. If you're still deciding which kind of owner to go after, [our guide to business coaching niches](/blog/business-coaching-niches) goes through nine of them.

## Route two: you were senior in someone else's business

Maybe you were an operations director, a general manager or the person who ran a branch. You didn't own it, but you sat in the meetings where the money was decided.

Picture an owner asking you "have you run a business?". A good answer sounds something like this made-up one: "Not my own, no. I ran the operations side of a regional logistics firm with 60 staff for nine years, so I've hired people, set budgets and sorted out the mess when a big contract went wrong." It doesn't dodge the question, and it gives the owner something real to judge you on.

What you won't have done is put your own money on the line. A department budget never kept you awake the way an overdraft and a Friday wage bill keep an owner awake, and some owners will mark you down for that. Don't pretend otherwise.

You're strongest with owners trying to grow into the kind of business you worked in. The person in that made-up answer would do well with growing transport firms, and badly with a life coach working alone.

For your first clients, start with your old industry. Think of the smaller firms you bought from, sold to or competed against, and get in touch with the owners of two or three of them.

## Route three: you specialised in one part of business

You might have been a sales director, a finance manager, a marketing person or an HR lead. You know one part of a business inside out.

An owner who's terrible at sales will get more from someone who properly knows selling than from someone who's a bit good at everything. We found 6 books written just on sales for entrepreneurs and founders, and 10 on pricing, contracts and getting paid, so there are whole books on single subjects, not just "business".

The danger is that you end up thinking your subject is the answer to everything. The sales specialist thinks every business needs more sales calls, when sometimes the owner's real problem is that they're losing money on every job. If you're from finance, coaching an owner to understand their numbers is fine, but tax and accounts advice belongs with a qualified accountant, so check the rules where you live.

Lead with the thing you know. I've sold double glazing, cars, event tickets, book publishing and retreats, so if I were going down this route, selling is what I'd lead with. If you're a specialist, call yourself something an owner would understand, like a sales coach for small business owners, rather than a general business coach. An owner will understand "I help owners who hate selling" far faster than "all areas of growth". [How to sell coaching without feeling pushy](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching) shows what selling coaching involves.

For your first clients, think about the owners who were your customers or suppliers when you were doing the job. They've already seen you do your one thing well.

## Route four: you came from a trade

This route is for people from a trade, like plumbers, electricians, hairdressers, builders or mechanics. You did the work, and quite possibly you ran your own van or salon at some point.

A builder will listen to another builder long before they'll listen to someone in a suit. You know how quoting works and why the paperwork piles up in the evenings.

The hard part is explaining the switch. People who knew you on the tools might wonder why you've stopped, and in my opinion a lot of trades people are wary of the word coach. So keep it simple and true. Take this made-up example of how someone might put it: "I was an electrician for 20 years and ran my own firm for ten of them. Now I help other electricians get their evenings back and price their jobs properly." That makes sense to them straight away, and you might not need the word coach at all.

Then there's charging people you used to work beside. It can feel awkward asking for money from someone you shared a site cabin with. Set one price and stick to it for everyone, friends included, because the first time you do it cheap for a mate, that becomes your price. If you're not sure where to start, [should you show your coaching prices](/blog/coaching-pricing) goes through how to set them and whether to put them on your website.

For your first clients, the people you used to work alongside and the firms you subcontracted for are your first list. Pick three who are struggling with the business side, stick to trades close to your own, and ask them for a coffee.

## Route five: you've coached, but never run a business

This is the hardest route, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. You're a trained coach, maybe a good one, and you want to work with business owners, but you've never owned a business or been senior in one.

The owner asks "have you run a business?", the true answer is no, and some of them will go cold on the spot. What you do have is the coaching itself. You know how to listen, how to ask questions and how to let someone work things out for themselves, and plenty of people with business experience are poor at that.

The coaching world doesn't help here, in my opinion, because it often mixes up coaching and teaching. If you're helping someone find their own answers, you don't need to have run their business. If you're telling them how to fix their pricing, you do.

So be clear which one you're doing. Coach the person rather than the business. Think of the founder in our research who wrote "After a failed startup, I feel useless and not motivated to start again and lost discipline". Your own coaching practice is a small business too, so be upfront about where it's at, and once you've got paying clients and real results of your own, say so. Don't sell business strategy you haven't got.

For your first clients, start with owners you already know through friends, family or local networking. Offer two or three of them a proper conversation about where they're stuck.

## Setting up and saying it out loud

Whichever route you're on, the setting-up side is simple. In the UK you can start as a sole trader by registering with HMRC, and in the US the simplest start is usually as a sole proprietor. It's worth getting professional indemnity insurance, which covers you if a client says your work cost them money. The details change from place to place, so check the rules where you live.

Then put your route where owners will see it. On your website, say who you help and where your experience comes from, in a sentence or two near the top. [What business owners check on a business coach website](/blog/business-coach-website) shows you how to lay that out.

In your first conversation, don't wait to be asked. Tell them your route in one or two sentences, then spend the rest of the time asking questions about their business that only someone who understands it would think to ask. [How to run a coaching discovery call](/blog/coaching-discovery-call) goes through that conversation from start to finish.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a business coach?

No. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so you don't legally need a certificate to call yourself a business coach. Check the rules where you live if you're also giving advice in an area like tax or law.

### Can I be a business coach if I've never run a business?

Yes, but be honest about it. Coach the owner on the things a coach can help with, like confidence, decisions and how they spend their time, rather than selling business strategy you haven't tested yourself.

### What kind of business coach should I be?

Pick the kind of owner your background fits best. A former sales director suits owners who hate selling, and an electrician suits other trades.

### What's the difference between a business coach and a business consultant?

A coach mainly asks questions so the owner works out their own answers. A consultant mainly gives answers and advice. Plenty of people do a bit of both, as long as they're clear which one they're doing.
