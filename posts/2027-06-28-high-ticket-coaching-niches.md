The high ticket coaching niches are the ones where the problem is big and costly, and the person paying can afford to fix it. In practice that means three kinds of buyer: a company paying for one of its leaders, a business owner paying out of their profit, and an individual paying from their own savings because something in their life has to change. Leadership, founder and executive coaching sit in the first group, coaching for business owners sits in the second, and money, divorce and midlife change can sit in the third. But the label on the niche doesn't make anything high ticket. The size of the problem and the buyer's ability to pay do.

![A coach in a charcoal suit listening to a senior client in a bright office](high-ticket-coaching-niches.jpg)

## Why the label doesn't make it high ticket

Plenty of people online tell coaches to "go high ticket", as if it's a niche you choose off a menu. They're not wrong that charging more for fewer clients can work. But they make it sound like the word "executive" on your website does the job for you.

Think about your own money. You'll pay a lot for a builder when the roof is leaking, because leaving it costs you more. You won't pay a lot for something that's a nice idea. Coaching is no different. Someone pays a high fee when the problem is costing them more than the fee, and they've got the money or the budget to fix it.

The book counts below come from our research into what people buy across 117 coaching areas. A book count is a rough sign of how many people care about a problem. It doesn't tell you who has money, and I'll show you where that trips people up. For the busiest areas by book count, see [which coaching niches make money, and a better question to ask](/blog/most-profitable-coaching-niches).

## The first buyer: a company paying for one of its leaders

This is the one people picture when they hear "high ticket". The coaching is for a leader, but the money comes from the company, usually through HR, a learning and development budget or the leader's own boss.

### The niches in our data that fit

Leadership is one of the biggest areas we looked at, with 136 books, and founder and executive coaching has 46.

Here's how the leaders describe it:

> "my teammates don't respect me and don't listen to me"

> "I am capable of managing people but don't enjoy that side of the job. I feel this may be holding me back"

And the founders:

> "a profound sense of alone-ness (loneliness) during the pre-launch phase"

Those are expensive problems. A manager whose team won't listen costs the company money every week, and a founder who feels alone and constantly overwhelmed is making big decisions for the whole company in that state.

### What this buyer needs to see before paying a lot

There are often two people reading your website, the leader who'll be coached and the person signing off the budget. The leader wants to know you get it and that it's private. The person paying wants to know what will change, how long it takes, how they'll know it's working and what it costs.

Credibility does a lot of the work here. When we mapped what helps different types of buyer decide, authority helped nearly 9 in 10 of them. For a company that means relevant experience, who you've worked with (with permission) and what changed. There's more on this in [an executive coach website read by the leader and the person paying](/blog/executive-coach-website).

### The trap

The trap is writing like every other executive coach. We found coaches in this area using lines like "scale yourself as a leader, sharpen your decision-making skills, and achieve peak performance". Nobody describes their own problem that way. The founders talked about loneliness and wanting to "deal with the stress of being a startup founder", so use their words.

Founders also worry that admitting they're struggling looks weak to investors or their team. If your page doesn't say clearly that it's confidential, you'll lose them.

## The second buyer: a business owner paying out of profit

The second buyer is the owner of a small or medium business. Nobody signs off the budget except them, and the money comes straight out of what the business makes.

There are plenty of them. We found 126 books for small business owners and 77 on business systems and scaling. But for a high fee, the number of owners out there doesn't help you much. What you need to know is whether the business already makes enough money to pay you and still feel it was worth it.

Here's an owner who sounds like they could:

> "How do I scale a profitable business to the next level and reach a direct audience?"

They've got profit, and they've got a clear next step they're stuck on. That's the kind of owner a high fee can work for.

### What this buyer needs to see before paying a lot

A business owner looks at coaching like any other cost. What do I get back? If you can show that working with you means more profit, more time or fewer expensive mistakes, a high fee starts to look sensible.

They need a clear outcome tied to the business, a plain package with a start and an end, and proof from owners like them. The best way to find out what that outcome is for your owners is to ask them before you write a word. If you're not sure how to lay out what they get, [how to put a coaching package together](/blog/coaching-packages) builds one step by step.

### The trap

The trap is aiming at owners who haven't got any profit yet. Look at this quote from someone in the startup and entrepreneur group:

> "I want to start my own business... without fear of not having the freedom to go on vacation or spend time with my friends or family"

That's a real worry, but this person hasn't started yet, so there's no profit to pay a big fee out of. They might join a group, and that's fine. Just don't build your whole offer around a buyer who'd love to say yes but hasn't got the cash. A high ticket offer for owners works with people whose business already makes money and who are stuck at the next step.

## The third buyer: a person paying from their own savings for a life change

The third buyer is a person paying with their own money, often their savings, because something big in their life has gone wrong or has to change. The problem can be huge here, but some of these people have savings or a pension pot to draw on, and some are living month to month.

### The niches in our data that fit

Money and wealth has 199 books, which puts it among the busiest areas we looked at. Relationships and divorce has 81, and midlife reinvention has 68.

From the divorce buyers:

> "I initiated my divorce and now I feel so lonely and lost. I went from always having somebody home to nobody"

> "How do I continue my life after having a failed marriage?"

From midlife reinvention:

> "How can I change my life at 50?"

### What this buyer needs to see before paying a lot

This person is spending their own money, so they need to trust you before anything else. They want to see people like them who've worked with you, a real photo, and a clear idea of what happens in the first few weeks. A payment plan helps, and so does a price on the page, so they don't feel caught out on a call.

Be careful about the line between coaching and other professions here. A coach isn't a lawyer, a therapist or a financial adviser. In the UK, advice on specific investments or pensions is regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority, and the US has its own rules, so a money coach should stick to habits, planning and decisions, and send people elsewhere for regulated advice. If someone going through a divorce is struggling with their mental health, point them to their GP or a qualified professional. Check the rules where you live.

### The trap

This is the biggest trap of the lot. Money and wealth has 199 books, so it looks like a huge, high paying area. But look at what a lot of those buyers say:

> "living pay check to pay check"

> "Feeling like you're constantly broke"

Those people need help, but they can't pay a high fee, and pushing them into one would be wrong. The buyer who can pay sounds more like this:

> "When I hit my 50s and started looking at my full financial picture, I realized I was winging it... That's when I decided to get serious"

Both people are in the same niche. Before you put a high price on a money coaching offer, find out which of the two you're actually talking to, and write your website for that one.

## Don't fake urgency to make it feel high ticket

Plenty of advice says high ticket sales need pressure, so you get "only three spaces left", prices that go up at midnight and countdown timers on the sales page.

In our research, urgency and scarcity helped the decision for fewer than 1 in 20 of the types of buyer we mapped. That's tiny next to authority. People paying a lot want to feel sure, and pressure makes them less sure.

It's worse with the third group. Someone who's just separated, or who's 50 and scared they've left it too late, is already under strain. A fake deadline on a big decision is a horrible way to treat them. If you genuinely have limited room, say so plainly. If you haven't, don't pretend.

What works is asking good questions, listening properly, telling them the price clearly and then letting them make their own mind up. [How to sell coaching without feeling pushy](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching) goes through how.

## How to tell if your niche can carry a high fee

Run these four questions on any niche, including yours.

First, who writes the cheque: a company, a business owner or the person themselves? If you don't know, you can't write your website, because you don't know who it's for.

Second, how big is the problem? Is it costing them money, sleep, their job, their marriage or their health, or is it something they'd like to sort out one day?

Third, can they pay? Look at the words your buyers use. If they talk about being broke, starting out or a small budget, a high fee won't work for that group, however good you are. If they talk about scaling, running a team or getting serious at 50, it might.

Fourth, what do they need to see before trusting you with a lot of money? For a company it's credibility and clear results, for an owner it's a return, and for an individual it's trust and people like them who've done it.

## FAQ

### What are the best high ticket coaching niches?

The ones where the problem is big and the buyer can pay. In our data that's leadership (136 books), founder and executive coaching (46), small business owners (126) and business systems and scaling (77), plus parts of money, divorce and midlife change where the buyer has savings. Within each one, aim at the people who already have the profit, the budget or the savings.

### Can a life coach charge high ticket prices?

Yes, if the person you help has a big problem and the money to fix it. A life coach whose clients are broke can't, whatever they call the package.

### Do I need scarcity and urgency to sell high ticket coaching?

No. In our research urgency and scarcity helped fewer than 1 in 20 types of buyer decide. Credibility, proof and a clear offer do far more for people spending a lot of money.

### Is money coaching a high ticket niche?

Sometimes. Money and wealth has 199 books behind it, but plenty of those buyers describe "living pay check to pay check". The ones who might pay a high fee sound different, like the person in their 50s who decided to get serious about their finances.
