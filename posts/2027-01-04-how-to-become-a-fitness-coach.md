It's the first week of January. The gyms are rammed, the running apps are being downloaded by the million and everyone's bought new trainers. By February, plenty of those people will have stopped. Not because they're lazy, whatever they tell themselves, but because they hit a wall nobody warned them about.

If you want to become a fitness coach, that wall is your business. Anyone can write a training plan. There are thousands of free ones online. What people can't get for free is someone who's there when it gets hard, and who knows what to say when they want to give up.

So this post isn't a list of courses. It's about the moments people go looking for help, what they're thinking when they do, and how to make sure it's you they find.

## Personal trainer or fitness coach?

People use the two words for the same thing, and that's fine. But there's a difference worth knowing about, because it changes how you sell.

A personal trainer is usually paid for sessions. You meet, you train, they pay for the hour. A fitness coach is usually paid for a result over a period of time. You might train them, but you're also helping with what they eat, how they sleep, what they do on the days you're not there and what happens when life gets in the way.

That's why plenty of personal trainers move into coaching. They get sick of selling hours, and they can see their clients' real problems happen outside the gym. If that's you, good. You already know more about why people quit than you realise.

## The five moments people go looking for a fitness coach

Here's the bit I'd build your whole business around. When we looked at the books people buy about fitness, and what they write when they're struggling, the same moments came up again and again. Health, weight and fitness had 151 books in our research, more than any other health subject, so there's plenty of noise. But the moments themselves are surprisingly predictable.

### Moment 1: "I can't even do the first bit"

Somebody starts, and the very first step is harder than they expected. One person on a beginners' running plan wrote that they "can't even jog for two minutes". Another said "I'm running slower than I can walk... but if I run at that speed I get winded quickly".

**What they're thinking:** maybe I'm just not built for this. One person blamed it on having "small lungs". They're embarrassed and they're close to quitting before they've really begun.

**What you say to them:** that it's normal, and that it's not their body that's the problem. It's that they started at the wrong level. A coach who says "if you can't jog for two minutes, we start with one" is offering something a free app can't, which is a plan that bends to fit them.

### Moment 2: "I'm stuck on week 5"

This one surprised me. People don't only quit at the start. They quit in the middle, when the plan suddenly gets harder. Someone wrote "I am stuck on week 5 of couch to 5K, running 20 minutes". Another said "Run 2 Week 5 was a struggle and I am quite afraid... of the third run".

Afraid of a run. Think about that. If you've ever done the [Couch to 5K](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couch_to_5K) plan, you'll know the week they're talking about.

**What they're thinking:** I was doing so well, and now I'm failing. Maybe I've reached my limit.

**What you say to them:** that week 5 is where loads of people get stuck, and there's nothing wrong with repeating it. If your website names the exact week people get stuck on, the person reading it knows straight away you've been there before.

### Moment 3: "It's stopped working"

They've been going for a few months, it was working, and now nothing's happening. "I don't feel like I am making any progress." In weight loss, people wrote things like "I can't seem to lose weight anymore after losing 10 kgs in a few months" and "I can't seem to lose weight, despite eating less than I usually eat".

**What they're thinking:** I'm doing everything right and it's not working, so what's the point?

**What you say to them:** why it happens, in plain English, and what you'd change. This is where your knowledge does the selling. Not the science words, though. They don't want "metabolic adaptation". They want to know why the scales haven't moved in six weeks.

### Moment 4: "My body's changed and the old stuff doesn't work"

This moment comes from life, not from the training. A baby. The menopause. Turning 40 or 50. People wrote "I have 20 lbs of baby weight in my stomach at six months postpartum" and "menopause is causing massive weight gain around my tummy". People over 40 asked "Why am I losing muscle and gaining fat if I'm working out and eating properly?"

**What they're thinking:** I used to know how to do this, and now I don't. And a lot of the advice out there is written for 25-year-olds.

**What you say to them:** that you work with bodies like theirs, at this stage of life. This is one of the strongest places for a new fitness coach to start, because people at these stages don't want a general plan. They want someone who gets what's changed.

### Moment 5: "I've done it before and put it all back on"

The last moment is the saddest one, and plenty of your future clients will be in it. They've lost the weight before. More than once. And they put it back on. One person asked "what's the point of losing weight if I have to keep suffering to keep it off". Another wrote "I'm so lazy and I always stress eat".

**What they're thinking:** it's me. I'm the problem.

**What you say to them:** that it isn't laziness, and that keeping it off is a different job from losing it. Hardly anyone sells help with the bit after the weight comes off. If you're good at it, say so loudly.

## Pick one moment and own it

Now here's the decision. You can't be the coach for all five moments at once, not on one website. The person afraid of week 5 and the person who's lost and regained three stone four times want different things, and they'll recognise different words.

So pick the moment you understand best. Usually that's the one you went through yourself. If you got fit after your second baby, start with moment 4 for new mums. If you were a personal trainer watching clients regain the weight every summer, start with moment 5.

Then put that moment at the top of your homepage, in their words. Something like this, for a made-up coach who works with women over 45:

> **Doing the same workouts as ever, and gaining weight anyway?**
>
> Your body's changed. Your training should too. I help women over 45 get strong, sleep better and stop fighting their own body, with three short sessions a week you can do at home.

That's an example to show you the shape, so write yours in your own way. But notice there's no "transform your body" and no "unlock your potential". It names one moment and one person, and they'll know within a few seconds whether it's for them. Only 14.3% of coaching websites could be understood by a stranger within five seconds, so being that clear puts you ahead straight away. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check yours.

## Do you need a qualification to become a fitness coach?

In the UK and the US, there's no law that says you need one to call yourself a fitness coach. But in my opinion you should get one, for three reasons.

First, you're dealing with people's bodies, and you can hurt someone. A recognised personal training qualification teaches you how to train people safely, including the ones who've got an injury or a health condition.

Second, you'll need insurance, and insurers want to see a qualification before they'll cover you. If a client gets injured and you're not covered, that's your business gone.

Third, gyms and studios won't let you work there without one, and for plenty of new coaches that's where the first clients come from.

What a qualification won't do is get you clients. Nobody picks a fitness coach because of the certificate. They pick the one who seems to understand their problem and has helped people like them before. So get qualified, then put your energy into the stuff people actually look at. And if you want to give advice on food beyond the basics, [the nutrition coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-nutrition-coach) covers what you can and can't call yourself.

## Online or in person?

Both work, and they suit different moments.

In person is better for moments 1 and 4. Someone who can't jog for two minutes, or who's just had a baby, often needs you there watching how they move. You'll pick things up in a minute that you'd never spot on a video call. It also means your clients come from your local area, which is easier to start with.

Online is better for moments 3 and 5. The plateau and the weight that keeps coming back are mostly about what happens between sessions. Food, sleep, stress, the Friday night takeaway. You can coach that from anywhere, and you can work with people in other countries.

Plenty of fitness coaches start in person, build up their proof, then add an online option once they know what works. That's a sensible way to do it.

## Your first fitness clients

Start with the people who've already watched you change. If you got fit after 40, the people at your school gate, your work and your family have seen it happen. Some of them will want the same. [How to get your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into this in more detail.

And collect proof from day one. Before-and-after photos are what everyone in fitness uses, and in my opinion they're the weakest kind of proof you can have, because people have seen thousands of them and half look fake. What's far more believable is a client saying what's different in their life. "I ran my first 5K at 52." "I can pick my grandkids up without my back going." "It's the first summer in ten years I didn't put it all back on." [The testimonials post](/blog/coaching-testimonials) covers how to get them.

When you're ready to put a package together, don't sell sessions. Sell getting through the moment. Twelve weeks to get past week 5 and run your first 5K. Three months to get strong again after a baby. [The coaching offer post](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you how to set it out, and [the coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers whether to put the price on your website.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to become a fitness coach?

You can get a recognised personal training qualification in a few months, depending on the course and how much time you've got. Building a business takes longer. Expect your first year to be about finding your first clients and collecting proof, not about filling your diary.

### Can I become a fitness coach if I'm not super fit myself?

Yes. Clients don't need you to look like a fitness model. Often they'd rather you didn't. A coach who's 50, strong and has had two kids can be far more believable to a woman in the same position than a 23-year-old with a six-pack.

### Is January the best time to find fitness clients?

It's the busiest time, but it's also when everyone else is advertising. Lots of people who start in January are stuck or quitting by February. If your message speaks to the person who's just hit week 5 or just stopped seeing results, you'll be talking to them when they actually need help.
