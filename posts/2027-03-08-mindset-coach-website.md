A mindset coach website has a harder job than a fitness coach's website, in my opinion, because nobody can see a mindset. A fitness coach can show a before and after photo. A mindset coach is selling something that happens inside someone's head, and if the website only talks about that, the person reading it has nothing to picture.

Your website has to describe what's different in your client's ordinary week once the work is done, like what they do on a Monday morning that they couldn't do before. I call it the Monday morning test.

![A woman at her kitchen table, chin in hand, staring at a half-finished to-do list](mindset-coach-todo-list.jpg)

To show you how it works, here's a conversation with a coach called Lena. Lena isn't real. She's a made-up mindset coach, and so is her website, but everything on it is the kind of wording you'll recognise from real coaching websites.

## Lena's message

Lena sent me a link with a short note.

**Lena:** Can you have a look at my website? I've had it eight months and I've had two enquiries, and one of them was someone trying to sell me help getting found on Google. People who meet me in person book, but people who find the website don't.

**David:** Send it over. First, tell me who you help, the way you'd say it in the pub.

**Lena:** I help people who are stuck in their own heads. People who know what they want but keep getting in their own way.

That's a decent answer, because it's in plain words. But it's not what her website says.

## The first screen

Here's the top of Lena's made-up homepage, the bit you see before you scroll.

> **Transform your mindset. Transform your life.**
> I'm Lena, a certified mindset and transformation coach. I help you break free from limiting beliefs, rewire your subconscious and step into your power.
> **[Book a breakthrough call]**

**David:** Right. If I'd never met you, what do I know after reading that?

**Lena:** That I'm a mindset coach and I help with limiting beliefs.

**David:** And who's it for? Everyone's stuck in some way, so nobody reads that and thinks "that's me". And what's different after I work with you?

**Lena:** They feel more confident. They stop holding themselves back.

**David:** Those are feelings. I can't see them, and neither can the person reading your site. What you do is invisible, so the website has to make it visible some other way.

We've run a lot of coaching homepages through [the five-second test](/blog/five-second-test), which asks whether a stranger could say who a page is for and what it does after five seconds. Only around 1 in 7 pass. Lena's wouldn't.

## "But my clients do say limiting beliefs"

**Lena:** I'm not making those words up. People actually say limiting beliefs and growth mindset. Shouldn't I use them?

**David:** You're right that they use them. When we went through the books people buy about mindset and the questions they ask, the words "growth mindset", "limiting beliefs", "self-sabotage" and "reprogram your mind" all came up from the buyers themselves. Mindset had 255 books, more than any of the 117 areas we looked at.

**Lena:** So I'm right.

**David:** Half right. Listen to what those same buyers say next, though. One person wrote "I've been trying to change my mindset for years now and still feel like a failure sometimes". Another asked "How do I prevent my mind from self-sabotaging when I am making progress".

These people already know the words. They've read the books and tried for years, and they've still got the problem. So when your website hands them "limiting beliefs" again, it sounds like every other thing they've tried. Use their words to get found, but then show them what their life looks like once it's working, because nobody's shown them that.

In my opinion this is where a lot of mindset coaches go wrong. The reader doesn't doubt you know the subject. They doubt it'll work for them, because nothing else has.

## The Monday morning test

**David:** So here's the test. Pick one of your actual clients. What did they do on a Monday morning after working with you that they wouldn't have done before?

**Lena:** OK. There was someone I worked with last year who'd just been promoted to team leader. Before, they'd rewrite every email four or five times and stay late preparing for meetings they already knew everything about. In the Monday meeting they'd sit there with the answer and say nothing.

**David:** And after?

**Lena:** Now they just send the email, they say what they think in the meeting, and they go home at half five.

**David:** That's your website. "They go home at half five" is worth more than "step into your power", because anyone in that situation reads it and recognises their own Monday.

You see, people don't pay for a better mindset. They pay to be able to speak up in that meeting on a Monday morning. The mindset is how you get them there, and that's your job, but it's not what they're picturing when they decide to pay you.

Here are a few Monday morning lines Lena and I came up with from her old clients:

- They send the email the first time, without rewriting it.
- They say what they think in the meeting, even when their voice is a bit shaky.
- They stop spending Sunday evening dreading the week.

## Who is it actually for?

**Lena:** That client I mentioned had imposter syndrome. But I don't want to box myself in.

**David:** Let me show you the four areas closest to yours in our research, because the buyers in each one talk differently.

**Mindset** (255 books). People who've been trying for years and feel like they keep getting in their own way.

**Confidence and self-worth** (236 books). People wearing a mask. One wrote "Its so bad to the point where I never take off my mask in public. I'm also very jealous of my friends but never show it".

**Self-compassion** (183 books). People in real pain who are very hard on themselves. "Why am I so hard on myself" and "I feel useless and embarrassed of myself" are how they put it.

**Imposter syndrome at work** (20 books). Much smaller, but very specific. These people often have a clear moment that sent them looking: "I got a new job and am now panicking". They already know the name for what they've got, and they want a way to beat it, not an explanation of what it is.

**Lena:** So the imposter one is the smallest. Isn't that a bad sign?

**David:** Not for a coach working one to one. A small group with a clear moment, like starting a new job, is easy to write for, because you know exactly what their Monday looks like. A huge, vague group is hard to write for, because their Mondays are all different.

Look at how coaches talk about imposter syndrome compared with how buyers do. Coaches in our data write things like "a persistent internalized fear of being exposed as a fraud" and "reframe narratives of self-doubt". The buyers say "feel like a fraud" and "now panicking I can't do it and will fail". It's the same problem, but they use completely different words. It's the same with self-compassion, where coaches write "thank your inner critic for trying to help" and buyers say "beating myself up". Your website should sound like the buyers, because if a stranger has to translate your homepage into their own words, they won't bother.

For Lena, the answer to who her website is for was sat in her own example, and [the post on finding your ideal coaching client](/blog/ideal-coaching-client) goes into this properly. She does her best work with people in their first year of a new job or promotion who feel like a fraud.

## The bit about therapy

**Lena:** Some people who contact me are clearly struggling more than that. They mention anxiety or depression.

**David:** Deal with that on the website. In the self-compassion data, some buyers mention diagnoses and therapy. One wrote "My therapist is trying to encourage self-compassion, but I hate myself so much, and now I can't seem to get any further with our therapy".

Coaching isn't therapy, and a coach can't diagnose or treat anything. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so it's on you to be clear about what you do and don't do. In the UK some titles are protected, like "practitioner psychologist" and "clinical psychologist", so don't use anything that suggests you're one of those unless you are. In the US, "psychologist" and licensed titles like "licensed professional counselor" or "licensed therapist" are controlled by each state. Check the rules where you live.

A short line near the bottom of the homepage does the job. Something like "I'm a coach, not a therapist. If you're struggling with your mental health, please speak to your GP or doctor first. If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call 999 or Samaritans on 116 123 (UK), or call or text 988 (US). In an emergency in the US, call 911." Check the numbers are still right before you put it up. It protects the person reading it, and it shows everyone else you know where your work stops.

## Proof you can picture

**Lena:** I haven't got any testimonials on there. I felt a bit awkward asking.

**David:** Two thirds of the coaching homepages we checked had no proof at all. For mindset coaching, though, proof does a lot of work, because the reader can't see the result any other way.

The trick is to ask for the Monday. Don't ask a client "how did coaching make you feel?" because you'll get "Lena was amazing, I feel so much more confident", which tells a stranger nothing. Ask them "what do you do now at work that you didn't do before?" That gets you something like "I spoke in the quarterly review for the first time in two years". People believe that because it's specific. Always get permission before you use it, only use real quotes from real clients, don't change what they meant, and only claim results your clients actually got.

## The rewritten top of Lena's homepage

Here's where we ended up. It's still made up, and the price is a placeholder, but this is the shape I'd put live.

> **New job or promotion, and spending every Monday waiting to be found out?**
> I'm Lena. I coach people in their first year in a new role who feel like a fraud at work. After a few months together, my clients send the email without rewriting it five times, say what they think in the meeting, and go home on time.
> *"I spoke in the quarterly review for the first time in two years." Team leader, logistics company*
> Six sessions over three months, from £X. **[Book a free 20-minute chat]**

**Lena:** It doesn't even say mindset.

**David:** Not in the headline. Put "mindset coach" in your page title (the bit that shows up in Google search results) and in a line further down, so you get found. The top of the page is for the person who's already found you, and they're asking one thing: is this for me?

Compared with the first version, a stranger now knows who it's for, what changes in their week, roughly what it costs and what to do next. [What to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage) covers everything below the first screen.

## FAQ

### What should a mindset coach put on their website?

Who you help, in plain words, and the moment they're in. Then what changes in their ordinary week after working with you, a specific testimonial, a rough price and one clear next step. Save the methods and qualifications for further down.

### Should I use words like "limiting beliefs" on my mindset coach website?

You can use them to get found, because buyers use those words themselves when they ask for help. But don't lead with them. The people reading have usually heard those words many times already and still have the problem, so show them what their week looks like when it's working.

### How do I show results when mindset coaching is invisible?

Describe behaviour, not feelings. "They speak up in the Monday meeting now" is something a reader can picture. Ask clients what they do now that they didn't do before, and use their answers, with permission, as testimonials.
