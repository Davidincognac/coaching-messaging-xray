There's no licence or exam you have to pass to call yourself a life coach in the UK or the US, because coaching isn't regulated in either. What you do need is a group of people and a problem you can help them with, real training with plenty of practice, insurance and the basic admin sorted, a clear idea of where coaching stops and therapy starts, and then your first paying clients. The order you do those things in depends on you, your money and who you already know, and that's why a single checklist doesn't work for everyone.

![A woman on a park bench in autumn with a notebook, thinking](how-to-become-a-life-coach.jpg)

So instead of one list for everyone, I've put six yes or no questions below. Answer each one honestly and it tells you what to do next: carry on, pick a group first, train first, or learn the limits of coaching first.

## Why there isn't one right way in

You'll probably have seen the adverts promising you can become a certified life coach in a weekend and earn a full-time income in your first year. A lot of those adverts come from people who make their money selling training to new coaches, so of course the answer is always "buy our course first".

I think that's the wrong place to start for a lot of people. Someone who already has friends queuing up for their advice needs something very different from someone with a good heart, a bit of savings and no idea who'd pay them. In my opinion, your way in should fit your own money, time and contacts, not somebody else's sales page.

## All six questions in one place

Three of these can stop you and send you off to do something first: question 2 sends you to pick a group first, question 5 sends you to train first, and question 6 sends you to learn the limits first.

1. Have you been through the thing you want to help with?
2. Are you drawn to one problem or many?
3. Do you already know people who'd pay you?
4. Can you go months without coaching income?
5. Have you had real training and practice?
6. Would you know what to do if a client needed more than coaching?

## Yes or no 1: have you been through the thing you want to help with?

**If yes.** That's a real head start. You know how it feels from the inside, you know the words people use when they're stuck in it, and people trust someone who's been there.

There's one thing to check, though. Are you through it, or still in the middle of it? If you're still raw, coaching other people on the same thing can drag you back in, and you'll end up talking about what happened to you instead of listening to what's happening to them. If you're still in it, give yourself more time before you start.

**If no.** That's fine, and plenty of good coaches haven't lived the exact problem they help with. You'll just have to work harder to understand people. Read what they write, talk to them and listen. A coach who hasn't been through a midlife crisis can still help someone through one, as long as they understand that the person is asking things like "Is it too late for me?" and not asking for a new five-year plan. Whichever answer you gave, question 2 is next.

## Yes or no 2: are you drawn to one problem or many?

**If one.** Brilliant. You already know roughly who you want to help, so go to question 3.

**If many.** It's easy to get stuck here, and I get why. "Life coach" sounds like it covers everything, so it's tempting to offer everything. In my opinion, the people you want to help think about their problem, not about "life coaching", and our research backs that up.

When we mapped the reasons people hire a coach, we found over a hundred of them, and they're very different from each other. For example, we found 236 books tied to confidence and self-worth, 159 tied to career and purpose, 81 tied to relationships and divorce, and 68 tied to midlife reinvention. Someone who writes "I'm stuck in a career rut and I'm becoming more miserable by the day" wants something completely different from someone who writes "What do I do after divorce life becomes blank? I don't understand from where to start". One website and one way of talking can't do both jobs well.

So stop here and pick a group. [Our list of coaching niches](/blog/coaching-niches) shows you the options, and [how to choose a coaching niche when you can do several](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) helps you decide. You can change it later. Then come back to question 3.

## Yes or no 3: do you already know people who'd pay?

I mean real people you could name, not just a feeling that lots of people need this.

**If yes.** Talk to them before you spend money on anything else. Have a real conversation, ask what's going on for them, and if you can help, tell them what you'd offer and what it would cost. Don't build a big website first. A simple page or a clear LinkedIn profile will do for now.

**If no.** Then you've got a longer road, and it's better to know that now. Start making a list of everyone who knows you, because someone on it will know someone with the problem you help with. Then go where your group already gathers, like a local divorce support group, a Facebook group for people changing career, or LinkedIn if you help people at work. Turning up means answering people's questions and being useful, not posting offers. [Finding coaching clients on Facebook](/blog/coaching-clients-on-facebook) shows what that looks like day to day. This takes months, not weeks, which is why question 4 is next, whichever answer you gave.

## Yes or no 4: can you go months without coaching income?

Be honest with this one, because it decides how you start.

**If yes.** You've got room to build slowly. You can take time over your training, practise with a handful of people, and grow without panicking. Just don't spend savings on a logo or a brand photoshoot yet.

**If no.** Keep your job, or some other income, and start coaching alongside it. Evenings and weekends are fine for a while. Starting part time means you can pick clients who suit you instead of saying yes to anyone because the rent's due. Check your employment contract first, because some don't allow other paid work. Then move on to question 5.

## Yes or no 5: have you had real training and practice?

You don't legally need a qualification to call yourself a life coach in the UK or the US. Nobody checks, and "coach" isn't a protected title. Rules differ from country to country, so check the rules where you live, and be careful what you call yourself. In the UK you can't call yourself a clinical psychologist, counselling psychologist or practitioner psychologist unless you're registered with the HCPC, and "dietitian" is protected too.

So why train at all? Because coaching is a skill, and being kind and good with people isn't the same thing. In my opinion, a common mistake is telling people what to do, when coaching is about asking the right questions so the person works it out for themselves. That takes practice.

Here's what good training gives you:

- Lots of practice coaching real people, with someone watching and telling you what you did well and badly.
- A clear idea of the difference between coaching, teaching, mentoring and therapy.
- A code of ethics, so you know what to do about things like confidentiality.
- Some supervision, meaning someone experienced you can talk your cases through with.
- A certificate, which some clients and some companies like to see.

Be wary of anything that promises you'll be fully trained in a weekend, or that spends more time on how to sell than on how to coach. Look for training that includes observed practice and is approved by a professional coaching body. That's an organisation that sets standards for coaches and checks training courses against them. Ask the course which one it's approved by, then check on that body's own website.

**If yes.** Good. Don't buy another course just because you feel nervous. Go to question 6.

**If no.** Get some before you take money from anyone. Coach a few people for free or very cheap, with a clear end date, so you can practise and ask for honest feedback. Then go to question 6.

## Yes or no 6: would you know what to do if a client needed more than coaching?

This is the one that can do real harm if you get it wrong.

Coaching is for people who are basically OK and want to move forward with something. Coaches don't diagnose or treat anything. Therapy and counselling are for people who are struggling with their mental health, working through trauma, or finding it hard to cope day to day.

Some signs that a client needs more than you can give them:

- They talk about hurting themselves or not wanting to be here.
- They can't get out of bed, eat properly or go to work.
- Every session goes back to something painful from the past, again and again.
- They mention heavy drinking, drugs, or problems with food.

If someone tells you they're in danger, tell them to call the emergency services, and don't carry on coaching as normal. In other cases, pause the coaching, tell them kindly that this is beyond what coaching is for, and suggest they speak to their GP or doctor, or a qualified therapist. Write this into your client agreement before you start.

**If yes.** You're ready to start, and the next part covers the boring bits.

**If no.** Learn it before you take on anyone. Good training covers it, and so does supervision.

## Insurance and the rest of the admin

In the UK and the US, coaches don't usually have to have insurance by law, but I'd get it anyway. Professional indemnity insurance can cover you if a client says your coaching cost them money or caused them a loss. What's covered depends on the policy, so check with a broker. Public liability is less important if you only coach online, and more if people come to your home or you run workshops. Tell the broker about the work you actually do.

You'll also need to register as self-employed or as a business, depending on where you are, keep records of money in and out, and keep your clients' notes private and secure. [How to start a coaching business](/blog/how-to-start-a-coaching-business) goes through all of that in order, with the first 90 days planned out.

## When you're ready for a website

When you do get a website, keep it simple and make it obvious who you help. When we checked nearly 11,000 coaching websites, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with. [What to put on a life coach website](/blog/life-coach-website) shows you how to avoid being one of them.

## FAQ

### Can I become a life coach with no coaching experience at all?

Yes, everyone starts somewhere. Get training that includes observed practice, then coach a few people for free or very cheap, with a clear end date, before you charge full price. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, but check the rules where you live.

### How long does it take to become a life coach?

There's no set time, because there's no legal route. It depends on your training, how much practice you get and whether you already know people who'd pay. Starting part time while you learn is a sensible way in.

### What's the difference between a life coach and a therapist?

A life coach helps someone who's basically OK move forward with something, mostly by asking questions. A therapist helps people with mental health problems and past trauma. Coaches don't diagnose or treat anything, and should point people to a doctor or therapist when that's what they need.
