Everybody's a nutrition expert. The bloke at the gym who swears by chicken and rice. Your auntie who's been on every diet since 1985. The influencer with the perfect kitchen and the discount code. The back of the cereal box. The person you're hoping to coach has heard from every one of them, and plenty of them have contradicted each other.

So if you want to become a nutrition coach, the hard part isn't knowing about food. You probably know plenty already, or you'll learn it on your course. The hard part is getting someone to believe you, when they've been told so many different things that they've stopped believing anyone.

That's what this post is about. Not which qualification to get, but how to be the one person in all that noise that they actually trust. And with January round the corner, when half the country decides to sort their eating out, it's worth thinking about now.

## Two people, two very different problems

Let me introduce you to two people from my research. They're not real individuals, they're what came through when we looked at the books people buy about food and their bodies, and what they write when they're struggling.

**The first one has a stomach problem.** Gut health and nutrition had 50 books in our research, and the people in it were worried and confused. They wrote things like "my stomach is getting worse every day and I don't know where it's coming from". One detail stood out more than anything else. One person said the problem started when they tried to do the right thing: "This started once I started working out and trying to eat healthier."

Think about what that does to someone. They followed the advice, and it made them worse. So the next person who tells them what to eat is starting from a long way back.

**The second one has been on and off diets for years.** Nutrition and healthy eating was one of the biggest groups we found, with 60 books aimed at people trying to lose weight and keep it off. They wrote "I can't seem to lose weight, despite eating less than I usually eat" and asked "is there a plan to stop the cycle of yo-yo dieting?" And a lot of them blamed themselves. "I'm so lazy and I always stress eat."

That person doesn't need another diet. They've done the diets. They need someone who won't make them feel lazy, and who can help them eat in a way they can actually keep up.

Those two people want completely different things from a nutrition coach. One wants their stomach to stop hurting and to understand why. The other wants to stop the cycle and stop hating themselves for it. A website that tries to speak to both at once won't really speak to either, so pick the one you understand best.

## Listen to how they talk, then listen to how coaches talk

This is the part that surprised me. When we looked at how nutrition coaches describe what they sell, we saw programme names like these:

- "12-Week Total Gut Restoration"
- "Leaky Gut Reset Program"
- "Heal Your Gut: A 4 Step Plan"
- "Restore microbiome diversity and target the root causes"

Now put those next to what the person with the stomach problem actually wrote. "My stomach is getting worse every day and I don't know where it's coming from." "This started once I started eating healthier."

Do you see the problem? The coach is talking about resets and restoration and root causes. The person is talking about a sore stomach and being confused about what went wrong. They're not even in the same conversation. And to someone who's already been let down by "healthy" advice, a "Total Gut Restoration" programme sounds like one more promise that might not work.

The fix is simple to say and takes a bit of nerve to do. Lead with their words, not your programme name. "If your stomach got worse when you started eating healthier, you're not imagining it, and you didn't do anything wrong" will stop that person scrolling far faster than any "reset".

We've seen this play out on real websites too. When we looked at 10,954 coaching websites, the health and nutrition sites were harder for a stranger to understand in the first five seconds than coaching sites overall. In my opinion, this is a big part of why. The language from the training ends up on the homepage, and the person reading it doesn't speak that language.

## Trust is the whole job

If you only take one thing from this post, take this one. A nutrition coach isn't really selling food knowledge. Anyone can get that for free. You're selling the feeling that, this time, someone's telling them the truth.

So how do you build that before you've even spoken to them?

**Give something useful away first.** Not a "free discovery call", which is really a sales call. Something they can use tonight. A one-page guide to the five foods that commonly cause bloating and how to test them one at a time. A short list of why diets stop working after a few weeks. When someone gets real help from you for nothing, they start to believe the paid stuff might be worth it too. [The lead magnet post](/blog/lead-magnet-ideas-for-coaches) goes into what makes a good one.

**Show results that sound like real life.** Not "lost 3 stone in 6 weeks", which sounds like every advert they've ever ignored. Something like "I can eat out with friends again without spending the evening in the toilet" or "it's the first January in ten years I haven't started a diet". Small, specific and believable.

**Be honest about what you don't know.** If the research on something is mixed, say so. If something needs a doctor, say so. Every time you admit a limit, you become more believable on everything else.

## What a "what I won't do" section looks like

Here's something I'd like to see far more nutrition coaches do. Put a short section on your website saying what you won't do. For someone who's been burned by bad advice, it's often the most reassuring thing on the page.

Here's an example for a made-up nutrition coach who works with people whose digestion has gone wrong. It's there to show you the shape, so write yours in your own words and only promise what you'll stick to.

> **What I won't do**
>
> I won't tell you to cut out whole food groups unless there's a good reason, and if there is, I'll explain it.
>
> I won't sell you supplements. I don't earn anything from what you buy, so my advice is just my advice.
>
> I won't diagnose anything. If what you're describing needs a doctor, I'll tell you, and I'll work alongside them.
>
> I won't put you on a plan you can't keep up. If it doesn't fit your actual life, it isn't working.

Read that as the person whose stomach got worse when they ate healthier. Every line answers a worry they've already got. That's what builds trust, far more than a certificate on the About page.

## Be careful what you call yourself

This is the one bit of admin I'd sort out before anything else. Some titles in nutrition are protected by law. "Dietitian" is protected in both the UK and the US, and in some places other titles are controlled too. Training as a nutrition coach doesn't make you a dietitian, so don't use the word unless you've qualified as one.

"Nutrition coach" itself isn't a protected title, and you don't need a licence to use it. But that's exactly why your training, your honesty and your results have to do the work. Check what you can and can't call yourself where you live, and keep well away from anything that sounds like you're treating an illness.

## Where your first nutrition clients will come from

Not from Instagram, at least not at first. Nutrition is one of the noisiest subjects on social media, and a new coach is up against people with years of content and big followings. [The social media post](/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coaches) goes into why one channel you keep up with beats four you don't.

Your first clients are more likely to come from people who already know you, and from people who see your future clients every week. Personal trainers whose clients aren't getting results because of what they eat. Yoga teachers and physios. Hairdressers, even, who hear everyone's problems. Get to know a few of them, tell them exactly who you help in one sentence, and ask if they know anyone. It's slower than going viral, but it's far more likely to work.

## Should you sell supplements?

In my opinion, be very careful. It's tempting, because it's extra income and plenty of nutrition coaches do it. But the moment you're selling something you earn money from, every bit of advice you give looks a bit less trustworthy. For people who already don't know who to believe, that can cost you more than the commission is worth.

If you do recommend products, say clearly whether you earn anything from them. If you don't, say that too, because it's a selling point.

## FAQ

### Can a nutrition coach help someone with IBS or Crohn's?

A nutrition coach can help someone change what and how they eat, but conditions like IBS and Crohn's need a doctor's diagnosis and care. Work alongside their doctor, never instead of them, and be clear on your website that this is how you work.

### Should I specialise in one diet, like vegan or keto?

You can, but think about the person rather than the diet. "Vegans who are always tired" is a person with a problem you can help. "Keto coaching" is a method, and the person reading might not even want it. Lead with the problem, then explain your approach.

### Is January the best time to find nutrition clients?

It's when lots of people start thinking about their eating, so it's busy. But it's also when everyone else is advertising. People who are still struggling in March, after the January diet has fallen apart, are often more ready for real help, and there's far less competition for their attention.
