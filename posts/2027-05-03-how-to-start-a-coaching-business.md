To start a coaching business, you decide who you help and what with, you put together one simple offer with a price, you sort out the basic admin, and then you talk to people who already know you until someone pays you. A logo, a big website and a second certificate can all wait. If you do those few things in the right order, 90 days is plenty of time to go from an idea to your first paying clients, or at least to know exactly why you haven't got them yet.

![A woman at her kitchen table late at night, planning in a notebook with a laptop open](how-to-start-a-coaching-business.jpg)

Before the plan, one thing I wish I'd done myself. When I started out on my own, I thought my experience and my degree were enough. They weren't, because I didn't have anyone asking me the right questions. I should have put some help on a credit card. In my opinion it would have saved me a year. So if you can find someone who's done it, and who'll ask you awkward questions, it's often worth paying for.

Here's how I'd spend those 90 days if I were starting from nothing.

## Why people start in the wrong place

If you've already spent money on a logo or a course, that's not on you. The coaching world is full of people selling courses, templates and "six figure" programmes to new coaches, and it's in their interest to make starting out look complicated.

We looked at 40 books tied to people building a coaching business, and at the questions new coaches ask, and those questions are very ordinary. One wrote "I am a new life coach. What is the best way to get new clients and make a living coaching?" Another asked "How could I find clients for coaching without spending any budget on marketing (I only charge 20$/session)?"

Nobody's asking about brand colours there, they just want to know how to get paid, so that's what this plan is built around.

Think about someone opening a food van. They don't get the van painted and the sign made before they've decided whether they're selling burgers or curry. They decide what they're selling and who to, they cook it for friends to see if anyone likes it, and they sort out the food hygiene rules. The paint job comes later. A coaching business works the same way.

## Weeks 1-2: decide who you help

The first two weeks are about one sentence. Who do you help, and what do you help them with?

Write down the people you'd love to help and the problem they've got, and be specific about it. "I help people be happier" is far too wide. "I help parents who've been at home for years get back into work" is something a stranger can picture. If you can do several things and you're stuck choosing, [how to choose a coaching niche when you can do several](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) goes through it properly.

Then start a list of everyone who knows you. I mean old colleagues, friends, people from the gym, people you trained with, your neighbour, your cousin's husband. Don't judge who might buy yet, you'll need this list in a few weeks.

What I'd leave alone for now is the logo, the business cards, the business name you've spent three days on and the next course you were thinking of buying. You don't need any of it yet.

You're on track when you can say your one sentence out loud to a friend, and they can tell you back who you help without you having to explain it again.

## Weeks 3-4: a first offer and the boring bits

Now you turn that sentence into something people can actually buy, and you sort out the admin so you're legal from the first payment.

Start with the offer. Write down what someone gets if they say yes: how long it lasts, how often you meet, what happens between sessions and what it costs. Keep it to one page. [How to put together a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you what to say, and [how to put a coaching package together](/blog/coaching-packages) builds one step by step.

Put a price on it, even if you're nervous. Nobody can say yes to coaching that hasn't got a price.

Then do the admin. None of this is exciting, but it's much easier to set up now than to fix later. I'm not an accountant or a lawyer, so treat this as a starting list and check the rules where you live.

- **Registering.** In the UK, if you're working for yourself you'll usually need to register as self-employed with HMRC, so you can file a tax return and pay tax and National Insurance on your profits. You need to register by 5 October after the end of the tax year you started in, so if you start coaching in May, that's 5 October the following year. The government's [working for yourself pages](https://www.gov.uk/working-for-yourself) explain it in plain English. In the US, the rules depend on your state and sometimes your city or county. You may need a business licence or to register a business name, and you'll pay self-employment tax to the IRS. Check your state and local rules.
- **Keeping records.** Open a simple spreadsheet from day one with money in, money out, the date and what it was for. Keep receipts. A separate bank account makes this much easier, even if it's just a second personal account to start with.
- **Insurance.** In the UK and the US, coaches don't usually have to have insurance by law, but it's sensible to get it. Professional indemnity insurance covers you if a client says your advice cost them money. Public liability is less important if you only coach online, but more important if people come to your home or you run workshops. Ask a broker what fits the work you actually do.
- **Data protection.** You'll be keeping notes about people, sometimes personal ones. In the UK that falls under data protection law, and you may need to pay a small yearly fee to the Information Commissioner's Office. The ICO website has a short online check that tells you whether you need to pay the fee. Wherever you are, keep notes somewhere locked, only keep what you need, and tell clients what you keep and why.

One more thing while we're here. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, and "coach" isn't a protected title. Some titles are, though. In the UK you can't call yourself a clinical psychologist, counselling psychologist or practitioner psychologist unless you're registered with the HCPC, and "dietitian" is protected too. Coaches don't diagnose or treat anything, so if someone needs medical or mental health help, point them towards it.

I wouldn't bother yet with three tiers of packages, a group programme, a membership or an online course, because one offer is enough. Don't agonise over setting up a limited company either. In the UK, starting as a sole trader is usually the simpler route, and you can talk to an accountant about changing later.

You're on track when your offer fits on one page with a price on it, you know how and when you need to register where you live, and your money spreadsheet exists, even if it's empty.

## Weeks 5-8: first clients from people who know you

This is the bit people put off, and it's the bit that actually starts the business.

Go back to your list from week one. Pick the people who either have the problem you help with, or know someone who does. Then message them, one at a time, like a normal person. Something like this made-up example:

> "Hi, I've started coaching people who've been at home with the kids and want to get back into work. If you know anyone who's thinking about it, would you mind passing my name on? I'm taking on a few people at the moment."

It's just telling people what you're doing and asking if they know anyone. When someone's interested, have a proper conversation. Ask what's going on for them, listen, and if you can help, tell them what you offer and what it costs.

When I sold book publishing, masterminds and retreats in the coaching world, I spoke directly to the majority of the people who bought. These days, my best clients still come from taking people offline and having a proper conversation, often before there's any talk of a discovery call. Let people get to know you first. You don't want them feeling like they've been put into a process.

Why people you know? Because strangers need proof before they'll trust you, and you haven't got any yet. Our research found that proof from other people helps the buying decision for more than 9 in 10 of the types of coaching buyer we mapped. People who already know you don't need that proof, because they've seen what you're like for years. [Getting your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into this in more detail.

Do you need a website? Not yet, in my opinion. When we checked 10,954 coaching websites, only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds. A website that doesn't make sense won't win you anything. A clear LinkedIn profile or a simple booking page can do the job for now, and [how to get coaching clients without a website](/blog/coaching-clients-without-a-website) shows you what to put in its place.

What I'd stay away from here is paid ads, sending cold messages to hundreds of strangers, and coaching people for free with no end date. A couple of reduced-price places in exchange for honest feedback and a testimonial is fine, but coaching for free forever isn't a business.

You're on track when you've had proper conversations with people about their problem, and you've actually said your price out loud to some of them. Even if nobody's said yes yet, you'll have learned more in these four weeks than in any course.

## Weeks 9-13: proof, and fixing what didn't work

By now you'll have either a client or two, or a handful of conversations that went nowhere, and you can learn from both.

If you've got clients, do a really good job and keep notes on what changes for them. At the end, ask for a testimonial in their own words. Ask what was going on before, what changed and what they'd tell a friend. That's the start of your proof, and it's what lets you talk to strangers later.

If you haven't got clients yet, look at where it stopped. Did people not get what you do? Then your sentence from week one needs work. Did they get it, but not want it? Then maybe it's the wrong problem or the wrong people. Did they want it but say "I need to think about it" when they heard the price? Then look at the offer, or how you're saying it.

This is also when a simple page starts making sense, because now you've got words from your clients to put on it.

Try not to rebrand, change your niche every week because one person said no, or buy a big funnel system at this stage. Make one change at a time so you can see what worked.

You're on track when you can say, from the conversations you've had, which part of your plan works and which part doesn't, and you've got at least one person's words about working with you that you're allowed to share.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to start a coaching business?

No. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so you don't legally need a certificate to call yourself a coach. Training can make you a better coach and some clients like to see it, but it won't get you clients on its own. Just don't use protected titles like clinical psychologist or dietitian unless you're registered.

### Can I start a coaching business while I'm still in my job?

Yes, you can. Check your employment contract first, because some say you can't do other paid work or work with your employer's clients. In the UK you'll still usually need to register as self-employed with HMRC for the coaching income, even with a job.

### How much money do I need to start?

Very little. You need a way to talk to people, like a phone or video calls, a way to get paid and somewhere to keep records. Insurance is worth paying for. Leave the bigger spending until you've got paying clients and know what you actually need.

### Should I set up a limited company?

You don't have to. In the UK, starting as a sole trader is usually the simpler route. A limited company can make sense later, but the rules and costs are different, so talk to an accountant before you decide. In the US, check your state's rules for business types.
