A mental health coach website has a harder job than a lot of coaching websites. The person reading it is often scared, exhausted or low, and they're deciding whether to trust a stranger with something they might not have told their own family. In short, your website needs to say who you help, say clearly that you're a coach and not a therapist, make crisis numbers easy to find, and make the next step feel safe.

![Two people talking on a park bench, one listening carefully](mental-health-coach-bench.jpg)

Open your homepage in another tab. I'm going to give you 10 checks, and for each one you give yourself a point if the answer is yes. Be strict. If you have to squint to find it, that's a no. Keep a tally as you go. Any example lines I give are made up, so change them to fit you.

## Before you start: what the people reading your website actually say

Our research matched self-help books to the kinds of people they're written for, and collected what those people say in their own words. For mental health, four groups stood out. There were 147 books written for people with anxiety, 128 for people with stress and burnout, 63 for people with depression and 41 for people recovering from burnout at work.

The words people used were plain and raw. Someone anxious wrote "I feel anxious all the time about not being able to cope with what life throws at me". Someone burnt out wrote "I worked too hard for too long. I feel like no amount of rest will help". Someone depressed wrote "I've been feeling very dull like an empty shell". That's who's reading your website.

## Check 1: the top of the page says who you help and with what

Someone anxious or worn out hasn't got the energy to work out what you do. When we checked 10,954 coaching websites, only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds. A soft line like "Find your calm" could be a spa, a candle shop or a yoga app.

So name the person and the problem in everyday words. For example (made up): "I coach people who are burnt out at work and can't take time off, to get their energy back without quitting." [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to test it. Point if a stranger could tell who you help from the top alone.

## Check 2: it says near the top that you're a coach and not a therapist

A lot of people don't know the difference between a coach, a counsellor, a therapist and a psychologist. If they think they're booking therapy and they get coaching, that's bad for them and bad for you. Being clear also shows you know your limits, and in my opinion that makes people trust you more, not less.

Here's what I'd put. One or two calm sentences, for example (made up): "I'm a coach, not a therapist or a doctor. I don't diagnose or treat mental health conditions. What I do is help you make practical changes to your day, your work and your habits, alongside any support you already have."

Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no body that makes you say this. You have to say it yourself. Point if it's easy to see.

## Check 3: someone in crisis can find help within one click

This is the biggest check on the list, and the one I'd fix first. In the depression research, one person wrote "I can't go on anymore". That person might find your website at two in the morning. They don't need a discovery call. They need a person to talk to, right now.

Put a short crisis section on your website and link to it from every page's footer. For example:

> If you're in crisis or thinking about ending your life, please don't wait for a coaching session.
> In the UK, call Samaritans free on 116 123, any time, day or night. You can also call NHS 111 (in England and Wales, choose option 2 for mental health). In Northern Ireland, call Lifeline on 0808 808 8000.
> In the US, call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline.
> If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 999 in the UK or 911 in the US.

If your clients live elsewhere, add their numbers. Services change, so check the numbers before you publish. Point only if it's linked from every page.

## Check 4: you say who you don't work with

Turning people away sounds odd, but it protects people who need more than coaching, and everyone else feels safer with someone who knows their limits.

A short list, said kindly, is enough. For example (made up): "Coaching isn't the right support if you're in crisis, having thoughts of harming yourself, living with an eating disorder, psychosis or severe depression, or if you need medical help to stop drinking or using drugs. If that's you, please speak to your GP or doctor first. If you're not sure, get in touch and I'll help you find the right kind of support, even if it isn't me."

[The sobriety coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-sobriety-coach) makes the same point about drinking. A coach helps with day-to-day things like routine, work and relationships, and leaves the medical side to doctors. If you work with grief, [the grief coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-grief-coach) covers where to draw the line and when to refer someone on. Point if your list is on the website.

## Check 5: you use your title honestly

People trust titles. Suggesting you're clinically trained when you aren't misleads someone vulnerable, and can break the law.

In the UK, "practitioner psychologist", "clinical psychologist" and "counselling psychologist" are protected titles. You can only use them if you're registered with the HCPC. "Coach", "counsellor" and "therapist" aren't protected titles in the UK, but if you're not trained as a counsellor or therapist, don't call yourself one. In the US, titles like psychologist and licensed counselor are controlled state by state. Check the rules where you live.

"Mental health coach" or "wellbeing coach" is fine. Then say what training you've actually done, in one plain line. Point if your title is honest and your training is listed.

## Check 6: your words sound like your clients, not a textbook

In our research, the coach language in this area includes lines like "Diaphragmatic breathing activates your parasympathetic nervous system" and "regulate their nervous system, reclaim their energy, and reconnect with the life they actually want to live". Compare what an anxious person wrote: "I feel like I can't breathe".

So use their words. "You can't switch off, you can't focus at work, and you're fed up of being told to just breathe" will mean far more to someone than a paragraph about the nervous system. Read your homepage out loud. If it sounds like a leaflet from a hospital waiting room, that's a no.

## Check 7: you admit what they've already tried

People with anxiety and burnout often say they've been given the same advice over and over. Rest more, breathe, journal, have a bath. One person with anxiety wrote "yet I always manage to cope so why won't the anxiety go away". Someone with depression wrote "Talking about it hasn't helped at all". Someone burnt out asked "How do you handle burnout when quitting isn't an option". If your website gives them the same tips again, they'll assume you're the same as everyone else.

Say out loud what they've tried and why it hasn't been enough, then say what you do differently. For example (made up): "You've had the breathing apps and the self-care lists, and you're still exhausted. In my coaching we look at what's actually draining you at work, and change that, rather than just trying to rest it off."

Be careful here. Don't promise a cure, don't promise a timeline, and don't say you can fix anxiety or depression. You're helping with practical change, not treatment. Point if you name what they've tried and stay clear of promises.

## Check 8: there's proof from real people

The question people keep asking is whether anyone actually gets better. People buying burnout books asked "How did you heal from serious, serious burnout?" and people buying books on depression asked "Once you've been severely depressed, can you go back to normal?" Yet when we checked coaching websites, two thirds showed no proof at all.

Use short, specific testimonials, always with written permission. Mental health is private, so initials and a short description, like "Teacher, 38", are fine. Never share details that could identify someone. And if a testimonial promises something you can't stand behind, like "this coach cured my anxiety", don't use it. I'd rather see two honest lines from real clients than a wall of five-star gushing. Point if there's at least one real testimonial on the page.

## Check 9: you explain how it works and roughly what it costs

Someone who's anxious hates not knowing what's going to happen. Every unknown is another reason not to book.

Write a short "how it works" section. Say how many sessions, how long, online or in person, and what happens if they want to stop. Then give at least a starting price, or a clear range. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers how to do that without scaring people off.

It's also worth saying that coaching can sit alongside therapy or medication, and that you'll never suggest someone stops either. That's a decision for them and their doctor. Point if a visitor knows how it works and roughly what it costs before getting in touch.

## Check 10: the next step is gentle

Some coaching websites push hard, with countdown timers, "only two spaces left" and "book your breakthrough call now". For someone who's already anxious, that's the last thing they need. In my opinion countdown timers have no place on a mental health coach website, and if you've got one, I'd take it off today.

Make the next step small and safe, and tell them exactly what it involves. For example (made up): "If you'd like to talk it through, book a free 20-minute call. There's no sales pitch, and if I don't think coaching is right for you, I'll tell you and help you find what is." Point if nothing on the page pushes them.

## How did you score?

First, one rule that beats the score. If you said no to any of checks 2, 3, 4 or 5, fix those first whatever your total, because they're about keeping people safe and being honest with them.

9 or 10 points means you're in good shape. Ask someone who's never seen your website to read it on their phone and tell you what you do. If they can, you're done.

6 to 8 points means you've got the basics, but one or two of your no answers are either putting people off or leaving them without help when they need it. Fix whichever of checks 2 to 5 you said no to first. The others are about getting clients, and they can come after.

5 or fewer means there's work to do, but don't panic, none of it needs a designer. Start with checks 2 to 5, then work through the rest. [What to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage) is a good place to go next.

## FAQ

### Can a mental health coach diagnose or treat anxiety or depression?

No. That's for doctors and registered or licensed professionals. A coach helps with practical changes in someone's life and work, often alongside other support, and points them to medical help when they need it.

### What should a mental health coach put on their website?

It needs to say who you help and with what, that you're a coach and not a therapist, easy-to-find crisis numbers, who you don't work with, your honest title and training, real proof, how it works, a rough price and a gentle next step.

### Do I need crisis numbers on my coaching website?

There's no law that says you must, but if you work with people's mental health you should. In the UK that's Samaritans on 116 123, or NHS 111 (in England and Wales, choose option 2 for mental health), and Lifeline on 0808 808 8000 in Northern Ireland. In the US it's the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Put them on their own page and link to it from every page.
