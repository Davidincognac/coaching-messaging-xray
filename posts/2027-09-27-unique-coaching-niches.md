A unique coaching niche can work, but only if five things are true. People are already asking for help in their own words, very few books or coaches serve it, someone in the picture can pay, you can find those people in one place, and you either understand the problem from the inside or can learn it quickly. If a niche fails one of those, being unusual won't save it. From our research, toddler sleep, helping parents of teens who vape and single fathers look the strongest, and gambling addiction is one to avoid.

![A coach pointing at a wall of index cards while two people look on](unique-coaching-niches.jpg)

We took nine unusual groups from our research, which covers more than 900 types of buyer and around 2,000 books, and ran each one through the tests. For each one you get the number of books we found for them and what real people wrote when asking for help, copied word for word. We call those comments.

## The five tests

**Test one: people are already asking for help in their own words.** That means real questions written by the people themselves, in places like forums, Facebook groups and question sites. If you can't find anyone asking, you'll spend your time convincing people they have a problem, and that's slow and expensive.

**Test two: very few books or coaches serve it.** We found 128 books on stress and burnout, for example, so a coach there is one voice in a crowd. If there's one book, people looking for help have very little to choose from.

**Test three: someone can pay.** The person with the problem isn't always the person with the money, and sometimes nobody in the picture has any money at all. You need to know who's paying before you build anything.

**Test four: you can find them in one place.** If your people are spread thinly, you'll struggle to reach enough of them, so look for a group or forum where they turn up.

**Test five: you understand it from the inside, or can learn it fast.** People with an unusual problem can tell very quickly whether you get it. If you haven't lived it, you need to learn it properly, and some problems need a qualification you can't pick up in a weekend.

## Nine unusual niches, run through the tests

### Toddler and child sleep: passes

There's just 1 book written for this group, and we found 13 questions parents posted online asking for help. One wrote "My toddler refuses to go to sleep or even stay laying down in his bed, what can we do? We barely get any sleep these days". Another asked "How do I get my 4 year old son to sleep in his own room and fall asleep without me laying with him?"

That's tests one and two passed. Test three is strong because the parent pays, and exhausted parents will pay to make it stop. Test four is good, because parents of young children gather in parenting groups. For test five, having been through it yourself helps. Just be clear that anything to do with a child's health goes to the health visitor, GP or doctor, not to you.

### Helping teens quit vaping: passes, if you coach the parent

"I caught my 16-year-old daughter vaping and sneaking out. How should I go about this?" That's typical of the 22 comments we found, many from parents. There's only 1 book. And this one: "I took away her car, computer, phone, door, pillows, blankets, TV, and she's grounded for 2 years. Should I have done more?"

That parent is desperate, and the parent is the one with the money, not the teenager, so test three passes. And the parent has to be the coaching client, because a teenager who doesn't want to stop isn't going to sign up. Working with under-18s also brings extra responsibilities, so check the rules where you live. There's also free help for teenagers who want to quit, so you'd have to give the parent something the free help doesn't.

### Single fathers: probably passes, but check test four

Test one passes, with 12 comments from single dads. One dad asked "Why do I feel so overwhelmed as a single parent when it looks so easy for everyone around me?" Another wanted to know "How to become a better single father with no help from my family". We only found 2 books, and when we looked for coaches talking to single dads we found very few, so test two looks like a pass. Do your own search to be sure. For test three, one of them mentions working full time, so there may be money, though not much spare time.

Test four is the weak spot. Our research doesn't show where single dads gather, so find that out before you build anything. Some of what they ask about, like custody, is a legal question for a solicitor or lawyer, so stay on the parenting side.

### Stay-at-home dads: fails test three

The pain is real, and a lot of it is loneliness. "How do stay-at-home dads deal with loneliness and isolation?" and "As a man have you ever felt emasculated because you are a stay at home parent" are two of the 11 comments, and there are 2 books.

The trouble is money, because the dad at home isn't the one earning. One asked how to battle depression as a stay-at-home dad, and that's for a doctor, not a coach. This looks better as a free group than a paid service.

### Making friends after a move: fails test three

Test one is a clear pass here, with 19 comments, though there are 3 books. "I shifted to a new city 5 months back and since then I am not able to make any friends here" is about as plain as it gets. But look at how they ask. "How did you make friends and get connected after moving to a new city?" They're asking other people who've moved for tips. They want friends, and nothing in what they wrote suggests they'd pay a coach to get some. That's test three failing, and it makes test four hard too, because the whole problem is that they don't know where anyone is yet. Some also wrote about "feeling lonely and depressed", and anything heading towards depression belongs with a GP.

### Gambling addiction: fails, steer clear

On paper this looks great, with 17 comments and just 1 book. "I have tried for many years but I can't" and "I always tell myself I'll stop but I never do" are two of the comments.

Then you hit test three. One wrote "I am maxed out on credit and but still find myself betting with the little bits of money I do have". Another said "I barely have enough money to pay the bills". That's test three failing badly, because this person can't afford a coach. Problem gambling is an addiction that doctors and specialist services treat, so the right place for that person is their GP or a specialist gambling support service.

### Parents worried about a child's gaming addiction: maybe, with thin evidence

The 4 books we found are about gaming and screen addiction, mostly aimed at parents of younger children. There were only 7 comments, but they include parents of sons in their 20s, and they're strong: "My son is 26 years old and addicted to video games. He rarely leaves his room, doesn't work, and pretty much ignores me. I don't know what to do?"

The parent can probably pay, so test three looks fine. The catch is the same as with vaping, because the parent is asking for help and the son isn't. So you'd be coaching a worn-out parent, like the one who wrote "How do I stop my son's addiction to video games without him getting upset with me? I'm very tired." If the son's gaming looks like an illness, or the son seems low or cut off from everyone, that's for the son's GP or doctor, not for you. Seven comments is thin for test one, so find a lot more parents saying the same thing first.

### Strength and fall prevention for older people: passes four tests, but test five needs a qualification

This one had 35 comments, more than any other niche on this list, and only 2 books. The people asking are often the grown-up children: "She doesn't move much anymore because she is so afraid of falling but she is also afraid of losing ability to walk. It is a cycle we cannot seem to break".

Those grown-up children are usually the ones who'd pay, so tests one to three pass, and test four looks good too. Test five is where it falls down for a general coach. This is physical work with people who could get hurt, which makes it a job for a physiotherapist or a fitness professional trained to work with older adults. If you've got that background it looks strong, and if you haven't, it isn't yours.

### The partner who cheated: not enough evidence yet

"How do I repair my relationship and regain her trust after having an affair and lying about it" is one of just 4 comments, and there's 1 book. Someone in that position may be desperate to save the relationship, so they might pay.

But 4 comments is very little, so test one fails. My guess is shame. People who've cheated don't announce it in public groups, which makes test four hard as well. It might work, but I wouldn't build a business on 4 comments.

## Unusual isn't the same as good

A low book count can mean nobody has got round to serving these people yet, which is good news for you. Or it can mean there aren't enough people with money who want help. The book count alone can't tell you which, and that's why tests one and three count for more than the others.

So when an unusual niche excites you, go and read what the people in it actually write. If you can't find them asking, or can't see who'd pay, keep looking. ## FAQ

### What is a unique coaching niche?

It's a group of people with a specific problem that very few coaches or books serve. Toddler sleep and single fathers are two examples from our research, with 1 and 2 books written for them.

### Is a niche with no competition a good sign?

Not on its own. It might mean nobody has spotted the chance yet, or it might mean nobody can pay. Check that people are asking for help and that someone in the picture has money.

### How do I check whether people will pay for an unusual niche?

Work out who the buyer is first, because it isn't always the person with the problem. Then read what they write when they ask for help. Someone who says they can't pay their bills is telling you something.

### Can a coach work in a niche that involves health or addiction?

Only on the coaching side, and only alongside proper medical help. Coaches can't diagnose or treat anything, so point people to their GP or doctor, and check the rules where you live.
