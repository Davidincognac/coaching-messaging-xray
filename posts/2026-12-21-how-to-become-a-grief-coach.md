Nearly everyone who becomes a grief coach has lost someone. A parent, a partner, a child, a friend. They got through the worst of it, often with very little help, and came out the other side wanting to make it easier for the next person. I've got a lot of respect for that. It's one of the hardest kinds of coaching there is, and one of the most needed.

It's also one of the hardest to market, for an obvious reason. The people who need you are going through the worst time of their lives. They're not shopping around, they're not comparing packages, and anything that smells of a sales pitch will push them away. So the usual marketing advice doesn't fit, and a lot of it would do real harm.

So instead of telling you which course to take, I'm going to do something different with this one. Below are five things grieving people actually said, taken from my research into what people buy and write about grief. After each one is what I think it means for you if you want to become a grief coach.

## First, how big is this area?

Grief and loss had 67 books in our research. That's plenty of help out there, but it's far less crowded than something like weight loss, which had 151. And when we looked at what's already being offered to grieving people, there was much less of the hard-sell, big-promise stuff you see in other areas. What people have mostly been offered, by the people around them, is advice to move on.

That means there's room for a grief coach who's gentle, clear and honest about what they do. You don't need a clever system or a ten-step method. In fact, in my opinion, anything that sounds too much like a system will feel cold to someone who's grieving.

## 1. "Nobody understands the grief we're going through"

People wrote this, or something like it, when they talked about grief. Grieving people feel alone, even when they're surrounded by people. Friends don't know what to say, so they say nothing, or they say the wrong thing.

**What it means for you:** your own experience is the most important thing you've got. When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That's the sense that you've seen their problem before. A certificate is the weakest version of it. Having lost someone yourself, and found a way to keep living, is the strongest.

So tell your story on your website, gently and briefly. Not every detail, just enough for someone to think, she gets it. Of the coaching websites that did best in my research, 90.7% had a story on the page, and a third of all coaching websites had nothing human on them at all. For a grief coach, a website with nothing human on it is a non-starter.

## 2. "People keep telling us to move on"

This one is a big deal. Grieving people wrote about the people around them not acknowledging the loss, or telling them it was time to move on. And one of their biggest fears was that "moving on" meant forgetting the person they'd lost.

**What it means for you:** never use the words grieving people are already sick of hearing. No "move on", no "get over it", no "find closure", no "time to let go". If those words are on your homepage, you've just become one more person who doesn't understand.

Use their words instead. The people in our research wrote about wanting to "allow myself to grieve" and wanting to know they'd "fully processed grief in a healthy way". So say something like "You don't have to get over them. But you don't have to feel like this forever either." That's honest, it's gentle, and it tells them you're on their side.

## 3. "Am I grieving wrong? Will I be stuck like this forever?"

Underneath a lot of what people wrote was a fear that there was something wrong with them. That they were grieving the wrong way, or too long, or that they'd be "stuck in mourning and despair forever".

**What it means for you:** part of your job, and part of your marketing, is reassurance. A lot of grieving people simply need to hear that what they're feeling is normal. Say that on your website. Explain what grief tends to look like, without making it sound like a checklist, and say plainly that there's no right timetable.

But this is also where you need to know your limits. Some grief doesn't lift on its own and needs specialist help. It even has a name, [prolonged grief disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prolonged_grief_disorder), and it needs a trained therapist or doctor, not a coach. Grief after a suicide or a sudden, traumatic death can be the same. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so nobody will stop you taking those clients on. That's why you need to decide where your line is, say so on your website, and have people you trust to refer them to. Grieving people trust you more, not less, when you're honest about what you can and can't do.

## 4. "We lost her yesterday and I feel so empty"

Some people go looking for help within days. Others come years later, when they realise they never really grieved at all. Either way, they're in no state to make a big decision.

**What it means for you:** don't make your only option "book a call". 94.3% of the coaching websites we looked at ask a stranger to book something, and for a lot of them that's the only thing on the page. For someone who's grieving, picking up the phone to a stranger can feel impossible.

So give them a gentle first step they can take on their own. Here's an example, for a made-up grief coach who works with people who've lost a parent. It's just to show you the shape, so write yours in your own way.

> **Not ready to talk to anyone yet? That's alright.**
>
> I've written a short guide called *"The first Christmas without them"*. It's what I wish someone had given me the year my mum died. No sales, no follow-up calls, just a few pages to read when you're ready.
>
> Pop your email in and I'll send it straight over. If you ever want to talk, you'll know where I am.

That works because it doesn't ask for anything they're not ready to give. They can read it at three in the morning, and when they're ready for more, they already know you. And notice what's missing: no "limited time", no countdown, no pressure. In our research, urgency helped the buying decision in just 4.4% of the types of buyer we mapped. With grief, pressure is the quickest way to lose someone's trust for good.

## 5. Not all grief is the same

Grief sounds like one thing, but when we looked closer, it split into at least eight different kinds of loss, and each one needs something different:

- Losing a parent
- Losing a husband, wife or partner
- Losing a child
- Grief after a suicide or a sudden, traumatic death
- Grieving someone who's still alive, like a parent with dementia you're caring for
- Grief that hasn't lifted after years
- Grieving the end of a marriage
- Losing a pet, often made worse because other people don't take it seriously

**What it means for you:** pick the kind of loss you understand best, usually the one you've lived through, and speak to those people first. "I help people who've lost a parent and are trying to keep going for everyone else" is something a person recognises straight away. "Compassionate grief support for all types of loss" isn't.

Some of these are quieter areas where people are badly served. People grieving a pet, for example, often say the hardest part is that others treat their grief as silly. A grief coach who takes that seriously will mean a lot to them. Others, like losing a child or grief after a suicide, need a lot of skill and training, and you should only go there if you've got both.

## What should a grief coach's website say?

Put together, it looks like this. A headline that says who you help in their words, without "move on" anywhere near it. A short version of your own story. Reassurance that what they're feeling is normal. A plain explanation of what working with you looks like. Proof from past clients, with first names only if that's what they'd prefer. A gentle first step for people who aren't ready to talk, and a clear way to talk for those who are. And honesty about where coaching stops and other help begins.

We looked at 10,954 live coaching websites and checked each homepage for 10 things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch. Only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. Someone who's grieving has even less patience for a page they have to work out. ## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a grief coach?

Not by law, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. But grief is an area where training is especially worth having, both to help people well and to know when someone needs a therapist or doctor instead. Your own experience of loss counts for a lot with clients, but it's not a replacement for knowing your limits.

### What's the difference between a grief coach and a bereavement counsellor?

A bereavement counsellor is trained to work with the emotional and mental health side of grief, including grief that's become stuck or traumatic. A grief coach helps people find a way to live with their loss and take the next steps in their life. If someone needs a counsellor, a good grief coach tells them and helps them find one.

### How do I market grief coaching without feeling like I'm cashing in on people's pain?

By being gentle, honest and patient. Give people something useful for free, never use pressure, and let them come to you when they're ready. Your marketing should feel like the way you'd treat someone at a funeral: kind, quiet and there if they need you.
