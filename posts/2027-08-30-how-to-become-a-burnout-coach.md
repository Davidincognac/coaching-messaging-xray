To become a burnout coach, you need proper coach training with plenty of supervised practice, insurance and the basic admin. You also need to know, every time you see a client, whether you can help them on your own, whether their doctor needs to be involved, or whether they need urgent help instead. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no licence to get and nobody else is checking where your limits are.

![A woman with her head in her hands at her desk late at night](how-to-become-a-burnout-coach.jpg)

The simplest way I know to keep it straight is a traffic light that you check in every session, not just at the start. Green means you coach them yourself. Amber means you coach them while their GP or doctor looks after their health. Red means you stop coaching and point them to urgent help, today. Someone can start on green and move to amber a month later, so you keep watching.

The quotes below are copied word for word from our research. The things a coach might say are made-up examples.

## Burnout isn't a medical condition, but the symptoms still need a doctor

The World Health Organization describes burnout as [an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_burnout). In plain English, it's a problem caused by work, and the WHO says the word should only be used about work.

That helps you as a coach, because how someone works and how they get back on their feet are coaching conversations. Exhaustion, bad sleep, low mood and not being able to think straight can come from lots of things, though, including illnesses that have nothing to do with work. You can't tell the difference and shouldn't try, so the symptoms need a doctor.

## Picking one group of worn-out people to help

Burnout is a big area. We found 128 books written for people dealing with stress and burnout, and 13 things real people wrote when they were looking for help with it. On getting over burnout at work there were another 41 books and 12 of those real comments. That's a lot of people, too many to talk to at once, so pick one group.

**Managers and corporate high-performers.** We found 12 books on overworked corporate high-performers and executives. Sometimes the employer pays for coaching, rather than the person themselves. If you fancy working with senior people, [how to become an executive coach](/blog/how-to-become-an-executive-coach) is worth reading too.

**Health workers.** We found 6 books on burnout in healthcare and frontline work. Nurses in our research asked things like "How do you keep a healthy work-life balance as a nurse and avoid burnout?"

**Teachers and other caring jobs.** We only found 1 book aimed at teachers, social workers and therapists in our workplace burnout research, so we found very little written for this group. That could be a chance for you, or a sign fewer people are looking, so talk to teachers before you build anything.

**Founders and people who run their own business.** We found 7 books on founders and entrepreneurs running on empty. One founder wrote "I experienced severe burnout at a startup, but now I feel unchallenged and trapped at a large company... I feel empty and depleted". There's no manager to share the load with.

Whichever group you pick, watch how you describe what you do. In my opinion burnout coaches have the same problem as every other kind of coach. They use words only coaches use. One line we found was "regulate their nervous system, reclaim their energy, and reconnect with the life they actually want to live". Tell a worn-out nurse you'll help them reconnect with the life they want and you've told them nothing about what happens on Monday. The people you want to help say "I still feel exhausted" and "no amount of rest will help". Use their words on your website, not words from coaching courses.

## Green: coach on your own

Green is someone who is overloaded but still coping. They're going to work, they can still sleep well enough to function, and what they want is to change how they work before it gets worse. Here's how people in this group put it:

- "I like my job but recently I've got overloaded with work and deadlines which almost makes me hate it"
- "Am I burnt out or do I just hate my job?"
- "How do you handle burnout when quitting isn't an option"
- "What is the best way to avoid burn out as a startup founder?"

With someone on green, you help them look at what they've said yes to, what they could hand to someone else and how they'd talk to their manager about the workload. The founder who wrote that "most of the business' activities are not delegated" doesn't need a lecture about self-care. They need help working out what to let go of and who to give it to. Here's an example, made up, of how you might open:

> "From what you've told me, you're still getting through the week, but it's costing you more than it should. Let's look at where the time and energy are going, and what you could change first."

Even on green, ask every session how they're sleeping and whether they've seen their GP. If the answers worry you, treat them as amber or red instead.

## Amber: coach alongside a GP or doctor

Amber is someone who's signed off work, has had weeks of poor sleep, or has been exhausted for months with rest not touching it. You can still coach them, but only alongside their GP or doctor, and the doctor comes first. Here's how they put it:

- "I recently became burnt out from over exerting myself work wise. It's been a few months and I still feel exhausted"
- "I worked too hard for too long. I feel like no amount of rest will help"
- "for the past 3 days I just want to skip it all and cry"
- "How do I get out of the deep fog of startup founder depression?"

If they haven't seen their GP or doctor, ask them to book an appointment before you carry on with any big plans. Once they're seeing someone, your job is the practical side, like getting ready for the appointment or planning a slow return to work with their employer. You don't tell them what's wrong, you don't suggest supplements or treatments, and you don't push them back to work faster than their doctor is happy with.

Someone who wants to cry and skip it all, or who uses the word depression, needs one more step. Ask them plainly whether they've had any thoughts of harming themselves. It feels awkward, but the answer tells you whether you're still on amber or straight on to red. You might say something like this (made up):

> "I can help you plan how you get back to work and what you say to your boss. What I can't do is work out why you're still this exhausted after months, and that needs your GP to look at. Can you book an appointment this week? We'll keep going with the coaching alongside it."

## Red: stop coaching and point them to urgent help

Red is someone who can't function, or who tells you they've had thoughts of harming themselves or of not wanting to be here. At red you stop the coaching session. Carrying on as if it's a coaching problem could put them in danger.

Nobody in the burnout quotes we collected talked about self-harm, but be ready for it. The closest to red was this:

- "At work my emotions suddenly switched off, I suddenly had trouble processing what people were saying, couldn't form basic thoughts very well or do basic tasks I was used to, and my movements were very slow"

Problems with thinking, speaking or moving that come on suddenly can have medical causes that need checking straight away. If it's happening now, that's 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. If it's passed, they need to speak to a doctor or NHS 111 today, not wait for next week's session.

If someone tells you they're thinking of harming themselves, stay calm, stop the session and help them get help now. In the UK that means 999 in an emergency, or NHS 111 if it's urgent but not an emergency, and they can call Samaritans any time on 116 123. In the US they can call or text 988, or call 911 in an emergency. Here's a made-up example of what you could say:

> "Thank you for telling me. I'm going to stop the coaching here, because I want you to speak to someone who can help today. Can we look at who you're going to call right now?"

Write down what happened, keep it private, and talk it through with your supervisor. Don't pick the coaching back up until they're safe and being looked after.

## Training to become a burnout coach

You don't legally need a certificate, but you do need to be good at coaching. Look for training with plenty of live practice and someone watching you and giving feedback. If you've never trained as a coach at all, [how to become a life coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-life-coach) covers the basics of picking a course.

Then learn enough about stress, sleep and mental health, from reliable sources, to spot amber and red. You're learning the signs so you know when to send someone to their doctor, not so you can treat them.

Get supervision too, which means talking through your clients in confidence with an experienced coach who can spot when someone has drifted from green into amber.

## Insurance and setting up

**Insurance.** The law doesn't usually make coaches get insurance in the UK or the US, but get professional indemnity insurance anyway. It covers you if a client says your coaching cost them money or harmed them. Tell the insurer you work with stress and burnout, because some treat anything close to mental health differently.

**Data protection.** What clients tell you about their sleep, mood and time off sick is health information, and in the UK that's treated with extra care under data protection law. You may need to pay a small yearly fee to the ICO (the Information Commissioner's Office, the UK body that looks after data protection), and their website has a quick check that tells you.

**Tax.** In the UK you need to register as self-employed with HMRC. In the US you report your coaching income to the IRS on your tax return, and your state may have its own rules too. Check the rules where you live.

**Your client agreement and first session.** Say in plain words that you're a coach, that you don't diagnose or treat anything, and that you'll point them to their GP or urgent help if you're worried about their health or safety. Then ask the same simple questions in every first session, about sleep, work, their GP and any thoughts of harming themselves, so nobody slips through. [What to put on a mental health coach website](/blog/mental-health-coach-website) shows how to explain your limits on your site without scaring anyone off.

**Your offer.** Decide what someone actually gets, how long it lasts and what it costs. Worn-out people won't sign up for anything that sounds like hard work, so keep it simple. [How to build a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) walks you through it.

## FAQ

### Can I coach someone who has been signed off work with burnout?

Yes, as long as their GP or doctor is looking after them and you stick to the coaching side, such as planning their return to work. Don't push them back faster than their doctor advises.

### What should I do if a client mentions harming themselves?

Stop the coaching and help them get help now. In the UK that's 999, NHS 111 or Samaritans on 116 123, and in the US it's 988 or 911. Then talk it through with your supervisor.

### Do I need a qualification to become a burnout coach?

No law requires one in the UK or the US, since coaching isn't regulated in either. Training with plenty of real practice is still worth doing, and you need to know enough to spot when someone needs their doctor.
