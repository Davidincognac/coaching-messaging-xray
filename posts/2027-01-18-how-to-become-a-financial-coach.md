Money is one of those subjects where everybody thinks they know how it works, and hardly anybody feels in control of it. That's exactly why financial coaching can be such a good business. People are stressed, they're embarrassed, and they don't know who to ask.

But it's also the one kind of coaching where getting it wrong can land you in real trouble. Talk about someone's relationship and the worst that happens is bad advice. Tell someone which pension to move their savings into, and you could be breaking the law.

So I've done this one differently. There are seven things people tend to believe when they're thinking about becoming a financial coach. Some are half true, some are completely wrong, and one could get you into serious bother. Here's each one, and what's actually true.

![A couple at a kitchen table going through bills with a calculator and a laptop](financial-coach-kitchen-bills.jpg)

## Belief 1: "I'd need to be an expert in investing"

**What's actually true:** no, and in some ways it's better if investing isn't your thing.

Think about who actually goes looking for a financial coach. When we looked at the books people buy about money and what they write about it, money and wealth was one of the biggest subjects in our whole study, with 199 books. And the people in it weren't asking which fund to pick. They were writing things like "living pay check to pay check", "feeling like you're constantly broke" and "where or how do I start".

There's a whole group of people who can't hang on to their money for a month, never mind invest it. One person wrote "no matter how much I earn, I spend all of my salary in 2 weeks and the last week of the month is very hard for me". That person doesn't need an investment expert. They need someone who can help them change what they do with money, week by week, without making them feel stupid.

That's coaching. It's about behaviour, habits and decisions, not about picking stocks.

## Belief 2: "I can tell people what to do with their money"

**What's actually true:** this is the one that can get you into trouble, so read it twice.

In the UK, giving advice on specific investments, pensions or mortgages is regulated by the [Financial Conduct Authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_Conduct_Authority). So is debt advice, which surprises plenty of people. If you're not authorised, you can't tell someone which pension to pick, whether to move their savings into a particular fund, or which debt solution to sign up for. In the US, giving personal investment advice for money generally means registering as an investment adviser. Other countries have their own rules.

What a financial coach can do is still a lot:

- Help someone understand where their money actually goes
- Help them build a budget they'll stick to
- Help them work out what they want their money to do for them
- Help a couple talk about money without it turning into a row
- Help someone stop avoiding their bank statements
- Explain how things work in general, like what a pension is or how interest adds up
- Point them to a regulated adviser, or a free debt charity, when they need one

The line is roughly this. You can help people understand and change their behaviour. You can't recommend a specific financial product or tell them which debt solution to take. I'm not a lawyer, so check the exact rules where you live before you start, and say clearly on your website what you do and don't do. Done well, that line is a selling point. "I won't sell you anything and I don't earn commission from anyone" is a sentence a nervous person wants to read.

## Belief 3: "Financial coaching is for people in debt"

**What's actually true:** some of it is, but there are plenty of other groups, and they want completely different things.

Here's how it broke down in our research, by how many books we found on each:

- **Money and wealth in general**, 199 books, including money mindset (45) and investing for beginners (38).
- **Frugal living and spending less**, 48 books. People who "read multiple books, magazines, and blogs" to make a budget and "failed miserably in the first month".
- **Retirement**, 40 books. Including people like the one who wrote "I'm 48 and have to start over... I've virtually no savings, and no retirement".
- **Getting out of debt**, 27 books. "The interest and fees keep adding up."
- **Financial independence for women**, 26 books. Women who feel "guilty, useless and even resentful about being financially dependent".
- **Couples and money**, 19 books. "My husband's spending is out of control."

The person who's 48 with no pension, the couple who row about spending and the woman who wants to stop relying on her partner all need a financial coach. But they'd never pick the same one. Pick the group you understand best, usually the one you've been in.

## Belief 4: "I need to be good with money myself"

**What's actually true:** you need to be good with money now. You don't need to have always been.

In fact, some of the best financial coaches got there the hard way. They were in debt, they lived month to month, they hid letters from the bank, and then they sorted it out. That story is worth more to a nervous client than any qualification, because it says "I know how embarrassing this feels, and I know it can change".

But you do need to have sorted it. Nobody wants money help from someone who's still in the middle of the mess. And you need to know your stuff. There are financial coaching courses, and in my opinion one is worth doing, both for the coaching skills and to learn exactly where the legal line is.

## Belief 5: "People will pay because money is important"

**What's actually true:** money being important doesn't make people buy. Shame is the thing that stops them.

People are embarrassed about money in a way they aren't about almost anything else. They'll tell a friend about their divorce before they'll tell them about their overdraft. So the biggest thing your website has to do isn't convince them money is important. They already know. It's make them feel safe enough to get in touch.

That means no judgement anywhere on the page. No "stop wasting money on coffee". No "people are just lazy with money". It means real stories from people who were in the same mess and got out. And it means a gentle first step for people who aren't ready to talk yet. A free spending tracker, a short guide to the first week of sorting your money out. Something they can use on their own at the kitchen table without anybody seeing. [The lead magnet post](/blog/lead-magnet-ideas-for-coaches) goes into what makes a good one.

## Belief 6: "It's all about mindset"

**What's actually true:** mindset helps, but on its own it's not enough, and plenty of buyers know it.

Money mindset is a big subject. It had 45 books in our research, and lots of financial coaches lead with it. "Heal your money story." "Clear your money blocks." For some people that's exactly what they need, especially people who feel anxious spending money even when they've got enough. One person wrote "Growing up, I lived in poverty. I am stable now, but I can't spend my money without feeling anxious".

But for someone who's three months behind on their rent, "heal your relationship with money" sounds like a luxury. They want to know what to do on Monday. So if mindset is your thing, link it to something practical. "We'll work out why you spend the way you do, then build a plan you'll actually stick to" works far better than mindset alone.

## Belief 7: "Couples will come to me together"

**What's actually true:** usually it's one of them, and the other one doesn't know they're looking.

Look at what people in our research wrote about couples and money. "How do I get my spouse to spend less money?" "How do I talk to a significant other about their poor saving/spending habits without them getting mad?" One person wrote that when they'd tried to talk about it, their partner said "he's the head of the household".

So the person on your website is worried, and probably on their own. If you work with couples, speak to them first. Say you can start with just them. Say how you'd help them raise it with their partner. And if what they're describing sounds like one partner controlling the other's money, that's not just a budgeting problem, and you need to know where to point them for proper help. [The relationship coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-relationship-coach) covers more on selling to one person about two.

## What your first session could look like

Here's a made-up example of a first session with a couple, to show you what financial coaching actually is in practice. It's the shape that's useful, not the detail.

Before the session, they each list every regular payment going out, separately. When they arrive, you put the two lists side by side. Straight away there's a surprise, because there always is. A subscription one of them forgot about. A loan the other didn't know was still running. You don't judge any of it. You just get it all on one page.

Then you ask each of them what they want their money to do. Not a budget yet, just what they want. Usually one says "stop worrying" and the other says "a holiday", and that's the real conversation. By the end, they've got one shared picture of where the money goes, one thing each they want, and one small change to try before you meet again.

No products sold, no investment tips, nothing regulated. Just two people finally looking at the same numbers. That's the job.

When you turn that into something to sell, make it a package with a clear end point, like three months to get on top of your money as a couple, rather than single sessions. [The coaching offer post](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you how to set it out, and [how to get your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) covers where the first people usually come from.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to be a financial coach?

Coaching itself isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no legal requirement to be a financial coach. But giving regulated financial advice is a different thing and needs authorisation. A good financial coaching course will teach you where that line is, which on its own makes it worth doing.

### What's the difference between a financial coach and a financial adviser?

A financial adviser is regulated and can recommend specific products, like pensions, investments or mortgages. A financial coach helps people change how they handle money day to day, like budgeting, spending and talking about money. A good financial coach knows when someone needs an adviser and tells them.

### Can a financial coach help someone in serious debt?

A coach can help someone with their budget and their habits, but in the UK debt advice is regulated, and in places like the US there are rules too. Someone in serious debt should talk to a free debt charity or a regulated debt adviser. You can work alongside that help, not instead of it.
