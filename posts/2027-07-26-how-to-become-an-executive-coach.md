To become an executive coach, you need to be able to coach senior people without being thrown by their status, and understand how companies work, including who signs off the money. You also need to ask questions a leader hasn't thought of, agree with the leader and whoever's paying what the coaching is for, keep things confidential, and give buyers a good reason to trust you. There's no licence to get first, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so anyone can call themselves an executive coach tomorrow. That's exactly why the title on its own won't get you hired, and the skills below will.

![An executive coach in a blue coat talking with a senior leader outside an office](how-to-become-an-executive-coach.jpg)

Below, you score yourself from 1 to 5 on each skill, then see how to raise the weak ones. Be hard on yourself, because the people you want to coach will be.

## Who you'd be coaching and who'd be paying

In our buying research we read books written for different kinds of buyer, to see how they describe their own problems. More books means more people looking for help. Leadership is one of the biggest areas we looked at, with 136 books, and founder and executive coaching has another 46. Inside those, 21 books are aimed at leaders at the very top, like the chief executive or finance director, and 11 at founders and CEOs whose business is growing fast.

The leadership buyers we found are often people newly put in charge of a team, and they write things like "I am stuck between the manager and team members" and "my teammates don't respect me and don't listen to me". Founders sound different again. They talk about "the deep fog of startup founder depression" and being "constantly overwhelmed".

There's also the money side. Sometimes the leader pays for coaching themselves. Other times the company pays, and then you've got two people to win over, the leader who'll be coached and the person in HR, or the boss, who signs it off. I haven't been paid by a company to coach one of its leaders, so I'm not going to pretend I know that side from the inside.

## Skill one: coaching senior people without being intimidated

Picture a finance director of a big company sitting opposite you, slightly impatient, checking their watch. Would you ask them the awkward question, or would you go soft and agree with them? Give yourself a 5 if you'd ask it calmly, and a 1 if you'd nod along and hope the hour went quickly.

If you tend to talk too much to fill silences, apologise for your questions, or list your qualifications before anyone's asked, you're nearer the bottom.

To raise it, spend time with senior people wherever you can, like local business groups, charity boards or old colleagues who've moved up, and notice how ordinary they are. Then practise coaching people who are more senior than you, even for free at first, and notice that the good ones like being challenged. Senior people get agreed with all day, so a coach who just agrees is no use to them.

## Skill two: understanding how companies work and who pays

Could you explain how a company makes its money, what a board does (the group who oversee the company), what a budget year is (the twelve months a company plans its spending over), and who would sign off a coaching invoice? Score 5 if you could explain all of that to a friend over a coffee, and 1 if half of those words make you nervous.

Low scorers coach the person as if they live on their own, when in fact they've got a boss, a team, targets and a budget that runs out at a set time of year.

To raise it, read the annual reports that a lot of companies publish, and talk to people you know who work in HR, finance or management. Ask them how training and coaching gets paid for where they work, and who decides. If you're weighing up which kind of leader to go after, [our guide to high ticket coaching niches](/blog/high-ticket-coaching-niches) looks at who can actually afford it.

## Skill three: asking questions that feel like advice

This is the one I know best from my own selling, because it's what lost me sales.

For me, it was never about price. It was always about whether the person thought their outcome was worth it, and whether I could help them achieve it. Anything that lost me a sale was me giving advice in the call. The questions I ask should be advice in themselves. The person thinks "I didn't think of that question", and that is seen as advice. That comes from selling book publishing, masterminds and international retreats.

Now look back at your last three coaching or sales conversations. How many times did the other person stop and say something like "good question, I hadn't thought of that"? If it happens in nearly every conversation, that's a 5. If you can't remember it ever happening, that's a 1.

A low score looks like jumping in with answers, telling stories about what you'd do, or asking the same safe questions every coach asks, like "what does success look like for you?".

To raise it, after each conversation write down the question that got the biggest reaction and the one that got nothing, and build your own list. Before a call, think about the person's actual situation and work out what question nobody around them is likely to ask. For example, with the new manager "stuck between the manager and team members", you might ask what their own boss would say the team is for, and whether the team would give the same answer. [How to run a coaching discovery call](/blog/coaching-discovery-call) goes through how to run that first conversation.

## Skill four: agreeing the work with everyone involved

When a company pays, there are usually three parties: the leader being coached, their boss or someone from HR, and you. Each one wants something slightly different, and you've got to agree at the start what the coaching is for, how long it runs, what gets reported back and what doesn't.

Imagine the leader's boss stops you halfway through and asks "so how's it going with them?". Write down now what you'd say. Score 5 if you already know the answer because it was agreed at the start, and 1 if you'd make it up on the spot.

When this goes wrong, it's usually a vague start where nobody writes anything down. Three months in, the boss expects a progress report, the leader expects total privacy, and you're stuck in the middle.

To raise it, write a simple agreement before any coaching starts. It should say what the coaching is aiming to change, how many sessions there are, what you'll tell the company (for example, attendance and the agreed goals) and what you won't (what the leader says in sessions). Get everyone to agree it, ideally in one short meeting. If it's a founder paying for themselves, it's just the two of you, but write it down anyway.

## Skill five: keeping confidences

Senior people tell a coach things they can't tell anyone at work, and founders in our research worry that admitting they're struggling will look like weakness to their investors or their team. If they think what they say will get back to their boss, their board or their team, they'll stop being honest with you, and the coaching becomes a waste of everybody's money.

Have you ever repeated something a client or colleague told you in confidence, even as a funny story with the name taken out? Score 5 if you never have and never would, and 1 if you've done it more than once. Name-dropping clients online, or letting slip to HR something the leader said in a session, both count against you.

To raise it, decide your rules before you need them and put them in your written agreement. Never use a client's name, company or story without written permission. Be clear about where confidentiality stops, for example if someone is at risk of harm. That last point is important because founders in our research talk about depression, and a coach can't diagnose or treat that. If a leader sounds like they're struggling with their mental health, the kind thing is to suggest they speak to their doctor, alongside or instead of coaching.

## Skill six: earning credibility

Credibility is the big one for executive coaching. In our research, founders trust people with a real track record of running things, and they want to hear from other founders who've been through the same thing.

Imagine a busy chief executive looks you up for 30 seconds. Would they find a clear reason to take you seriously, like relevant experience, a specific kind of leader you help, or results you can show with permission? Score 5 if yes, and 1 if all they'd find is a nice photo and the words "executive coach".

A low score looks like a LinkedIn headline that just says "Executive Coach", or borrowing the same lines as everyone else. We found coaches in this area promising to help leaders "see your blind spots, make faster decisions, and grow into the leader your company needs you to be". Nobody describes their own problem like that, and it sounds the same as the next coach.

To raise it, be specific about who you help and why you're the right person, using your real background. Someone who spent years running sales teams could help new sales managers, and a former founder could help founders in their first year. Say it in the leader's words, not coaching talk, so a founder sees "get rid of that lonely feeling as a startup founder" rather than a line about blind spots. [What the leader and the person paying look for on an executive coach website](/blog/executive-coach-website) shows how to put all of this on your site.

## Adding up your score

Add your six scores together. If you scored 24 or more, you're ready to start talking to leaders now, and your job is to find them. Between 15 and 23, you can start, but pick your two lowest scores and work on those first, because a senior buyer will spot them. Under 15, get more practice coaching people more senior than you before you call yourself an executive coach.

From my own selling, skill three is where the sale is won or lost. Skill six is the other one to watch, because the founders in our research lean so heavily on track record when they decide who to trust.

## Setting up: training, insurance and registering

In the UK you can start as a sole trader by registering with HMRC. In the US the simplest start is usually as a sole proprietor, which just means you run the business in your own name. Professional indemnity insurance is worth having, because it covers you if a client says your work cost them money. A company buyer may also ask what training you've had, so a recognised coaching course can help, even though it isn't a legal requirement. Data protection rules also apply to the notes you keep on clients. The details vary, so check the rules where you live.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become an executive coach?

No. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no legal requirement. Some companies may still ask what training you've done before they'll pay, so it can help you get hired.

### Can I become an executive coach without being an executive?

Yes, but it's harder to win trust. Be honest about your background, pick leaders it fits, and let your questions show them you understand their world.

### Who pays for executive coaching?

A founder will usually be paying for themselves, because there's no company budget above them. When a company pays, the money usually comes from HR or the leader's boss, so you need to win over both people.

### How is executive coaching different from life coaching?

The basic skill of coaching is the same. The difference is that you're working with someone inside a company, so their boss, their team and whoever's paying are part of the picture too.
