To become a divorce coach, you need three things before you need a logo. First, you need to understand what a divorce feels like at each stage from the client's side. Second, you need to know which parts of it are your job and which belong to a solicitor, a mediator, a financial adviser or a therapist. And third, you need to describe the situation the way the person living it would.

![A man sitting alone at a kitchen table with a mug, boxes behind him](how-to-become-a-divorce-coach.jpg)

Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no licence to get, and the setting up is covered near the end.

A divorce is a string of different problems that turn up one after another, sometimes over years. So let's go through it in the order the client lives it.

## Stage one: thinking about leaving

Nobody has said the word divorce out loud yet, or if they have, it was in an argument. One person in our research put it like this: "My husband and I live like roommates. What should I do?". For others the children are what keeps them stuck: "I am unhappy in my relationship. We have 2 young kids together. Is it selfish to want to leave or should I stay for the kids' happiness?".

Your part here is asking questions that help them see what they want and what they're scared of. It isn't your job to tell anybody to leave or to stay.

If both of them want to try to save the marriage, a couples counsellor or therapist is often a better fit. If the person is low day after day, or struggling to sleep or eat, they should see their GP or doctor. And if they talk about not wanting to be here, that isn't coaching. Point them to emergency help if they could be in danger now, or to their GP or doctor straight away.

## Stage two: deciding

Something happens that they can't explain away. People talk about the last straw a lot. "When did you know your marriage was over? How did you know this is it, it's over? What's the last straw?". Sometimes it's an affair: "My husband is having an affair, and he is not ending it. He doesn't want a divorce though". And even once they've decided, the guilt is still there: "How do I leave an unhappy marriage and not feel bad about leaving him?".

A coach is a real help with the next few weeks. Who do they need to talk to first? What do they need ready before they tell their partner? How and when will they tell the children?

What they're entitled to and what they should or shouldn't sign are questions for a solicitor in the UK or a lawyer in the US. You can help them write down what to ask, but you don't answer it.

## Stage three: the legal and money part

This is the stage where a coach can easily end up doing a solicitor's job. The client is scared and wants answers, so they ask you what they're entitled to, whether they'll keep the house or what they should sign. It's tempting to answer, and the moment you do, you're giving legal or money advice you're not qualified to give.

Our research also shows what people really want help with here. We found 8 books on money and divorce or separation and another 6 on financial recovery after divorce. But the questions we collected from people going through divorce are about feelings, loneliness and blame, and none of them are about money or the legal process. One person wrote "My parents always blame me for the failure of my marriage that I did not endure much. How do I deal with that? It hurts me a lot". Someone else had it from their partner: "My husband says I am the problem in our marriage because he doesn't even talk much".

So your job at this stage is to keep them steady while the legal side goes on. Help them get organised, prepare for meetings with their solicitor, avoid big decisions made in a temper, and cope with the blame from family.

Legal advice belongs with a solicitor or lawyer. Splitting money, the house and pensions belongs with a solicitor and, for the money side, a regulated financial adviser. A mediator helps the couple agree things without going to court. In England and Wales, people usually have to go to a meeting about mediation before they can apply to court about children or money, though there are exceptions, including where there's been domestic abuse. The rules are different in Scotland, Northern Ireland and each US state, so check the rules where you live, and never tell a client what they're entitled to.

## Stage four: the first year alone

The papers might be done, but this is often the hardest part. The house is empty and the routine has gone. "I initiated my divorce and now I feel so lonely and lost. I went from always having somebody home to nobody". And "What do I do after divorce life becomes blank? I don't understand from where to start".

This is where coaching really helps. You can help them build a week that works, with new routines, people to see and small goals, and help them get through the first Christmas and the first weekend the children spend with the other parent.

Keep an eye on how heavy things are getting, though. If the grief or loneliness turns into weeks of not getting out of bed, they should see their GP or doctor straight away, and your coaching carries on alongside that rather than replacing it. If they mention thoughts of self-harm and could be in danger now, they need emergency help: 999 or 911, or A&E or the ER.

## Stage five: rebuilding

Later, people start to look forward. They wrote things like "How can I recover and feel more comfortable with myself?" and "How do I get over my failed marriage and move on?". One wrote "at 55 I want to feel loved before I go".

By now they've got some energy back and want to get on with things. You can help them set goals for the life they want now, whether that's work, money habits, friendships, a new home or dating again when they're ready.

Long-term financial planning is for a financial adviser, and if old patterns keep repeating, like always ending up with the same kind of partner, a therapist may be a better fit for that part.

## Safety comes before coaching

You'll meet people in divorce who aren't safe. Some are leaving abusive relationships, and some are scared of their ex. Coaching can't fix this, so say on your website and in your first conversation that if someone isn't safe, they need to contact the people below first.

If someone is in danger right now, they should call the emergency services: 999 in the UK or 911 in the US. In England, the National Domestic Abuse Helpline is free and open 24 hours on 0808 2000 247. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own helplines. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is on 1-800-799-7233. Check the numbers where you live before you put them up.

Children need thinking about too. If you're ever worried a child is at risk, that goes to the police or children's services, not into a coaching plan, and your terms should say so. If a client talks about harming themselves, point them to emergency help if they could be in danger now, or to their doctor straight away.

## The mistake I see divorce coaches make with their marketing

A lot of divorce coaches make their marketing a reflection of their own story, what they went through and what helped them. They don't understand the audience. They think the audience is them. A broken clock is right twice a day, and that's what they get with their marketing.

Picture a made-up homepage that tells the coach's own story, from the night they packed a bag, through the court dates, to getting back on their feet on a retreat a year later.

Now picture two people reading it. The first is in stage one, still asking whether it's selfish to leave when there are two young kids at home. They haven't packed a bag and they're not sure they want to, so nothing on that page is about them. The second is in stage four, the person who wrote "I went from always having somebody home to nobody". The court dates are behind them, and a retreat a year away doesn't help with this Saturday. Both of them close the page. The only reader who sticks around is the one whose divorce happened to go the same way as the coach's. That's the broken clock.

There's nothing wrong with having your story on your site, and [how to become a relationship coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-relationship-coach) explains why it helps. It just needs to come after the reader's situation, so they see themselves first and you second.

The other thing they're not good at is describing the situation. They come out with coaching phrases like "clarity" and "abundance". Only coaches talk that way. People going through a divorce don't write like that. They write "What do I do after divorce life becomes blank?".

So pick a stage, read how people at that stage talk, and use their words on your page. Every quote from people going through divorce in this post is copied exactly from what they wrote in our research. ## How to become a divorce coach: training, insurance and setting up

**Pick your stage.** You don't have to cover the whole divorce. Picking one stage, like the first year alone or rebuilding later in life, makes you much easier to understand. [How to choose a coaching niche](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) helps you decide, and if you're wondering whether people will pay properly for it, [our guide to high-ticket coaching niches](/blog/high-ticket-coaching-niches) is worth a read.

**Get trained.** You don't legally need a qualification, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. But learn to coach properly, with lots of practice on real people and someone giving you feedback. Learn the basics of how divorce works where you live too, but knowing it isn't the same as being allowed to advise on it.

**Get insured.** Professional indemnity insurance covers you if a client claims your coaching was negligent and cost them money or caused them loss. Public liability is worth having if you meet people in person.

**Set up the business.** In the UK you can start as a sole trader by registering with HMRC. In the US the simplest start is usually as a sole proprietor, though the rules vary by state. Write a simple client agreement that says what coaching is and when you'll point someone to other help, and keep a list of local solicitors, mediators, financial advisers and therapists. Check the rules where you live.

**Then go and talk to people** about where they are in their divorce, and write down the words they use.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a divorce coach?

No. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so you don't legally need a certificate. Proper training with real practice is still worth doing, and you must not give legal or financial advice unless you're qualified to.

### What's the difference between a divorce coach and a solicitor?

A solicitor, or a lawyer in the US, gives legal advice and handles the legal process. A divorce coach helps the person cope, get organised and prepare for their time with the solicitor.

### Can a divorce coach come to mediation or court with a client?

Sometimes, but it isn't up to you. A mediator may not allow anyone extra in the room, so a coach often helps the client prepare beforehand and talk it through afterwards. Courts have their own rules about who can sit with someone and what they can do, and a coach can't speak for a client the way a lawyer can. Check the rules where you live before you offer it.

### Do I need to have been divorced myself?

No, but if you have, it can help, as long as you remember your clients' divorces aren't the same as yours. Spend time listening to how people describe their own situation before you write anything about it.
