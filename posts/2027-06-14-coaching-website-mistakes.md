If your coaching website isn't bringing in clients, the mistake depends on where people stop. If nobody's visiting, it's usually because nobody can find the site, or it's written in words nobody searches for. If people visit and don't book, I'd bet they can't tell in a few seconds who you help, or they can't see any proof. When people book calls but don't buy, they often booked without knowing enough, or the call never found out what they really wanted. And if people take your freebie but never book, the freebie probably has nothing to do with your paid work, and they heard nothing useful from you afterwards.

![A woman pointing at a problem on a coaching website while a man holds his head in his hands](coaching-website-mistakes.jpg)

The mistakes I see again and again are a headline that doesn't say who you help, no proof, no price, too many buttons, and a freebie that has nothing to do with your paid work. So don't start by changing the colours or buying a new template. Start with what you can actually see, and follow it back.

## Start here: which one is you?

Open whatever tells you how many people visit your site. That might be Google Analytics, or the stats page inside Wix, Squarespace or WordPress. Then look at the last three months and answer four questions in order:

1. Are more than a handful of people visiting each week?
2. Of those visitors, is anyone booking a call or sending an enquiry?
3. Of the people who book a call, is anyone paying?
4. If you have a freebie, are the people who download it ever booking a call?

The first question where the answer is "no" is your starting point. Go to that section below and ignore the rest for now. There's no point fixing your discovery call if nobody's visiting, and there's no point chasing more visitors if the ones you've got all leave.

## Symptom 1: nobody visits

First, check that Google even knows the site is there. Type "site:" followed by your web address into Google, with no space, like site:yourname.com. If nothing comes up, Google hasn't found your pages yet. The fix is to sign up for Google Search Console, which is free, and tell it your site exists.

The next mistake is waiting for Google to do all the work. A new site with five pages won't show up for "life coach" or "career coach" any time soon, because thousands of older sites are already there. In the early days your visitors come from you sending people there, from talks, conversations, emails, referrals and the odd social post. If that's not happening, the site has nothing to work with.

Another common one is writing in your words, not theirs. Nobody types "holistic transformation" into Google. They type the problem. In the research we did on what buyers write about their problems, career buyers wrote things like "I've basically sat still in the same position because I'm too scared to jump". Dating buyers wrote "I'm tired of being single". Those are the kind of words that get typed into a search box at eleven at night. If your pages don't use words like that, you won't be found for them.

**The fix:** write one page at a time that answers one question your clients ask, in their words. Put a plain sentence at the top that answers it straight away. Then keep sending people to the site yourself while Google catches up. If you'd rather not rely on posting online for that, [our guide to getting coaching clients without social media](/blog/coaching-clients-without-social-media) goes through eight other routes.

## Symptom 2: people visit, but nobody books

This is the one I come across all the time, and it's nearly always the homepage. When we score a coaching homepage, we check it for ten things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch. Here are the ones that cause this symptom, in the order I'd check them.

Start with whether a stranger can tell who it's for in five seconds. When we checked 10,954 coaching websites, only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds. The usual mistake is a headline that's your name, or something like "Find your inner power". It sounds nice to you, but the person who's just arrived doesn't know if you help with work, marriage, money or grief. So they leave. The fix is to say who you help and what changes for them in the first thing they read. [The five second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check your own.

Then look at whether the problem is named clearly. Some sites say who they help ("women over 40") but never say what with. Others name the topic ("confidence coaching") but not the situation the reader is actually in. The fix is a line that describes their week right now, the way they'd say it.

Next, can they see what you're selling? A lot of coaches say how they feel about coaching but never say what the client actually gets. Say how many sessions there are, over how long, and what it costs, or at least where prices start. Plenty of people won't book a call just to find out what the thing is. They'll leave instead. The ones you want book a call when they already think it might be right.

After that, check for proof. About two thirds of the coaching homepages we checked had no proof on them at all. There were no testimonials or client stories, nothing a stranger could check. Coaching is a leap of faith for the buyer, and without proof they're being asked to trust someone they've never met. The fix is two or three short, specific testimonials near the top, with the client's permission. [How to get coaching testimonials](/blog/coaching-testimonials) has the exact message to send and the questions that get useful answers. If you haven't got any clients yet, use what you've got honestly, like a past career, a qualification, or a story from someone you coached for free.

Then count the buttons. Some sites have five buttons going five different places: book a call, join my group, download this, follow me, buy my book. The visitor doesn't know which one you want, so they do none of them. Pick one main action for people who are ready, which is usually booking a call, and repeat it down the page.

Last, is there something of you on the page? Put a real photo on it, a few lines in your own voice, and a story that connects to the reader's situation. Your CV can go further down. People are buying an hour with a person, so let them see one.

If you want the full list in order, [what to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage) lays it out section by section.

## Symptom 3: people book calls, but don't buy

If people are booking, your site is doing a lot right. The problem is either that the wrong people are booking, or something's going wrong on the call itself.

The first mistake here is a site so vague that the wrong people book. If your homepage could be about anything, some people book a call just to find out what you do, and plenty of them were never going to buy. The fix is the same as symptom 2, a sharper headline, a clear offer and a price or a starting price, so the people who book already know roughly what they're getting into. You'll get fewer calls and better ones, and that's a good trade.

The second is hiding the price until the end of the call. If someone hears your price for the first time after 25 minutes of opening up, it can feel like a trap, and they'll say they need to think about it. Showing at least a starting price on the site takes that shock away.

The third is not finding out what they actually want. Two people can book a call about the same problem and want completely different things from it. If your call doesn't find that out, you end up pitching the wrong reason.

**The fix:** spend the first half of the call asking and listening, then say back what you heard before you say anything about how you'd help. [Our minute-by-minute guide to the coaching discovery call](/blog/coaching-discovery-call) shows you how to run it, including when to say the price and the follow-up email to send afterwards.

## Symptom 4: people take the freebie, but never book

The biggest mistake here is a freebie that has nothing to do with your paid coaching. If you coach people through career changes and your freebie is "10 tips for a morning routine", you'll attract people who like morning routines. They'll take the tips and leave. The freebie should be the first small step towards the problem you get paid to solve.

The next is that people hear nothing after they download it. Plenty of coaches set up the form, send the PDF and then don't write again for months. By the time the coach writes again, the person has forgotten who they are. The fix is a short run of emails over the next week or two that are useful on their own, with a plain invitation to book a call in at least one of them.

The last is follow-up that feels like a machine. If every email is clearly an automated sequence with a countdown timer, people feel like they've been put on a conveyor belt. Write like a person, ask them to reply, and when someone does, answer them yourself.

A word on the rules here. In the UK you usually need people's permission before you send them marketing emails, and in the US every marketing email has to include a way to unsubscribe. Check the rules where you live.

**The fix:** make the freebie a small, specific first step towards your paid work, follow up within a day, and give people a reason to reply. ## What if it's more than one?

It often is. Go back to the four questions at the top and fix the first "no" first. In my opinion it's usually the homepage, because that's where a lot of the other problems start.

It's very hard to read your own website the way a stranger does, because you already know what you meant. So show it to someone who doesn't know what you do and ask them who it's for. If they can't tell you, that's where to start.

## FAQ

### What are the biggest coaching website mistakes?

The ones that cost coaches clients again and again are a headline that doesn't say who you help or what with, no proof from real clients, no clear offer or price, and too many buttons asking for different things. Fix those first, in that order.

### Why is my coaching website getting visitors but no enquiries?

Usually because a stranger can't tell in a few seconds whether you can help them, or can't see anything that makes them trust you. Rewrite the headline in your clients' words and add two or three specific testimonials near the top.

### Should I show my prices on my coaching website?

I think you should show at least a starting price. It stops people booking calls just to find out what you charge, and it takes away the shock at the end of a call.

### How long before a new coaching website brings in clients?

On its own, through Google, it can take many months. In the meantime the site's job is to convince the people you send there yourself, from conversations, talks and referrals.
