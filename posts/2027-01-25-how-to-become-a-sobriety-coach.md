Nearly every sobriety coach I've come across is sober themselves. They had their rock bottom, or their slow realisation that the wine every night had got out of hand, and they came through it. Then they looked back and thought, I know exactly how hard that first month is, and I could help someone through it.

That lived experience is the best thing you've got. But it's not enough on its own, and I'll show you why by doing something a bit different with this post.

Below are two made-up sobriety coaches, Dan and Kerry. They're both four years sober. They did the same coaching course in the same month. They're both kind, both good listeners, and both completely serious about helping people. A year after starting, Dan has two clients and is thinking about giving up. Kerry has a waiting list.

The difference isn't talent or luck. It's a handful of decisions, and you can make the same ones Kerry did.

![Two men walking and talking on a coastal path next to a beach](sobriety-coach-beach-walk.jpg)

## Before anything else: the one thing you must know

There's something every sobriety coach has to understand before they take on a single client. Stopping drinking suddenly can be dangerous for someone who drinks heavily every day. [Alcohol withdrawal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_withdrawal_syndrome) can cause seizures, and in serious cases it can kill. That's not a coaching problem. It's a medical one.

So if someone drinks heavily every day, they need to speak to a doctor before they stop, and you need to tell them that, clearly, before you do anything else. Both Dan and Kerry know this. Kerry also puts it on her website, which we'll come back to.

The same goes for drugs. A coach isn't a detox service or a rehab. You're there for the life around the not-drinking, not the medical side of stopping.

## Decision 1: who they help

**Dan** helps "anyone who wants to stop drinking". He thinks that gives him the biggest pool of people to work with.

**Kerry** helps women in their 40s who drink a bottle of wine nearly every night, don't think of themselves as alcoholics, and want to stop before it gets worse.

Kerry's choice comes straight out of the research. When we looked at the books people buy about addiction and recovery and what they write about it, we found 40 books, and they split into very different groups. Alcohol addiction and sobriety had 12. Smoking had 9. Grey-area drinking had 6, from people who want to cut down or stop without calling themselves an alcoholic. Drug addiction had 5. The rest were spread across gambling, porn, food, and families of people with an addiction.

Those groups want completely different things. Someone who nearly went to prison because of their drinking and someone who's worried about their nightly glass of wine aren't going to recognise themselves in the same website. Dan's "anyone" means nobody reads his page and thinks, that's me. Kerry picked the group she came from, and every one of them who finds her page does.

## Decision 2: the words on the front page

Here's the top of Dan's homepage:

> **Reclaim your life from alcohol and step into your sober era.**
> Transformational sobriety coaching to help you break free and thrive.

And here's Kerry's:

> **Fine for the first few days, and then Friday comes?**
> I help women in their 40s stop drinking without having to call themselves an alcoholic, and without losing their social life.

Both are made up, to show you the shape. But look at where Kerry's words came from. People in our research wrote "I do fine the first few days, but I always end up slipping up." They wrote "Ever since I stopped drinking, I feel like I have lost my social life." Kerry just put those two worries at the top of her page.

Dan's words are the ones you'll see on half the sobriety websites out there. "Reclaim." "Break free." "Thrive." There's nothing wrong with them, but nobody types them into Google at eleven o'clock at night with a glass in their hand. We looked at 10,954 coaching websites, and only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. Kerry's passes that test. Dan's doesn't. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check yours.

## Decision 3: the label

This one's easy to miss. Dan's website talks about "addiction" and "alcoholism" all the way through. That's honest, and for some people it's exactly right. Someone who's hit rock bottom often needs to hear the word.

But Kerry's people would close the page. A lot of grey-area drinkers don't think of themselves as alcoholics, and being told they are is the fastest way to make them decide they're fine and pour another glass. So Kerry never uses the word unless a client does first. She talks about "drinking more than you want to" and "wine o'clock getting earlier".

Neither is right for everyone. The point is that the label has to match the person. If you work with people at rock bottom, use their words. If you work with grey-area drinkers, use theirs.

## Decision 4: what they actually sell

**Dan** sells sessions. £X an hour, book as many as you like.

**Kerry** sells a 90-day programme built around the moments people slip. It covers the first week, because people wrote "how do I get past the first week when quitting drinking?" It covers the first Friday night, the first birthday party and the first holiday. And it covers the bit nobody warns you about, which is the loneliness. People in our research wrote about losing their friends and asked "what activities can you do when you quit alcohol?" So Kerry's programme includes a small group where clients meet other people doing the same thing.

Kerry is selling getting through the hard bits. Dan is selling his time. Someone who's tried to stop five times before knows which one they need. [The coaching offer post](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you how to put a programme together like Kerry's.

## Decision 5: proof

**Dan** has three testimonials. All of them say something like "Dan is amazing, highly recommend."

**Kerry** has stories, with first names only, because sobriety is private. "I used to start on the wine while making the kids' tea. 200 days now, and I actually remember bedtime stories." "I went to my sister's 40th and didn't drink, and nobody even asked." Each one names a moment the reader is dreading.

Two thirds of the coaching websites we looked at showed no proof at all, so even Dan is ahead of plenty. But proof that sounds like real life beats praise every time. Always ask permission, and never use a full name unless someone actively wants you to. [The testimonials post](/blog/coaching-testimonials) goes into how to get the good ones.

## Decision 6: the limits

**Dan** doesn't mention what he does and doesn't do. He's worried it'll put people off.

**Kerry** has a short section on her website that says she's not a doctor or a therapist. It says that if you drink heavily every day, you need to speak to your GP before you stop, and she'll help you find support for that. It says she works alongside groups like AA, not against them, and that if someone needs more than coaching, she'll tell them.

That section doesn't put people off. It does the opposite. The people who'd be right for Kerry read it and think, she knows what she's doing. And the people who need more than coaching get pointed somewhere safe.

## Decision 7: what happens when someone slips

**Dan** doesn't really have a plan for it. When a client drinks again, they feel embarrassed, stop replying to his messages, and he never hears from them again. He tells himself they weren't ready.

**Kerry** expects it, because she knows it happens to nearly everyone. "I do fine the first few days, but I always end up slipping up" came up again and again in what people wrote. So on day one she tells every client: if you drink, message me within 24 hours, and we'll work out what happened. No telling off. A slip isn't the end of the programme, it's the best information you'll get all month, because it shows exactly which moment needs more work.

Her clients stay. And the ones who slipped and came back often write the best testimonials, because they're the stories the next nervous person needs to hear.

## Where Kerry found her first clients

Not from Instagram. From the people who'd watched her stop. Friends who'd noticed she'd stopped drinking at the school gate and asked how. A woman from her old book club who messaged her one evening. Her first five clients all came from people who already knew her story. [How to get your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into this step by step.

## FAQ

### Do I need to be sober myself to become a sobriety coach?

Not by law, but in practice, yes, it helps enormously. Clients want someone who knows what the first week feels like. If you're not sober yourself, you'll need a lot of training and experience to earn that same trust.

### Do I need a qualification to be a sobriety coach?

Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no legal requirement. But training will help you coach well and, just as importantly, spot when someone needs medical help or a therapist instead. Given the risks of stopping drinking suddenly, in my opinion it's worth doing.

### What's the difference between a sobriety coach and an AA sponsor?

An AA sponsor is a volunteer who's been through the twelve steps and helps someone else through them, for free. A sobriety coach is paid, works one-to-one or in small groups, and doesn't have to follow any one method. Plenty of people have both.
