A good wellness coach website tells a stranger, in plain words, who you help, what problem you help with and what will be different afterwards. In my opinion, a lot of wellness coach websites lean on the same soft words, like holistic, wellbeing, balance and thrive. Those words feel warm to you, but to someone who's tired, bloated or can't sleep, they don't say anything they can act on. So the quickest fix for your website is usually the words, not the design.

![A wellness coach typing on a laptop in a bright room full of plants, with a glass of herbal tea](wellness-coach-website.jpg)

I think I know why it happens. When you train, you pick up the words that go with the work, and then they come straight out when you write your homepage. You're describing it in your language instead of the language of the person you want to help.

## What the numbers say about wellness websites

When we checked 10,954 live coaching websites, 622 of them belonged to health, nutrition, wellness or fitness coaches. Each homepage was checked for 10 things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch, and not one of those 622 scored well enough to count as strong.

The biggest problem was being understood quickly. Across all coaching websites, only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds, and the health and wellness group did worse than that. There's good news too. They were better than coaching sites overall at showing a price and at offering something free for an email address. [The health coach websites post](/blog/health-coach-websites-scored) has the full breakdown.

Our research also matched self-help books to the people they're written for, and collected what those people write in their own words. We found 128 books on stress and burnout, 151 on health, weight and fitness, 50 on gut health and nutrition and 32 on sleep. Not one buyer quote in those four groups uses the word holistic, wellbeing or thrive (one person in the perimenopause research did, which I'll come to). They talk about being tired, bloated, stuck and fed up.

## Translate the wellness words

Here are seven words I see on wellness coach websites again and again. For each one, I've said what a visitor actually hears, and what you could say instead. The example lines are made up, so change them to fit you.

### Holistic

**What you mean:** you look at the whole person, so sleep, food, stress and work all come into it.

**What a visitor hears:** something vague, maybe a bit like crystals and incense. Holistic is on spa menus and supplement packets, and it says nothing about what happens in a session.

One person did use it, though. In the perimenopause research, someone wrote "I wanted to look into more holistic ways to handle my symptoms in the first instance". Even then, they meant something very specific by it: trying other things before medication.

**What to say instead:** say what the whole person bit looks like. "We look at your sleep, what you eat and how much you're carrying at work, because when you're exhausted it's rarely just one of those."

### Wellbeing

**What you mean:** how someone feels day to day, in body and mind.

**What a visitor hears:** a word from a staff survey. Nobody wakes up thinking about their wellbeing. They wake up shattered.

One person in the stress and burnout research wrote "I am chronically tired and have dropped my hobbies due to lack of energy". That's what poor wellbeing sounds like when a real person says it.

**What to say instead:** name the feeling. "If you're tired all the time and you've stopped doing the things you used to enjoy, this is for you."

### Balance

**What you mean:** a life where work, rest, family and health all get enough of your time.

**What a visitor hears:** something they've been told to find a hundred times, without anyone saying how. When we looked at how coaches in this area describe their work, we found titles like "Burnout to Balance: Reclaim Your Energy & Joy".

Some buyers do use the word. A burnt-out founder asked "How do I find purpose, balance, and myself again?". But look at what's going on in that sentence. They've lost something and they want it back, and the word balance on its own doesn't carry any of that.

**What to say instead:** say what's out of balance. "For people working 50-hour weeks who want their evenings back without quitting." That last bit is important, because someone in the burnout research asked "How do you handle burnout when quitting isn't an option". If your website only talks about rest, they'll assume you don't understand their life.

### Thrive

**What you mean:** enjoying life, rather than just getting through it.

**What a visitor thinks:** they've seen it on so many posters and Instagram posts that it doesn't mean anything any more. One menopause coach we looked at wrote "THRIVE in perimenopause rather than simply endure it". The idea is good, but someone who's exhausted is a long way from thriving, and being told to thrive can make them feel worse about how far off they are.

**What to say instead:** describe a normal good day, because that's what they want. "Waking up with enough energy to get through the day without three coffees."

### Mind-body

**What you mean:** stress shows up in the body, the body affects how you feel, and you work on both.

**What a visitor thinks:** it sounds like something they'd hear on a meditation app, and it doesn't describe anything they can see in their own life.

But they already know the link exists, because they're living it. In the sleep research one person wrote "I'm exhausted all the time but i CANT sleep. What do i do? Is this normal?". That's the mind and body pulling against each other, described by someone who'd never use the phrase.

**What to say instead:** describe the loop they're stuck in. "You're exhausted all day, then you lie awake at night with your head going. We work on both ends of that."

### Self-care

**What you mean:** the habits that keep someone well, like rest, food, movement and saying no.

**What a visitor thinks:** they picture bubble baths and face masks, because self-care has become a shopping category. Worse, people who are burnt out have usually tried it already. One wrote "I worked too hard for too long. I feel like no amount of rest will help".

**What to say instead:** say what they've tried and what you do differently. "You've tried early nights and days off, and you're still running on empty. We look at what's actually draining you and change that."

### Nourish

**What you mean:** eating in a way that properly feeds you, rather than dieting.

**What a visitor thinks:** it sounds like a recipe blog. It's a gentle word, but someone with a painful stomach wants to know what's wrong with it, not to be nourished.

Compare what people write about food and their bodies. One person in the gut health research wrote "my stomach is getting worse every day and I don't know where it's coming from". In the weight research, someone wrote "I can't seem to lose weight, despite eating less than I usually eat", and another asked "is there a plan to stop the cycle of yo-yo dieting?".

**What to say instead:** name the food problem in their words. "If you're fed up of diets that work for a month and then stop." And if someone's stomach is getting worse every day, that needs a doctor first, and your website should say so.

## Be careful what you promise

I'd get this bit right before anything else. Wellness coaching sits very close to health, and the person reading might have something medically wrong that nobody has spotted yet. A coach doesn't diagnose or treat anything. That's the job of doctors and other registered professionals.

So don't say you'll heal anyone's gut or cure their insomnia, and don't promise to fix their hormones either. Don't call yourself a dietitian unless you are one, because in the UK that title is protected by law. Coaching itself isn't regulated in the UK or the US, but what you say about health on your website still has to be true, and advertising rules apply to it. Rules on health claims and titles differ between countries, and in the US between states, so check the rules where you live.

A short, calm line does the job. For example (made up): "I'm a coach, not a doctor or dietitian. I don't diagnose or treat medical conditions. If your symptoms are new, getting worse or worrying you, please see your GP or doctor first." If your work touches on anxiety or low mood, [the mental health coach website post](/blog/mental-health-coach-website) covers where to draw the line and which crisis numbers to show.

## Rebuilding the top of a homepage

Here's the top of a homepage for a made-up wellness coach called Shona.

> **Holistic wellness coaching for mind, body and soul**
> I help women nourish themselves, find balance and thrive.
> [Book your free wellbeing session]

Nearly every key word in that is one of the seven above. A stranger wouldn't know who it's for or what actually happens in a session.

When Shona looked back at her clients, nearly all of them were women in their 40s who were tired all the time, even though they were doing the "right" things. That's what people in our energy and fatigue research describe. One wrote "I'm so chronically tired all the time, even though I eat healthy, exercise, and get 6-8 hours of sleep a night".

So here's the rebuilt top, again made up.

> **For women in their 40s who are tired all the time, even though they're eating well and sleeping enough**
> We'll look at your sleep, food, stress and everything you're carrying, and work out what's actually draining you. Then we change it, one step at a time, around work and family.
> I'm a coach, not a doctor, and I'll always tell you if something needs a GP or doctor first.
> [Book a free 20-minute chat] [Get my free guide: why you're tired when you're doing everything right]

Underneath, there's a photo of Shona, a few lines in the words her clients use, a short "how it works" section with her price, and two real testimonials used with permission.

The new one has no holistic, thrive or nourish, and it's much clearer. The whole person idea is still there in the second line. And the free guide, which wellness coaches are already better than average at offering, now matches the exact problem on the page.

To check your own, read the top out loud to someone who doesn't know what you do and ask who it's for. If they can't tell you, go back through the seven words above. And if you haven't picked who you help yet, [how to become a health coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-health-coach) covers that first.

## FAQ

### What should a wellness coach put on their website?

At the top, say who you help, what problem you help with and what will be different, in their own words. Underneath, add a photo of you, how it works, a price or starting price, real testimonials, a free first step and a line saying you're a coach and don't diagnose or treat.

### Is it wrong to use the word holistic on a wellness coach website?

It's not wrong, but on its own it tells a stranger nothing. If you use it, say straight after what it means in practice, like looking at sleep, food and stress together.

### Can a wellness coach give nutrition advice?

A coach can help people build habits and stick to healthy changes, but can't diagnose or treat medical conditions. In the UK, "dietitian" is a protected title, so don't use it unless you're registered. In the US, some states have laws about who can give people individual nutrition advice, not just who can be called a dietitian. Anyone with worrying symptoms should see a doctor first. Check the rules where you live.

### How do I know if my wellness website is clear enough?

Show the top of your homepage to someone who doesn't know you for five seconds, then ask who it's for and what you help with. If they say "wellness" or "feeling better", it's too vague. If they can name the person and the problem, you're on the right track.
