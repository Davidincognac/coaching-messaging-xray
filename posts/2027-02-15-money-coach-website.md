Somebody on a money coach's website is in an odd position. They've got a problem with money, and you're asking them to spend money to fix it. They're embarrassed, because money problems feel like a personal failure in a way other problems often don't. And there's a good chance the person they share a bank account with doesn't know they're looking.

So they don't ask you anything. They read your website, in silence, with a list of questions running through their head that they'd never say out loud. If the page answers them, they get in touch. If it doesn't, they close it and carry on worrying.

Here are the seven questions I think are going through their head, in roughly the order they'd think them, and what to put on your money coach website to answer each one. I'll use "money coach" here, but the same goes if you call yourself a financial coach.

## Question 1: "Is this for someone in my mess?"

Their mess is specific. It's not "financial wellbeing". When we looked at what people buy and write about money, it came out in plain, worried sentences. "Living pay check to pay check." "Feeling like you're constantly broke." "No matter how much I earn, I spend all of my salary in 2 weeks." "I'm 48 and have to start over... I've virtually no savings, and no retirement." "My husband's spending is out of control."

Those are five different people, and they need five different headlines. If your homepage says "Financial empowerment for a life of abundance", not one of them will see themselves in it.

**What to put on the page:** a headline that names one of those messes, in the words people use. For a made-up money coach who works with people in their 40s and 50s with no pension:

> **Late 40s, no real savings, and starting to panic about retirement?**
> It's not too late. I help people who are starting from scratch build a plan they can actually live on.

Pick your one group first. [The how to become a financial coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-financial-coach) shows how the groups break down in our research.

## Question 2: "Are you going to judge me?"

This is the big one, and it sits underneath everything else. People will tell a friend about their divorce before they'll tell them about their overdraft.

Look at the money advice they've already had. Cut the coffees. Make your own lunch. Stop being lazy with money. It's all a bit of a telling-off. So the second they spot a whiff of that on your website, they're gone.

**What to put on the page:** three things.

First, your own story, if you've got one. "Ten years ago I was hiding letters from the bank in a kitchen drawer" does more than any qualification, because it tells them you've been where they are.

Second, a plain line that says it out loud. "I won't judge you. Whatever's in your bank statements, I've seen worse, and probably done worse."

Third, a check of every word on your website. Anything that blames the reader, like "bad habits", "wasting money" or "irresponsible", gets taken out or reworded. Here's the difference one line makes:

> **Before:** Stop wasting money on things you don't need and take control of your finances.
>
> **After:** If your money disappears every month and you're not even sure where it goes, you're not bad with money. Nobody ever showed you a way that works for you.

Same point. One makes the reader feel told off, the other makes them feel understood.

## Question 3: "Are you going to sell me something?"

People who are struggling with money are used to being sold to. Loans, credit cards, "investment opportunities", courses promising passive income. So they're wary of anyone who sounds like they might have a product to push.

**What to put on the page:** say clearly that you don't sell financial products and don't earn commission from anyone. If that's true, it's one of the best things you can say. Put it near the top, not buried in the small print.

> I don't sell pensions, investments, loans or anything else. I don't earn commission from anyone. You pay me for my time, and that's it.

## Question 4: "Are you even allowed to do this?"

Some of your visitors will have a vague sense that money advice is regulated, and they're right. In the UK, advice on specific investments, pensions, mortgages and debt solutions is regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority. In the US, giving personal investment advice for money generally means registering as an investment adviser. A money coach who doesn't have that authorisation can't recommend specific products.

You'd think saying that would put people off. It does the opposite. It shows you know what you're doing.

**What to put on the page:** a short, plain section.

> **What I do, and what I don't**
>
> I help you understand where your money goes, build a budget you'll stick to, and make decisions you feel good about. I'm not a regulated financial adviser, so I won't tell you which pension or investment to choose. When you need that, I'll help you find a good one. If you're in serious debt, I'll point you to free, regulated debt advice first.

I'm not a lawyer, so check the exact rules where you live before you write yours.

## Question 5: "Can I afford this, when money's the problem?"

Here's the awkward one. You're asking someone who's short of money to pay you. They know it, you know it, and pretending it's not an issue won't make it go away.

In our research, 82.3% of coaching websites didn't show a price. For a money coach, hiding the price is worse than usual, because the visitor is already anxious about money and now has to guess at another bill. And somebody who's good with money, which you're claiming to be, should be able to say what something costs.

**What to put on the page:** the price, and an honest word about it.

> **What it costs**
>
> Three months, six sessions, £X. You can pay monthly.
>
> I know it's odd paying money to sort out your money. Plenty of my clients find enough in the first month, from things they didn't know they were paying for, to cover a good chunk of it. If it's really out of reach right now, start with the free guide below.

Only say the second part if it's true for your clients. If it is, it's a powerful thing to say. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers how to handle the price either way.

## Question 6: "Will anyone find out?"

Money is private, and plenty of your visitors share a bank account, a laptop or an email inbox with someone. Some of them are looking for help precisely because of what that someone is doing with the money.

**What to put on the page:** a line that tells them you've thought about it, and then actually doing it.

- Use a plain name that shows up on bank statements, not "Debt Rescue Coaching Ltd".
- Don't sign people up to a newsletter just because they downloaded something. Ask.
- Keep your email subject lines plain.

Then say so: "Everything's private. Nothing I send you will say what it's about, and what shows on your bank statement is just my name."

## Question 7: "What happens if I get in touch?"

They've got this far. They're nearly there. And now the last worry: if I click this button, am I going to be stuck on a sales call, or asked to send my bank statements to a stranger?

**What to put on the page:** say exactly what happens, step by step.

> **What happens next**
>
> 1. You book a free 20-minute call at a time that suits you.
> 2. We talk about where you are. You don't need to bring anything or show me anything.
> 3. If it sounds right for you, I'll explain how it works. If it doesn't, I'll tell you, and point you somewhere better if I can.

And give a gentle option for people who aren't ready to talk at all. A free spending tracker, a one-page guide to the first week of sorting your money out. Something they can use on their own at the kitchen table, without anyone seeing.

## Put together, in order

So the page answers the seven questions, roughly in the order they're asked:

1. A headline that names their mess, in their words.
2. Your story and a line saying you won't judge.
3. A line saying you don't sell products or earn commission.
4. A short "what I do, and what I don't" section.
5. The price, and an honest word about paying money to sort out money.
6. A line about privacy.
7. What happens when they get in touch, plus a free option for people who aren't ready.

Add two or three short stories from clients, with first names only, wherever they fit. "I'd been ignoring my bank app for two years. Now I check it every Sunday and it doesn't make me feel sick." Two thirds of coaching websites show no proof at all, and on a money coach's website a story like that answers question 1 and question 2 at the same time.

## FAQ

### What should a money coach put on their homepage?

A headline naming the specific money problem you help with, in your clients' words, then your story, a line saying you won't judge, what you do and don't do, the price, a privacy line and exactly what happens if they get in touch.

### Should a money coach show their prices?

In my opinion, yes. Someone with money worries is already anxious about costs, and hiding the price makes it worse. Show it clearly, offer to spread payments if you can, and be honest that paying to sort out money can feel odd.

### Should a money coach have a free guide on their website?

Yes. Plenty of people looking at a money coach aren't ready to talk to anyone about their money yet. A simple free tool, like a spending tracker or a short guide to the first week, lets them start on their own and get to know you without any risk.

### Can a money coach give financial advice?

Not regulated advice, unless they're authorised to. In the UK and US, recommending specific investments, pensions or debt solutions is regulated. A money coach helps with budgeting, habits and decisions, and says clearly on their website where that line is.
