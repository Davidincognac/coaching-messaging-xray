If you were hoping for links to real coaches with lovely websites, I'm not going to give you that. Instead I've written six made-up examples of the top of a coach's homepage, the bit a visitor sees before they scroll, and what each one gets right.

![A coach at her desk comparing two coaching websites on a laptop and a tablet](coaching-website-examples-laptops.jpg)

The short answer to "what does a good coaching website look like?" is this. In the first few lines it says who it's for, what problem they've got in words they'd use themselves, and what happens next.

## Why these examples are made up

At Going Beyond The Illusion we never link to or name other coaches' websites. They didn't ask to be picked apart in front of strangers, so every example below is made up. The coaches, their names, their testimonials and their homepages don't exist.

We checked 10,954 real coaching websites, and only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds. The examples follow what the better sites did, like showing proof and saying clearly who they're for.

We also looked at the books people buy about their problems and what they write. Each headline is built from words real buyers used, and I'll show you the original quote each time, copied exactly.

## Example 1: a career change coach

Megan is a made-up coach who helps people who've been in the same job for years and want out.

> **Stuck in a career rut, and getting more miserable by the day?**
>
> I help people who fell into a job they never really chose work out what they'd be good at next, then make the move without betting the house on it. No more quizzes or personality tests.
>
> Twelve weeks, one call a fortnight, from £600. You'll leave with two or three real options you've tested by talking to people who do those jobs.
>
> *"I'd done every quiz going. Megan got me to actually ring people. Now I'm retraining as a surveyor." – Hannah, 38 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free 20-minute chat]**

Megan's headline comes almost word for word from a buyer who wrote "I'm stuck in a career rut and I'm becoming more miserable by the day". In our research, 159 of the books people bought were about career and purpose. Another person wrote "I've basically sat still in the same position because I'm too scared to jump", and that fear of jumping and getting it wrong is what Megan's second line answers. The line about quizzes comes from one buyer's list of what they'd already tried: "making more lists, reading more books, taking more psychometric tests".

If your reader has already tried something that didn't work, say you won't make them do it again.

## Example 2: a menopause health coach

Bev is a made-up coach who works with women in perimenopause on sleep, food, exercise and energy.

> **Exhausted, foggy, and fed up of nobody listening?**
>
> I'm a health coach for women in perimenopause who feel like they've lost themselves. I'm not a doctor and I don't replace yours. I help you build daily habits that sit alongside whatever your GP or doctor is doing.
>
> Eight weeks, one call a week, plus a short check-in by message.
>
> *"For the first time in two years I didn't feel fobbed off." – Lorraine, 51 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free chat]** or **[get my list of questions to take to your doctor]**

Start with the safety bit. Bev says she's not a doctor in her second line. A coach can't diagnose or treat a medical condition, and the site should say so. The rules differ from place to place. Some US states limit who can give personal nutrition advice, and in the UK you can't call yourself a dietitian unless you're registered. So check the rules where you live.

The headline is built from two real questions. One person asked "How do I tackle perimenopause when all I feel is exhausted?" and another asked "What do you do when your doctors do not listen to your concerns?" There were 40 books on menopause and women's hormones in our research, and the fear of not being taken seriously by doctors came through. The free list of questions for the doctor helps with exactly that.

The thing I'd take from Bev is saying what you're not, early. People trust you more when you're upfront about where your help stops.

## Example 3: a coach for new managers

Carl is a made-up coach who works with people in their first year of managing a team.

> **Just been made a manager, and your old teammates won't listen to you?**
>
> I coach first-time managers who got promoted for being good at the job, then got handed a team with no training.
>
> Six one-to-one sessions over three months. If your company is paying, I'll give you a one-page summary to forward to your boss.
>
> *"I was dreading every Monday. Now I actually like the people side of it." – Ollie, 29 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free call]**

I'd start with the testimonial. Someone in our research asked "How do I learn to embrace & enjoy the people side of my role?", and Ollie's line answers it.

The headline comes from a buyer who wrote "my teammates don't respect me and don't listen to me", and another wrote "I am stuck between the manager and team members". There were 136 books on leadership in our research. Carl says exactly who he works with, people managing a team for the first time, so a senior director can see straight away it isn't for them.

Think about who pays, too. If it's the company, give the manager something easy to forward to their boss.

## Example 4: a divorce coach

Nadia is a made-up coach who helps people rebuild their life in the year after a divorce.

> **After the divorce, does life just feel blank?**
>
> If the house is quiet, the plans you made are gone, and you don't know where to start, that's what I help with. I've been through it myself.
>
> Ten weeks, one call a week. We'll start with one part of your life, not all of it at once, and work out what you want it to look like now.
>
> *"I thought I'd feel free. I just felt lost. Nadia helped me make a start." – Pauline, 56 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free chat]** or **[get the free guide to your first months on your own]**

Someone in our research asked "What do I do after divorce life becomes blank? I don't understand from where to start". We found 81 books on relationships and divorce, and one person put the loneliness plainly: "I initiated my divorce and now I feel so lonely and lost. I went from always having somebody home to nobody". Nadia's line about the house being quiet is aimed straight at that. The free guide gives people a slow way in if they're not ready to talk to a stranger yet.

Starting with one part of your life is far easier to say yes to than changing everything, so make your first step small too.

## Example 5: a sleep coach

Leon is a made-up coach who helps people with their sleep habits and tired mornings.

> **Exhausted all day, but you still can't sleep at night?**
>
> I help adults who are tired all the time change the habits around their sleep, including what happens at 3am when their head won't switch off.
>
> Six weeks, a call every week, with a simple sleep diary in between. If you're worried something is medically wrong, please see your doctor first.
>
> *"I didn't realise how much my evenings were keeping me awake. I'm waking up feeling human again." – Neil, 47 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free call]**

Leon's headline is lifted from a buyer who wrote "I'm exhausted all the time but i CANT sleep. What do i do? Is this normal?" Sleep was a smaller subject, with 32 books, and people were scared something was medically wrong. Leon tells them to see a doctor first, which is what a coach should do.

If your reader is worried about something, say it in the first few lines, before they have to ask.

## Example 6: a coach for small business owners

Ruth is a made-up coach who works with people running a business on their own.

> **Wearing all the hats, and wondering if you're cut out for this?**
>
> I coach people who run a business on their own and are close to burning out. Feeling overwhelmed usually means you're doing everything yourself, not that you're in the wrong job.
>
> Three months, a call every fortnight, from £450. We'll work out what to stop, what to hand over and what to charge more for.
>
> *"I nearly packed it in last December. This year I took a proper break and the business was fine." – Femi, 42 (made-up testimonial)*
>
> **[Book a free call]**

There were 126 books in our research for people running a business on their own. The biggest worry came through in one person's question: "Is it common to feel overwhelmed as solopreneur starting their first business or does it mean I'm not cut out to be an entrepreneur?" Ruth's headline asks it, and her second line answers it.

"Wearing all the hats" is a phrase people in that group used themselves, so the headline sounds like them rather than like a coach. The testimonial mentions December on purpose too, because someone asked "anyone else is feeling exhausted by December?"

## What the six have in common

All six are built the same way. The headline describes a problem in the reader's own words, and the line under it says who it's for. Each says what happens, and how long and how often, so the visitor can picture what they're signing up for. The testimonial names a real moment rather than just saying "highly recommend", and there's a clear next step at the end.

Only two show a price at the top, and the others would show it further down the page. For the full list of what goes on the page, [what to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage) goes through it in order.

## How to use examples without copying them

The worst thing you could do with this post is take Megan's headline, swap "career" for "confidence", and put it on your website. It won't work, because it isn't in your clients' words.

So use examples to learn the shape, then fill it with your own people. Write down the exact phrases your clients and enquiries have used about their problem in emails, calls and messages. Pick the one that keeps coming up and build your headline from it, the same way I did with each example.

The testimonials here are made up to show the shape. On your own website they must be real, from real clients, with their permission. Inventing them is against advertising rules in the UK and the US.

Your offer needs the same treatment. The good ones say how long, how often and what happens. If you can't say that about your coaching in two lines, the website isn't the problem, and [the post on building a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) will help more than any example.

Then show the top of your homepage to a stranger for five seconds and ask who it's for. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how.

## FAQ

### Where can I find real coaching website examples?

Look at coaches in your area, then put each site through the five-second test. Only around 1 in 7 coaching websites pass it, so there's a good chance the one you like is making mistakes you'd be copying too.

### Can I use my clients' words in my headline?

Yes, as long as it's the general way they describe their problem, not something private they told you. If you want to use a client's story or name as a testimonial, always ask their permission first.

### Should a coaching website show prices?

In my opinion, yes, or at least a starting price. People want to know what they're getting into before they book a call, and a price helps the right people say yes. If you're not sure what to charge, [the post on coaching pricing](/blog/coaching-pricing) goes through it.
