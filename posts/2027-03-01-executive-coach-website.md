Here's something a lot of executive coaches don't think about. Your website usually has two completely different readers, and they want different things.

One is the leader who'd actually be coached. The other is the person who'd sign it off and pay for it, often someone in HR or learning and development, sometimes a CEO or a board. If your website only talks to one of them, the other one crosses you off the list without telling you.

So rather than give you a checklist, I'm going to follow both of them through one ordinary Tuesday. Both people and the company are made up, but you'll recognise the day. Watch what each one looks for, and when they give up.

![An executive coach and a senior leader talking in a glass-walled meeting room](executive-coach-meeting-room.jpg)

## 7:40am, in the back of a taxi

**Priya** is 44 and was made Chief Operating Officer four months ago. She's good at the job, but she's not sleeping. She's gone from running one part of the business to being responsible for all of it, and she hasn't told anyone that some days she feels like a fraud in the board meeting.

Her CEO mentioned executive coaching last week. In the taxi, she searches "executive coach for new COO" and opens three websites on her phone.

The first opens with "Unlock Leadership Excellence". She has no idea what that means and she hasn't got time to find out. Gone.

The second has a long list of accreditations, a photo of the coach on a stage, and a page called "Our Methodology" with a diagram of five overlapping circles. She scrolls, can't find anything about people like her, and closes it.

The third opens with:

> **New in a senior role, and finding the step up harder than you expected?**
> I coach leaders in their first year at C-suite level. What you're feeling is normal, and it doesn't have to take you a year to find your feet.

She keeps reading.

### What Priya needed to see

**Her level, named.** Leaders want to know you understand the level they're at. When we looked at what people buy and write about leadership and founder coaching, the worries were very specific to the stage people were at. Newly promoted leaders wanted to "grow into the bigger role fast without being exposed". Founders wrote about "a profound sense of alone-ness" and being "constantly overwhelmed". A first-time manager wrote that "delegation feels like jumping out of an airplane and hoping the team is the parachute". Those are different jobs. Pick your level and say it.

**Something human, quickly.** Priya isn't going to read your methodology in a taxi. She's looking for one sign that you get what she's feeling, and that you won't think less of her for feeling it.

**A website that works on a phone.** Senior people read websites in the spare minutes of their day. Taxis, between meetings, last thing at night. If your website only makes sense on a big screen, a lot of them will never see it properly.

## 12:15pm, at her desk, ten minutes before the next meeting

Priya goes back to the third website. Now she's checking whether this coach is the real thing.

She looks for three things:

**Have you done this before, at my level?** Not just "I've coached 500 leaders", but leaders like her. She finds a line saying the coach was a COO herself for six years before training as a coach. That's the thing that does it. When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That's the sense that you've seen their problem before. For an executive, having sat in a similar seat is the strongest version of that.

**Will this stay private?** She's a senior leader. She doesn't want her team, her board or her CEO hearing what she says in a coaching session. She finds a short section on confidentiality that explains exactly what does and doesn't get shared with whoever's paying. That's a relief.

**What do other leaders say?** She finds two testimonials. One is from a "Finance Director, FTSE 250 company", the other from a "Founder and CEO, healthcare technology". No full names, which she understands, because she wouldn't want her name on there either. But they're specific about what changed, so she believes them.

She sends the link to Mark, who runs learning and development, with one line: "This one looks right. Can we do this?"

## 2:30pm, a different desk, with a different job

**Mark** is Head of Learning and Development. He's got Priya's email, and two other coaches the CEO suggested. He's got to recommend one, and he'll need to justify the cost to the finance director.

Mark reads the same website completely differently. He's not looking for empathy. He's looking for reasons it's a safe decision.

**How does it actually work?** He wants to see the structure. How many sessions, over how long, how they're run, and whether there's a meeting at the start with Priya and her manager to agree what they're working on. The website has a section called "How an engagement works" that lays it all out in five short steps. He likes that, because he can copy it straight into his recommendation.

**What does it cost, roughly?** He doesn't need the exact price on the website, but he needs to know whether it's in the right range before he wastes anyone's time. The site says "Six-month programmes for senior leaders typically start from £X". Good enough.

In our research, 82.3% of coaching websites didn't show a price at all. For executive coaching that's often deliberate, because every engagement is different. But giving the person paying a rough starting point saves everyone a phone call, and it means you're not ruled out just because Mark couldn't tell whether you'd fit his budget. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers how to handle showing a price or not.

**Is there proof it works?** Mark needs something to show the finance director. The site has a short case study: a newly appointed operations director, what they agreed to work on, and what changed after six months, including fewer issues being escalated to the CEO. No company name. That's exactly the kind of thing Mark can point to.

**Is the coach properly qualified?** Mark checks whether the coach has a recognised coaching qualification and works to a professional code of ethics. It's there, at the bottom of the About page, in one line. That's where it belongs. Priya didn't care about it. Mark needed it.

**What happens next?** The website says "If you're arranging coaching for someone in your organisation, book a 30-minute call and I'll send you a short proposal you can share." Mark books it.

## 4:00pm, the other two coaches

Mark gives the other two coaches a proper look. One has a website that's all about the coach: a long biography, a list of every company they've worked with, and no idea of how an engagement works or roughly what it costs. Mark can't recommend what he can't explain, so he moves on.

The other is fine, but it's written entirely for HR. "Leadership capability frameworks", "measurable ROI", "talent pipeline". Mark understands it, but he can tell Priya would never have got past the first screen. And Priya's the one who has to want to do it.

## 5:30pm, back with Priya

Mark emails Priya before she leaves. He's booked a call with the coach, and if it goes well, he'll set up a short meeting between the two of them first, to see if they get on.

Priya had already seen that on the website. A line under "How an engagement works" said: "Before anything's agreed, we'll have a 30-minute conversation, just you and me, so you can decide whether I'm the right person. If I'm not, I'll tell you, and I'll suggest someone who might be."

That line did two jobs. It told Priya she'd get to choose, which means a lot to someone who's used to being in control. And it told Mark the coach wasn't going to push, which made his recommendation easier. For a senior leader, the relationship with the coach is everything, and good executive coaching websites say so.

## What the third website got right

Read back through the day and the pattern is simple. The website that won spoke to both people, in the order they arrived.

**For the leader, near the top:** their level and their feeling, named plainly. A short line of the coach's own story at that level. A clear statement on confidentiality. Specific testimonials from leaders at a similar level.

**For the person paying, further down or on its own page:** how an engagement works, step by step. A rough starting price. A short case study with outcomes. Qualifications and ethics in one line. A next step built for them.

If you also coach business owners, [the business coach website post](/blog/business-coach-website) covers the checks an owner runs, which are different again.

Nothing on that list needs a big design budget. A lot of executive coaches spend thousands making their website look impressive, then leave out the two or three sentences that would actually win the work.

## While we're on it: your LinkedIn headline

Priya didn't find her coach on LinkedIn, but plenty of leaders do, and Mark will almost certainly look you up there before the call. We scored 11,377 coaches' LinkedIn headlines out of 10, and the average was 2.56. 420 of them had written "Executive Coach" and nothing else.

That's the category, not a reason to pick you. "I coach new C-suite leaders through their first year" tells both Priya and Mark something. [The social media post](/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coaches) goes into the headline scores in more detail.

## FAQ

### What should an executive coach put on their website?

For the leader: who you coach and at what level, in plain words, a line of your own experience, how you handle confidentiality and specific testimonials. For the person paying: how an engagement works, a rough starting price, a short case study, your qualifications and a next step built for them.

### Should an executive coach show prices on their website?

You don't need an exact price, because engagements vary. But a starting point, like "programmes typically start from £X", helps the person paying decide whether you're worth a call, and stops you being ruled out on budget before you've spoken.

### Do executive coaching testimonials need full names?

No. Senior leaders often don't want to be named. A job title and type of company, like "Finance Director, retail", is believable as long as the testimonial says specifically what changed. Always get permission first.
