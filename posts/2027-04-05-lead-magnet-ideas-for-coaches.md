A lead magnet is the free thing a visitor gets in return for their email address, like a checklist, a short guide, a quiz or a short video. The best lead magnet ideas for coaches are small, quick things that help with one problem the reader has today, and they're picked to suit how close that person is to hiring a coach. Someone who doesn't yet know what's wrong needs something different from someone who's already comparing coaches.

![A woman at her kitchen table filling in a printed worksheet while reading a short guide](lead-magnet-ideas-worksheet.jpg)

I've split the ideas into three groups depending on where your reader is, with a made-up example for each one and a note on when it works and when it doesn't.

## Why a free first step is worth having

When we looked at the 918 kinds of people who hire a coach, around 4 in 10 weren't looking for a coach yet. They either didn't know what was wrong, or knew the problem but hadn't started looking for help. Those people aren't going to book a call with a stranger. A "book a free consultation" button means nothing to them, because they don't think they need a consultation.

But they're still on your website, reading. If the only thing you offer is a call, they leave and you never hear from them again. A free first step gives them something safe to do, and gives you a way to stay in touch.

## A freebie isn't a bribe

There's an idea floating around that if you give people something for free, they'll feel they owe you and buy. People call it reciprocity. I'll be blunt, our research doesn't back it up for coaching. Reciprocity helped the buying decision in only about 3 in every 100 types of buyer we looked at.

So nobody is going to hire you just because you gave them a PDF. What happens is the PDF actually helps, and that makes them think "if the free bit is this useful, the paid bit must be good". If it's a thin advert for your coaching, people can tell straight away.

Every example from here on is made up. The coaches don't exist, but the problems come from our research into the books people buy about their problems and what those buyers wrote about their own situation.

## Ideas for people who don't yet know what's wrong

These people feel something is off but couldn't name it. They aren't searching for a coach. They're typing what they feel into Google in their own words, so the freebie has to help them put a name to it.

### A short self-check

This is ten plain questions that help someone work out what's going on. Made-up example: a burnout coach offers "Is it tiredness or is it burnout? A 10-question check you can do on your lunch break."

It works well when your reader keeps asking "is this normal?" and you can answer that kindly. Where it goes wrong is when it pretends to diagnose anything. A coach can't diagnose, so the check should point people to their doctor when the answers suggest something medical, and say so clearly.

### A "why this keeps happening" one-pager

This is one page that explains the pattern behind the problem in everyday words. Made-up example: a sleep coach offers "Why you wake up tired even after eight hours: a one-page explainer." In our research there were 32 books on sleep, and one person who bought books on it wrote "Doesn't matter how much I sleep, I'm still tired in the morning". That's the person this one-pager is for.

I'd use this if your reader blames themselves and needs to hear it's common. Bad if your explanation is just your opinion dressed up as fact. Keep it to what you know and say so when you're not sure.

### A short story, told by email

This is three short emails over a week, each one a real moment from someone who had the same problem (with their permission) or from your own life. Made-up example: a coach for new dads offers "Three emails from dads who didn't feel the bond straight away either."

This one suits a topic people feel ashamed of and won't talk about. I'd skip it if you haven't got the stories, because made-up ones will be spotted, and you mustn't pass off invented people as real.

There's more on giving something useful away first in [how to become a nutrition coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-nutrition-coach) and it applies to any coach.

## Ideas for people who know the problem

These people can name it. They've probably tried something that didn't work. They want to make progress on their own first, so give them the first proper step.

### A checklist for one specific moment

Don't try to cover the whole problem. Pick one moment in it, like the first week, and make the checklist for that. Made-up example: a debt coach offers "The first seven days: what to do the week you decide to sort your debt out."

This works when there's a clear "first thing" people get wrong. Bad if the checklist has 40 items, because nobody does 40 things, but they might actually try seven.

### A template they can fill in tonight

This is something they write on, rather than just read. Made-up example: a coach for carers offers "The one-page week planner for carers: what you have to do, what someone else could do, and one hour that's just for you, with a filled-in example."

It's a good pick if your coaching involves the client doing some thinking on paper. Where people go wrong is a template that only makes sense once you've explained it in a session. If it needs you there, it isn't a lead magnet yet.

### A short plan for the next step only

Made-up example: an empty nest coach offers "The first month after they leave: four small things to do for yourself each week." A parent in our research who bought books on the empty nest wrote "I want him to live his life. How do I begin to live one of my own?", and this answers the "how do I begin" part.

This suits a reader who's stuck at the start. Bad if you promise a result you can't be sure of, like "feel happy again in 30 days".

### A recorded short lesson

This is ten minutes of video or audio on one idea. Made-up example: a public speaking coach offers "How to stop your voice shaking in the first minute: a 10-minute audio lesson."

I like this one if your reader learns better by watching or listening, and if your voice or face helps them trust you. The trap is letting it turn into a 45-minute "masterclass" that's really a sales pitch with a bit of teaching at the front.

## Ideas for people comparing options

These people already know they want help. They're deciding between you, another coach, a course, an app, or doing it alone. The freebie here should help them choose well, even if that means choosing someone else.

### A "questions to ask any coach" list

Made-up example: a retirement coach offers "Eight questions to ask before you hire a retirement coach, including me."

Only do this if you're confident in your answers to the questions. If the list is rigged so only you pass, people notice that, and it looks like a trick.

### A plain comparison

This is one page showing the options side by side and who each one suits. Made-up example: a money coach offers "Coach, adviser, app or going it alone: which one fits where you are with money?"

It works if the options really do suit different people. Bad if you knock everyone else. Also be careful on money. In the UK and US, regulated financial advice is a separate job with its own rules, so a money coach shouldn't give personal investment advice and should say so. Check the rules where you live.

### A walk-through of your first session

Made-up example: a confidence coach offers "What happens in session one: the exact questions I ask and why."

This is useful when people are nervous about what coaching actually involves. It won't help if you hide the price everywhere else. People comparing coaches want to know what it costs.

## What not to make

### The 50-page ebook

They look impressive, but nobody reads 50 pages from a stranger. They download it, mean to read it, and it sits in their downloads folder forever. Cut it down to the one chapter that solves one problem, and give them that.

### "5 tips to a better you"

Generic tips are the opposite of what a stranger needs. "Drink more water, get more sleep, set goals." They've heard it all before. If you could swap your freebie onto another coach's website and nobody would notice, it's too generic.

### Anything that's really a sales call

A "free strategy session" is a call, and the people who aren't ready won't take it. Keep calls for the people comparing options, and give everyone else something they can use without talking to you.

### Anything that won't open on a phone

A big PDF with tiny writing loses everyone reading on the bus. Check it on your own phone.

## What happens after they sign up

Someone has just handed you their email address. They've trusted you with it, so don't start selling to them the minute they sign up.

Send the freebie straight away, in an email that says what it is and how to use it. Then send one more useful email a few days later, with another tip that builds on the freebie or a short story about someone it helped. Say who you are in a sentence or two and how they can reach you if they want to, and leave it there for now.

What you shouldn't do is put them into a seven-email sequence where every email ends with "book your call now, only three spots left". A reader who's only just learned your name will simply unsubscribe.

Then keep emailing them now and then with something useful, and when they're ready to get help, they'll know who you are.

One more thing on the rules. In the UK you generally need people's clear agreement before you send them marketing emails, so tell them on the sign-up form that they'll get emails from you, not just the freebie. In the US, every marketing email needs a clear way to unsubscribe and a real postal address for you. I'm not a lawyer, so check the rules where you live.

## Where to put it on your website

Put the freebie where the people who aren't ready to book will see it. That usually means near your main "book a call" button, as a softer second option, and at the end of your blog posts. [These coaching website examples](/blog/coaching-website-examples) include a couple of made-up homepages with a freebie next to the booking button, and for what else belongs on the page, see [what to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage).

Put a short testimonial next to the sign-up form too, ideally from someone who used the freebie or who started that way before becoming a client. [How to get coaching testimonials people believe](/blog/coaching-testimonials) has the exact message to send to get one.

## FAQ

### What is the best lead magnet for a coach?

The one that helps your reader with one problem they've got right now and can be used in under 15 minutes. For people who don't know what's wrong yet, that's often a short self-check. For people comparing coaches, it's often a list of questions to ask any coach.

### Do I need a lead magnet if I'm just starting out?

Yes, if people are finding you but not booking. A one-page checklist written in an afternoon is fine to start. You can make it better once you see what people sign up for.

### How long should a coaching lead magnet be?

Short enough to finish in one sitting. One page, a 10-minute recording or a 10-question check is plenty. If it takes longer than a cup of tea, it's probably too long.

### Should I charge for it instead?

No. Its job is to help people who aren't ready to pay yet and to show them you know what you're talking about. If it's good enough to charge for, that's a good sign, but keep it free.
