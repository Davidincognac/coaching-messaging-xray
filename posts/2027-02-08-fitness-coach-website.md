Here's how people find a fitness coach. They're on Instagram on the bus, or lying on the sofa after a day of telling themselves they'll start on Monday. They see a post that makes sense to them, tap your name, tap the link in your bio, and your website opens. On their phone. With their thumb.

They're not sitting at a desk with a cup of tea, reading your website the way you built it on your laptop. They're going to give it a few seconds, a couple of flicks of the thumb, and then decide.

So in this post I'm going to look at a fitness coach website the way it's actually seen. One phone screen at a time, from the top. If you've built yours on a laptop and never really looked at it on a phone, this is the post for you.

## Before the first screen: where they came from

Think about what they were doing one second before your website opened. They were looking at your post. Maybe it was about being stuck on week 5 of a running plan, or about women over 45 gaining weight while doing the same workouts as ever. Whatever it was, it's the reason they tapped.

So the first screen of your website needs to carry on from that. If your Instagram talks to new mums and your website opens with "Elite Performance Coaching", they'll think they've got the wrong page and leave. The website and the posts that send people to it need to sound like the same person talking to the same people.

If you're not sure who those people are yet, [the how to become a fitness coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-fitness-coach) goes through the five moments people go looking for help. Pick one, and make your website about it.

## Screen 1: the only screen everybody sees

Everybody sees the first screen. Plenty of people never see anything else. On a phone, that's roughly a headline, a line or two underneath, maybe a picture and maybe a button. That's it.

Here's what we usually see on that first screen of a fitness coach's website: a big photo of the coach mid-workout, with a headline like "Transform Your Body. Transform Your Life." laid over the top in white letters. And on a phone, half the headline's hidden behind someone's arm.

Here's what I'd put there instead, for a made-up coach who works with men over 50:

> **Over 50, out of shape, and fed up of feeling it every morning?**
> I help men get strong again with three short sessions a week, without a gym full of 25-year-olds.
>
> **[See how it works]**

It's an example to show you the shape. Notice what it does in about 30 words: who it's for, what's wrong, what changes, and what to do next. And the headline is plain text on a plain background, so it's readable on any phone, in any light, on a bus.

We looked at 10,954 coaching websites, and only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. When we looked at the 622 health, nutrition, wellness and fitness sites on their own, they were harder to understand in five seconds than coaching sites overall. On a phone, five seconds is generous. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check yours.

## The photo problem

That big photo deserves its own section, because it causes three problems on a phone.

**It pushes everything down.** A photo that looks lovely across a laptop screen can fill an entire phone screen. So the first thing a visitor sees is a picture, not a reason to stay.

**It's often the wrong photo.** A coach with a six-pack doing a pull-up is impressive, but to a 52-year-old who gets out of breath on the stairs, it says "this isn't for you". A photo of you working with someone who looks like your client says far more.

**It slows the page down.** Big photos straight off a camera or phone can be several megabytes each. On a phone signal in a car park, the page takes ages to load, and people don't wait. Shrink your photos before you upload them. A photo for a website rarely needs to be more than about 1,200 pixels wide.

## Screen 2: what actually happens

If the first screen made sense, they'll flick down once. The second screen has one job, which is to answer "what would I actually do with you?"

Fitness coaches are often vague about this. "Personalised training and nutrition coaching" could mean anything. On a phone, nobody's going to open three pages to find out what it means. So say it in a few short lines:

> **How it works**
>
> 1. We have a 30-minute call to see where you are and what hurts.
> 2. You get a plan you can do at home or in any gym: three sessions a week, 40 minutes each.
> 3. We check in every Sunday by message, and change the plan when life gets in the way.

Short numbered steps work brilliantly on a phone. They're easy to read with your thumb hovering, and they make the whole thing feel manageable. Which, for someone who's tried and failed before, is the point.

## Screen 3: proof

Third screen, and now they're thinking: has this actually worked for someone like me?

Two thirds of the coaching websites we looked at showed no proof at all. Plenty of fitness coaches lean on before-and-after photos to fill that hole. But on a phone, a grid of tiny before-and-after photos is hard to see, and people have seen thousands of them anyway.

What reads well on a phone is a short quote, in big enough text, with a first name and an age:

> "I'm 57. I can play football with my grandson again without paying for it for three days."
> *Steve, 57*

One good quote like that, sitting on its own, does more than a carousel of twelve. If you've got more, put them on a separate page and link to it.

## Screen 4: the price

When we looked at the 622 health and fitness coaching websites, they were better than coaching sites overall at showing a price. That's good news, and it's something to keep.

On a phone, keep it simple. One line is enough: "12 weeks, from £X a month." If you've got three packages, don't squeeze three columns side by side. On a phone they'll either be too small to read or stacked so far down that nobody gets to the third one. Lead with your main package, and put the rest on a pricing page.

If you're unsure whether to show your price at all, [the coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers how to make either choice work.

## Screen 5: the next step

Here's where fitness websites on phones fall apart. The visitor's ready, and then they hit one of these:

- A booking calendar that's too wide for the phone screen, so they have to pinch and zoom to tap a time
- A contact form with ten boxes, where the keyboard covers half of them
- A "Download my free guide" button that opens a PDF they can't read on a phone
- A button that says "Submit", which tells them nothing about what happens next

Make the next step easy to do with one thumb. A button that says exactly what happens, like "Book a free 30-minute call" or "Get the free 4-week home plan". A form with no more than two or three boxes. And a line underneath saying what happens next: "I'll message you within a day to find a time."

Health and fitness websites were also better than average at offering visitors something in return for their email address, like a free plan or guide. That's worth doing, because plenty of people who find you on the bus aren't ready to book a call. Just make sure it opens properly on a phone.

## The three things that annoy people on a phone

A few things are fine on a laptop and maddening on a phone. Go through your own site and check for these:

**Pop-ups.** A pop-up that covers the whole screen two seconds after the page opens, with a tiny cross in the corner you can't hit with your thumb. People don't hunt for the cross. They leave.

**Tiny text.** Text that looks fine on a laptop can be unreadable on a phone. If you have to zoom in to read it, so does everyone else.

**Videos that play by themselves.** Especially with sound. Somebody browsing on the bus doesn't want your intro video blaring out.

## How to check your own fitness coach website

This takes ten minutes and costs nothing.

1. Open your website on your own phone, not your laptop. Better still, borrow someone else's phone, because yours might have the site saved already.
2. Turn off your wifi, so you see how fast it loads on a normal signal.
3. Look at the first screen without scrolling. Could a stranger tell who it's for and what you do?
4. Flick down. Can you find what happens, proof, the price and the next step within a few flicks?
5. Try to book or sign up using only one thumb.
6. Then hand the phone to a friend who isn't into fitness, and ask them what you do. If they can't say it back to you, your website can't say it to strangers either.

Fix whatever annoys you. If it annoys you on your own website, it's annoying everybody else too.

## FAQ

### What should be at the top of a fitness coach website?

A headline that says who you help and with what, in plain words, then a line saying what changes, then one button. On a phone, that's all plenty of people will see before they decide to stay or leave, so keep the big photo from pushing it down.

### Does a fitness coach website need a booking calendar?

It helps, but only if it works on a phone. Test it with one thumb. If you have to pinch and zoom to book, use a simple button that says "Book a free call" and links to a booking page built for phones.

### Should a fitness coach show prices on their website?

Health and fitness coaching websites were better than coaching sites overall at showing a price, and in my opinion it's worth doing. On a phone, one clear line works best, with the full details on a separate pricing page.
