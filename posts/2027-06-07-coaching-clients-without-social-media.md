You can get coaching clients without social media by talking to people directly. Start with the people who already know you, then ask past clients for referrals, team up with professionals who meet your clients before you do, and get in front of small groups through talks, networking and podcasts. An email list and Google come later. Below are eight ways, ranked from the one I'd start with to the one that takes longest, each with a made-up example.

![Four people at a morning networking event in a café, two of them shaking hands](coaching-clients-without-social-media.jpg)

If you've been told that clients come from posting every day, and you've tried it and hated it, that's not your fault. There's a whole industry selling coaches posting plans and templates, and it suits them to make social media sound like the only way in. Coaches were getting clients by talking to people long before social media existed, and plenty still do.

## Why offline works so well for coaching

In my experience, the best clients come from taking people away from the screen for a real conversation, before anyone has even mentioned a discovery call. That way they get to know you as a person, and they don't feel like they've been fed into a process. Coaching is personal, and people want to know who they'll be spending an hour with before they hand over money.

The other reason is trust. When we mapped the types of people who buy coaching, proof from other people helped the decision for more than 9 in 10 of them. On social media you have to build that proof from nothing. Offline, it often comes with the introduction, because someone they already trust is pointing them towards you.

## How I've ranked them

I've ranked these for a coach who hasn't got many clients yet, by how much work each takes and how quickly it tends to bring in paying clients.

| Way to find clients | Effort | How fast it works |
|---|---|---|
| People you already know | Low | Days to weeks |
| Referrals from past clients | Low | Weeks, and it builds over time |
| Partner professionals | Medium | A few months to get going, then steady |
| Local talks and workshops | Medium | Weeks, because people can book on the night |
| Networking events | Medium to high | Slow at first, because people need to see you a few times |
| Speaking on podcasts | Medium | Slow and patchy, but an episode keeps working for months |
| Your email list | Medium, every week | Slow to build, but it keeps people warm |
| Being found on Google | High at the start | Slowest of all, often many months |

A business coach might move networking up, and a coach who helps local parents might move talks up. If you're not sure, start at the top.

## 1. People you already know

In my opinion this is the best place to look for your first client, and it's easy to skip because it feels awkward. You make a list of everyone who knows you, from old colleagues to people at the gym and your cousin's other half. Then you message them one at a time and tell them, in plain words, what you're doing and who you help.

All you're asking is for them to keep you in mind and pass your name on. Somebody always knows somebody.

*Made-up example:* Bridget is a career coach who used to be a teacher. She sends 40 personal messages to people she's worked with over the years, saying she now coaches teachers who want to move into a different job and asking if they know anyone thinking about it. Three people reply with a name, and one of those conversations turns into a client.

If you haven't had a paying client yet, [getting your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into this in more detail.

## 2. Referrals from past clients

If you've coached anyone at all, even at a reduced price, they're the best salespeople you'll ever have. The person they tell already trusts them.

The trouble is that a lot of us feel awkward asking, so we don't. Ask near the end of your work together, when they're feeling good about what's changed. Ask in a specific way, something like "Do you know anyone else who's where you were six months ago?" A woolly "let me know if anyone needs a coach" gets forgotten by teatime.

*Made-up example:* Adrian coaches people who've just been made managers. On the last session he asks each client whether anyone else at their company has just been promoted. One client mentions him to two colleagues, and both book a call within the month.

## 3. Partner professionals who see your clients first

Your clients nearly always talk to someone else before they think of a coach. A person going through a divorce talks to a solicitor or a mediator. A small business owner talks to their accountant. Those people hear the problem first, and they often don't have anyone good to send them to.

So find three or four people in those jobs, buy them a coffee, and ask about their clients. Don't pitch. Tell them who you help, and give them a one-page sheet they can hand over.

If you're thinking of paying someone for referrals, check first. Some professions, solicitors for example, have strict rules about referral fees and have to tell their client about them, so ask them before you offer anything. Check the rules where you live.

*Made-up example:* Yvonne is a divorce coach. She meets two family mediators and a solicitor in her town and explains that she helps people with the practical and emotional side of starting again. That's something mediators and solicitors don't do, so they're glad to have someone to send people to. Six months later one mediator is sending her someone every few weeks.

## 4. Local talks and workshops

Standing in front of 15 people for 40 minutes does more for trust than almost anything else on this list. The ones with the problem you help with will come up to you afterwards.

Libraries, community centres, business clubs and workplaces are often looking for someone to speak. Pick one problem your clients have and give a talk that's actually useful on its own, not a 40-minute advert. At the end, offer something simple, like a short call for anyone who wants to talk about their own situation.

If people come to a venue you've hired, it's worth asking an insurance broker whether you need public liability cover.

*Made-up example:* Patrick coaches parents of teenagers. He gives a free evening talk at a secondary school on what to do when your teenager stops talking to you. 22 parents turn up, five ask for a call, and two become clients.

## 5. Networking events

Networking gets a bad name because a lot of people go to sell. In my opinion hardly anyone buys coaching off the back of a 60-second pitch. What works is turning up regularly to the same group, being interested in other people, and becoming the person everyone knows helps with one particular problem.

Pick events where your clients go, or where the partner professionals from number 3 go. I'd rather you went to one group every month than tried ten groups once each.

*Made-up example:* Sunita is a business coach for small shop owners. She joins a monthly breakfast club for local business owners and goes every month for a year. By month four, people are introducing her as "the one who helps shop owners take a day off", and by the end of the year she's had five clients.

## 6. Speaking on podcasts

A podcast is like a talk, but the listener is on their own, often with headphones in, for half an hour or more. By the end they feel like they know you.

Skip the giant shows. Look for small podcasts about the problem you help with, the kind of small show where the host is always looking for guests. Searching your clients' problem in a podcast app is a good place to start. Write to the host with two or three specific things you could talk about. When you're on, tell stories and be useful. At the end, give one simple way to get in touch.

*Made-up example:* Marcus coaches men who've just become dads. He writes to six small parenting podcasts and gets on three. One episode brings in two enquiries in the first week, and the odd email a year later.

## 7. Your email list

An email list is for keeping in touch with people you've met in the other ways above, who aren't ready to buy yet. A short, useful email every week or two means you're the one they think of when they are ready.

Get people on your list after a talk, a podcast or a coffee by offering something they'd actually want, like a short guide or a checklist. Then write to them like a person, and every so often tell them how to work with you.

There are rules here. In the UK, you usually need people's permission before you send them marketing emails, and in the US every marketing email has to include a way to unsubscribe. Check the rules where you live.

*Made-up example:* Tanya coaches nurses through burnout. She collects 60 email addresses from two hospital staff talks and writes every fortnight. Nothing happens for three months. Then two readers reply to the same email saying "this is me", and both book calls.

## 8. Being found on Google

This one is last because it takes the longest, not because it doesn't work. Being found on Google means writing pages that answer the questions your clients type in. A page that answers one of those questions properly can bring you enquiries for years.

The catch is that you need a website people can understand. When we checked 10,954 coaching websites, only around 1 in 7 could be understood by a stranger in five seconds. If you meet clients in person, Google also lets some local businesses have a free business profile that shows up in local searches, so check Google's rules to see if you qualify.

*Made-up example:* Owen is a confidence coach for people who hate public speaking. He writes one plain page a month answering a question his clients ask him. After about eight months, those pages start bringing in an enquiry or two a month.

If you're not ready for a website yet, [how to get coaching clients without a website](/blog/coaching-clients-without-a-website) shows you what to use in its place.

## What I'd do if I were starting this week

I'd start with the first two straight away, because they cost nothing and they're the quickest. Then I'd pick one of the four after that which suits the people I help and stick with it for a few months. A divorce coach would go for partner professionals, for example. An email list and Google come later.

None of this means social media is bad. If you enjoy it and your clients are there, it can help, and we've written about [getting coaching clients on LinkedIn](/blog/coaching-clients-on-linkedin) and [getting coaching clients from Facebook](/blog/coaching-clients-on-facebook). ## FAQ

### Can you really get coaching clients without social media?

Yes. People want to talk to you before they buy coaching. Referrals, people you know, partner professionals and local talks all get you into those conversations without posting anything.

### What's the fastest way to get a coaching client without social media?

Message people who already know you, one at a time, and tell them who you help. Then ask any past clients if they know someone in the same situation they were in. Those two can work within weeks.

### Do I need a website to get coaching clients offline?

Not to start with. People who meet you or are referred to you will often book a call straight away. A clear website helps later, especially for Google.

### How do I ask for referrals without sounding desperate?

Be specific and ask near the end of your work together. "Do you know anyone else who's where you were six months ago?" is easy to answer. "Let me know if anyone needs a coach" isn't.
