Relationship coaching is one of those jobs people come to because they've been through something themselves. A divorce that nearly flattened them. A marriage they fought for and saved. Years of being single while everyone else paired off, and then working out how to be happy anyway. They come out the other side and think, I could help people with this. I think that's a great reason, and it'll make you better at the job than any course will.

But there are things about relationship coaching that the courses don't really prepare you for. Some of them are about the work itself. The rest are about getting people to trust you enough to pay you in the first place, which is harder in this area than almost any other kind of coaching.

So rather than another list of training providers, here are six things I'd want someone to tell me before I became a relationship coach. They come from two pieces of my own research: what people actually buy and write about their relationships, and what 10,954 coaching websites do when they're trying to win clients.

![A relationship coach listening to a client in a quiet sitting room](relationship-coach-session.jpg)

## 1. "Relationship coaching" is at least fourteen different jobs

Say "relationship coach" and you'd think it's one thing. It isn't. When we looked at the books people buy about their relationships and what they write about them, we found at least fourteen different reasons people go looking for help. And the person in one of them wants something completely different from the person in the next.

Here they are, biggest first, by how many books we found on each one:

- **Divorce and relationships ending**, 81 books. "We don't love each other anymore." "The last straw."
- **Saving a marriage**, 57 books. "He feels like we are just roommates."
- **Intimacy and sex**, 41 books. The dead bedroom that's turned into a crisis.
- **Being single and happy about it**, 40 books. "Everyone around me is getting married and I am still single."
- **Recovering from a toxic or abusive relationship**, 40 books. "No contact isn't helping."
- **Dating and finding love**, 37 books. "I'm really tired of constantly trying to find love."
- **Friendship and loneliness**, 30 books. "Friendless at 30."
- **Co-parenting with an ex**, 23 books. "Co-parenting seems impossible."
- **Attachment patterns**, 23 books, from people stuck in the same anxious or avoidant cycle.
- **Estrangement from family**, 23 books. "My mental health worsens when I talk to them."
- **Getting over an affair**, 21 books. "Partner of 7 years has been cheating for 3 years."
- **Couples and money**, 19 books. "My husband's spending is out of control."
- **Step-families**, 14 books. "I am at the end of my rope."
- **The empty nest**, 12 books, from parents who gave everything to their kids and now don't know who they are.

Now imagine one website trying to speak to all fourteen of those people at once. It can't. The woman whose husband has just told her about a three-year affair doesn't want to read about "deepening connection". She wants to know if anyone can help her get through the next month.

So the first decision isn't which course to take. It's which of those people you're going to help. Pick the one you understand best, usually the one you've lived, and lead with that. You can work with others too, but your website has to make the first group feel like it was written for them. [The niche and audience post](/blog/niche-versus-audience) goes into why that works.

## 2. You're often selling to one person about two

This is the one that catches a lot of new relationship coaches out. If you work with couples, the person who finds your website is almost never "the couple". It's one of them, usually the one who's more worried, and often the other one doesn't know they're looking.

Look at what people write about saving a marriage. One of the biggest worries is that their partner "refuses counseling". So the person reading your website is thinking two things at once. Can you help us? And how on earth do I get them to agree to it?

If your website only talks about "couples", you've missed the person actually reading it. Talk to them. Say you can start with just them. Say what you'd do if their partner won't come along yet. That one change can make the difference between a booking and someone closing the page with a sigh.

## 3. Coaching isn't therapy, and you need to know where the line is

Some of the people who'll find you are dealing with things that need a therapist, not a coach. Abuse, trauma, serious depression, someone who isn't safe at home. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so nobody's going to stop you taking those clients on. That's exactly why you need to decide for yourself where your line is, and stick to it.

Know when to say "this isn't for me, but here's who can help". Have a couple of therapists you trust that you can refer people to. And say on your website what you do and don't work with. It might feel like you're turning business away, but it does the opposite. The people who are right for you trust you more when they can see you know your limits.

This counts double if you want to work with people recovering from toxic or abusive relationships. There's a lot of demand there, with 40 books in our research and plenty of people writing that the usual advice isn't working. But it's also the area where you really need to know what you're doing and when to hand someone on.

## 4. Your story is your best asset, but it's not only your story

When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That's the sense that you've seen their problem before and know what to do about it. A certificate is the weakest version of that. Having been through it yourself is the strongest.

So if you got through a divorce, or saved your marriage, or rebuilt your life after an affair, say so. Of the coaching websites that did best in my research, 90.7% had a story on the page, and a third of all coaching websites had nothing human on them at all.

But here's the catch that's special to relationship coaching. Your story involves someone else. Your ex, your partner, your kids. So tell it in a way you'd be happy for them to read. Talk about what you went through and what you learned, not about what they did wrong. A relationship coach who's still slagging off their ex on their About page isn't going to fill anyone with confidence.

## 5. Proof is harder, because nobody wants their name on it

Two thirds of the coaching websites we looked at showed no proof at all. In relationship coaching there's a good reason that's tempting. Your clients don't want their mates knowing they went to a coach about their marriage, never mind putting their full name and photo on your website.

You can still get proof without embarrassing anyone. Ask clients if you can use their first name, or their first name and the city they live in. Get them to say what changed in their own words, because a specific change is believable even without a surname. "We'd stopped talking about anything except the kids. Now we have a proper night out every week and actually enjoy it" says more than "Amazing coach, highly recommend". And always, always ask permission first. [The testimonials post](/blog/coaching-testimonials) goes into how to get the good ones.

## 6. People find you at 2am, so your website has to sound like 2am

Think about when people go looking for a relationship coach. It's not usually a calm Tuesday afternoon. It's after the row, or after finding the messages, or lying awake next to someone who feels like a stranger. They type what they're feeling into Google, and they're in a hurry.

We looked at 10,954 live coaching websites and checked each homepage for 10 things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch. Only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. Someone in the middle of a relationship crisis gives you less time than that.

So write your headline in the words they'd use at 2am. Not "Deepen your connection and create the relationship you deserve". Something like "He says he doesn't love me any more, and I don't know if we can come back from this." When someone reads their own thought on your page, they stop. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check yours.

## What does that look like on an About page?

Here's an About page written two ways, for a made-up relationship coach who works with people whose marriage feels like it's turned into flatmates. Her name, her story and the details are made up to show you the shape. Yours have to be real.

**Before:**

> Hi, I'm Claire! I'm a certified relationship coach passionate about helping couples deepen their connection and create thriving, fulfilling partnerships. With a holistic, heart-centred approach, I empower my clients to communicate authentically and rediscover the love they deserve.

There's nothing wrong with Claire, but you learn nothing about her, and nothing about whether she gets your problem.

**After:**

> Six years ago my husband and I were living like flatmates. We were polite, we ran the house, we sorted the kids, and we hadn't had a proper conversation in about two years. I thought that was just what marriage turned into.
>
> It isn't. We didn't fix it overnight, and for a while I was the only one trying. But we got there, and that's what I help other people do now.
>
> I work with people whose marriage has gone quiet, and I'm happy to start with just you if your partner isn't ready yet. I'm not a therapist, and if what's going on needs one, I'll tell you and help you find a good one.

Now you know who she is, what she's been through, who she helps, that she'll start with just you, and where her limits are. All in about a hundred words.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to be a relationship coach?

Not by law, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. Training will make you better at the job and help you spot when someone needs a therapist instead, so in my opinion it's worth doing. But clients will care far more about your own experience and their results than your certificate.

### What's the difference between a relationship coach and a therapist?

A therapist is trained to work with mental health problems, trauma and abuse. A relationship coach helps people make changes in how they relate to each other and what they do next. If someone needs a therapist, a good relationship coach tells them and helps them find one.

### Should I work with couples or individuals?

Either can work, but remember that it's usually one person who finds you, even when you work with couples. Speak to that person on your website, and say you can start with just them if their partner isn't ready.
