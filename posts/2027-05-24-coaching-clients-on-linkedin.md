To get coaching clients on LinkedIn, you fix your profile first so a stranger can tell in a few seconds who you help and what changes for them. Then you post about your clients' problems twice a week, you talk to the people who respond, and you send connection messages that start a conversation instead of a sales pitch. The clients come from talking to people, and the profile and posts are what get those talks started.

![A man in a charcoal blazer reading his phone at a pavement café table with a coffee](coaching-clients-on-linkedin.jpg)

LinkedIn won't send you clients on its own, and it doesn't suit every coach. But for the right coach it's the best free place I know to find people who can pay, because they're already sitting there with their job title on show.

## Who LinkedIn suits, and who it doesn't

LinkedIn works for coaches whose clients are thinking about work when they're on it. So for example, executive coaches, career coaches, leadership coaches and business coaches are in the right place, because their buyers are on LinkedIn every day, and quite often the person paying is a company, not the person being coached.

It's a harder place for coaches whose clients have a private problem they'd never mention at work. If you're a dating coach, a grief coach, a parenting coach or a relationship coach, people aren't going to like a post about their divorce where their boss can see it. You might pick up the odd client there, but I wouldn't make it your main thing. [Our guide to social media for coaches](/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coaches) goes through which platforms suit which kind of coach.

## The profile, top to bottom

Before anyone replies to a message, they click on your name and decide whether you're worth a conversation. So the profile comes first, in the order a stranger sees it.

### The headline

This is the line under your name, and it shows next to every comment you leave, every post and every connection request. In my opinion it's the line people judge you on first, because they see it everywhere you go on LinkedIn.

We scored more than 11,000 coaches' LinkedIn headlines out of 10, and the average was about 2.6. 420 of them had written "Executive Coach" and nothing else, and another 364 had written just "Coach". In our sample alone, 420 people had the identical headline. If a buyer is scrolling past a list like that, why would they click on yours?

A good headline says who you help and what changes for them. Here are some before and after rewrites. All of them are made up as examples.

| Before (made up) | After (made up) |
|---|---|
| Executive Coach | I coach newly promoted directors through their first year in the job |
| Coach, Speaker, Author, Mentor, Mum | Career coach for teachers who want to leave teaching and don't know what else they can do |
| Helping leaders reach their full potential | Leadership coach for engineers who've just been made team leads |
| Business Coach / Strategist / ICF ACC | Business coach for accountancy firms with five to twenty staff who want the partners to stop doing everything |

The after versions are longer and plainer, and that's on purpose. Someone who's just been made a team lead will read "engineers who've just been made team leads" and think, that's me. Nobody thinks "that's me" when they read "full potential".

### The photo

Use a clear, recent photo of your face, where you look like the person who'll turn up on the call. You don't need a photographer. A friend with a phone, some daylight and a plain wall behind you is fine. Don't use a holiday photo cropped out of a group, a logo, or a picture from ten years ago. The person thinking about hiring you wants to know if they'd like spending an hour with you.

### The banner

The banner is the wide strip behind your photo, and plenty of coaches leave it blank or put a sunset on it. Use it to repeat your headline in a slightly different way, or to say what someone should do next. For example (a made-up one), "Coaching for new directors. Message me and I'll send you the questions I ask in a first session."

### The about section

This is where coaches write about themselves, and it's quite a big mistake. They start with "I'm a certified coach with 15 years' experience in..." and the reader is gone by the second line.

Write it to the person you help instead. Start with what their week looks like right now, in their words. When we read what people write about their problems, the leadership buyers said things like "I am stuck between the manager and team members" and "how to get people to respect and follow you as a leader". Career buyers wrote things like "I'm stuck in a career rut and I'm becoming more miserable by the day". If your first line sounds like that, the right person keeps reading.

Then say what changes for them, how you work, and one plain next step at the end. Put your background near the bottom. It backs up what you've said above it.

### The featured section

This is the row of boxes under your about section, and it's the closest thing LinkedIn gives you to a shop window. Put two or three things in it that help someone decide. A short story from a past client (with their permission), a post that explains how you work, and a link to your website or a page where they can book a call. If you haven't got a website yet, or yours needs work, [what an executive coach website needs](/blog/executive-coach-website) is a good place to start.

### The experience section

People skip this, but buyers read it, especially when a company is paying. A coach who used to be a finance director and now coaches finance directors has a very good story, and it should be easy to see.

So under your coaching business, write two or three lines about who you coach and what you help with. Then leave your older jobs in, with a line on each, because they're often the reason someone trusts you.

## The connection message that isn't a pitch

Here's where a lot of coaches go wrong. They connect with someone and send a sales message within the hour. You've probably had one yourself. "Hi, I help leaders become the best version of themselves. Do you have 15 minutes this week for a call?" You didn't reply, did you?

There's a newer version that's even worse. The message drops in my name, my town and what I do, all scraped from my profile by a tool. The sender thinks it's personalised. It isn't, and it's ugly. Anybody can see a machine filled in the blanks.

Think about meeting someone at a work do. You don't walk up, shake their hand and ask them to buy something, and LinkedIn is no different.

So when you send a connection request, add a short note that's about them and asks for nothing. Here's a made-up one you can change to suit you:

> "Hi [first name], I saw your comment on [person]'s post about managing people you used to work alongside. I coach new team leads and that's something I hear about a lot, so I thought I'd connect. I just liked what you said."

Don't add a link, an offer or a booking calendar. If they reply, ask them something about their situation, listen, and only mention coaching if they ask what you do.

If someone comments on your posts a few times, it's fine to send a message like this (again, made up):

> "Thanks for your comments on the last couple of posts, [first name]. Sounds like the new role is keeping you busy. How's it going with the team?"

If they say it's going badly and they don't know what to do, then you can offer a call. By then you're answering what they told you, so offering a call feels normal. When you do get on that call, [how to sell coaching without feeling pushy](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching) goes through what to do.

## What not to do

There are two things I'd stop straight away if you're doing them.

The first is automated messages. There are tools that send connection requests and follow-up messages for you, hundreds a day, and they're a bad idea. People can smell an automated message, and they'll ignore it or report it. LinkedIn's user agreement doesn't allow bots or other automated ways of using the site, and accounts do get restricted for it. Check LinkedIn's user agreement yourself, as the rules can change. Even if yours doesn't, your name is going on messages you didn't write, to people you know nothing about.

The second is the "I help X do Y" template pasted everywhere. You've seen it. "I help busy women find balance." "I help leaders become the best version of themselves." The shape of the sentence is fine. The trouble is that it's usually so vague it could belong to anyone, and when it's pasted on every post and message it starts to look like an advert you can't switch off. Say it once, clearly and specifically, in your headline, then talk like a person everywhere else.

## A normal week on LinkedIn

You don't need to be on LinkedIn all day. This takes about half an hour on the days you post and a few minutes on the days you don't. Before you start, rewrite your profile using the steps above, then spend a few days just reading. Follow 20 or 30 people who look like your clients, and write down the words they use for their problems. Give the routine below a month before you judge it.

### On the days you post

Post twice a week. Each post takes one problem your clients have and says something useful about it, in plain words. So for example, an executive coach might write about the first time a new director has to give a bad review to someone who used to be their peer. Tell a short story, either a real one you have permission to share, or a made-up one that you say is an example, then say what you'd do about it.

Don't end with "DM me to book a call". End with a question, or just stop.

Once a month, swap one of those posts for an offer people can say yes to in the comments or by message, like a short guide, the list of questions you ask in a first session, or a free 20-minute call for one specific group, like "new directors who've just inherited a team". If you're not sure how to run that first call, [the coaching discovery call](/blog/coaching-discovery-call) walks through it.

### On the days you don't

Leave a few proper comments on other people's posts. A couple of sentences that add something is plenty.

Then look at who's been liking and commenting on your posts, and who else is commenting on the posts where you've left comments. Send five to ten connection requests a day to the ones who look like clients, with a short, personal note like the one above.

Reply to every comment on your own posts, and when someone who looks like a client comments more than once, send them a short message about what they said.

After a month, look at which posts got comments from people who look like clients, rather than other coaches, and which messages got replies. Do more of those and drop the rest.

## FAQ

### How do I get coaching clients on LinkedIn without being salesy?

Fix your profile so it's clear who you help, post about your clients' problems, and send connection notes that ask for nothing. Only offer a call when someone tells you they're struggling and wants help.

### What should a coach put in their LinkedIn headline?

Who you help and what changes for them, in plain words. "Executive Coach" on its own tells nobody anything. Something like "I coach newly promoted directors through their first year" (a made-up example) gives the right person a reason to click.

### How often should a coach post on LinkedIn?

Twice a week is plenty to start with, as long as you also comment on other people's posts and reply to everyone who comments on yours.

### Is LinkedIn good for life coaches?

It depends on what you coach. If your clients bring the problem to work, like confidence in meetings or a career change, it can work well. If it's something private, like dating or grief, people won't engage where their colleagues can see, so look elsewhere first.
