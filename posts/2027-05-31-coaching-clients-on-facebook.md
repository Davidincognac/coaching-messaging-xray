To get coaching clients on Facebook, pick one of three routes and do it properly. You can post on your own profile so the people who already know you find out what you do. You can be helpful in other people's groups where your clients already spend time. Or you can run your own group and invite the members to something live, like a webinar. Whichever you choose, the clients come from conversations in Messenger or on a call, and the posts are there to start them.

![A woman curled up on a sofa in the evening, scrolling and typing on a tablet](coaching-clients-on-facebook.jpg)

I run my own Facebook group, and the biggest thing I learned from it is in the third route.

Facebook ads are a separate subject with their own costs, so I'm leaving them out. Everything here is free apart from your time.

## Is Facebook the right place for you?

Facebook suits coaches whose clients have a problem in their home or personal life, the kind of thing people talk about with friends and family. In my opinion people are much happier talking on Facebook about their kids, their marriage, moving abroad and being lonely than about wanting a promotion, where their boss might see it.

So if you coach parents, people going through a divorce, expats or people trying to build a social life, Facebook is where a lot of your clients already are, talking about exactly what you help with. If you coach new directors or business owners, [our guide to getting coaching clients on LinkedIn](/blog/coaching-clients-on-linkedin) is probably a better first stop. And if you're still not sure which platform suits you, [our guide to social media for coaches](/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coaches) goes through each one.

## The three routes side by side

| | Your own profile | Other people's groups | Your own group |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it works | People who already know you see what you do | You give useful answers where your clients ask questions | You gather your clients in one place and invite them to something live |
| What you post | Stories about the problem you help with, and the odd plain ask | Helpful comments and replies, hardly ever a post of your own | Regular useful posts, questions and invitations to a webinar |
| The usual mistake | Turning your profile into a billboard | Dropping links and sending sales messages to strangers | Judging the group by how many people comment |
| Who it suits | New coaches whose friends look like their clients | Coaches with a clear niche whose clients already gather in groups | Coaches who can stick at it for months and have something to teach |

## Route one: your own profile

Your profile is seen by friends, family, old school friends, people you used to work with, and their friends when they like or comment. They already know you, so a lot of the trust is already there. In my opinion your first few clients are quite likely to be people who know you, or know someone who does.

### What to post

Tell people plainly what you do, who you help and what changes for them. Do it once properly, then again every so often, because people forget.

In between, post short stories about the problem you help with, either real ones you have permission to share or made-up ones you label as examples. Here's a made-up post for a coach who works with people after a divorce:

> "A while ago someone told me the hardest bit of their divorce wasn't the paperwork. It was the first Sunday on their own, when the house went quiet and they didn't know what to do with themselves. If that sounds like you right now, you're not doing anything wrong. That first stretch is hard for nearly everyone I talk to."

And now and then, maybe once a month, make a straight ask. Again, this one is made up:

> "I've got space for two new clients in June. I work with people who've separated in the last year and feel stuck. If that's you, or someone you know, send me a message and we'll have a chat."

That's no more pushy than a friend telling you they've started dog walking.

Facebook has its own rules about using a personal profile for business, and those rules change, so check them yourself.

### Where people go wrong

Where people go wrong here is turning their profile into a billboard. Every post is an advert with a link that ends with "DM me", so friends scroll past or mute them, and they lose the very people likeliest to recommend them. Others are so worried about looking salesy that nobody knows they coach at all.

A parenting coach who's a parent already has a profile full of the people they want to help, which is why this route works so well for new coaches whose friends look like their clients.

## Route two: other people's groups

There are Facebook groups for local parents, for expats in a particular country, for people going through a separation, and plenty more. Your clients are already in there, asking questions. You become the person who gives a proper, useful answer, and over time people notice your name, click on your profile, and a few send you a message.

### Read the rules first

Every group is somebody else's space, and the admin makes the rules. So before you post or comment anything:

- Read the group rules. Some groups don't allow any promotion at all, some only allow it on a set day, and some don't allow links.
- Don't post your offer or your website unless the rules say you can.
- Don't send private messages to members you've never spoken to. That's spam, people report it, and the admin may well remove you.
- If you're not sure, ask the admin first.

If someone in a support group is clearly struggling badly, remember you're a coach, not a doctor or a therapist. Coaches can't diagnose or treat anything, so kindly suggest they talk to their GP or doctor, or a qualified professional. If someone seems to be in danger, point them to emergency services or a crisis line where they live.

### What to say

Answer the question that's been asked, properly, in the comments. We looked at 30 books people bought about living abroad and what the buyers wrote about them. One buyer wrote "I am living alone in a foreign country, what should I do with myself?"

Here's a made-up reply from an expat coach:

> "I remember that feeling well. What often helps is picking one regular thing every week where you'll see the same faces, like a language class or a running club. How long have you been there?"

There's no link and no mention of coaching. If that person replies and wants to talk more, move to Messenger then, and if they ask what you do, tell them.

### What gets coaches thrown out of groups

The thing that gets coaches removed is treating groups like a noticeboard: pasting the same "I help women find their confidence, message me for a free call" post everywhere, then messaging anyone who likes it. It gets you a bad name in exactly the places your clients are. Joining far too many groups doesn't help either, so pick two or three where your clients really are and turn up regularly. Be patient, because it takes a while before people recognise your name.

## Route three: your own group

Set up a group around your clients' problem rather than around you. "Starting again after divorce" is a better name than "Siobhan's Coaching Community" (both made up), because people join a group that's about them.

A group gives people a feeling of belonging, and that helps some people decide. When we looked at what pushes people to hire a coach, feeling part of a group helped a bit over 4 in 10 of the different kinds of buyer we looked at make up their mind. Seeing other people get results helped more than 9 in 10 of them. In a group, members see each other, and they see the people you've helped.

You can set questions people answer when they ask to join. Use them to check you can help the person, and to find out roughly what they're struggling with. Keep these questions light, don't ask for health details, and check the data protection rules where you live if you keep the answers.

### What running my own group taught me

Running my own Facebook group taught me that you don't need the members to talk in the group. What you want is to get them into a webinar. They still don't have to talk there either, but they're sharing your space and your energy.

### What to post

Post something useful once or twice a week, in the words your members use. We looked at 81 books people bought about relationships and divorce and what those buyers wrote. One wrote "I initiated my divorce and now I feel so lonely and lost. I went from always having somebody home to nobody". A post that starts from that feeling will be read by the right person, even if they never comment.

Then, every few weeks, invite them to something live. Here's a made-up invitation:

> "Next Thursday at 7pm I'm doing a free 40-minute webinar called 'The first six months after you separate'. I'll go through what usually goes wrong and what helps. You don't need to have your camera on or say anything. Just come along and listen. Comment 'yes' and I'll send you the link."

If you want people to sign up with their email too, [our list of lead magnet ideas for coaches](/blog/lead-magnet-ideas-for-coaches) has plenty of things you could offer.

### Don't judge it by the comments

It's easy to panic when a group goes quiet and think it's failing, then fill it with "Tell me your biggest struggle in the comments!" posts that nobody answers. Plenty of people read every post and never say a word, and that's fine. Keep an eye out for other coaches filling it with adverts too, and remove those posts.

You need to keep a group going for months and be comfortable running a webinar. If you can't see yourself doing that for six months, I'd start with one of the other two routes.

## From Facebook to a real conversation

All three routes end the same way. Someone sends you a message, asks for more in a comment, or stays behind after a webinar. Talk to them like a person, ask about their situation and listen. Don't send a booking link straight away, and if it sounds like you can help, offer a call. When you get on that call, [how to sell coaching without feeling pushy](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching) goes through what to do.

If you're starting from nothing, I'd begin with your own profile and two groups to help in, and leave your own group until you've got people to invite.

## FAQ

### How do I get coaching clients on Facebook without being salesy?

Tell people plainly what you do, then spend the rest of your time being useful. Post stories about the problem you help with, answer questions properly in groups, and only offer a call when someone tells you they're struggling and asks for help.

### Can I promote my coaching in Facebook groups?

Only if the group rules say you can. Read the rules before you post anything, don't add links unless they're allowed, and never send private sales messages to members you haven't spoken to. If you're not sure, ask the admin.

### Do I need my own Facebook group to get coaching clients?

No. Your own profile and other people's groups can both bring you clients without a group of your own. Your own group is worth it if you can keep it going for months and you're happy running webinars.
