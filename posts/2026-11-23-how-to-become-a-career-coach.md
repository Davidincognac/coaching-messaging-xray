If you search for how to become a career coach, you'll find page after page telling you which course to take. Some of them are good. But a course teaches you how to coach. It doesn't tell you who to coach, what they're actually worried about, or how they'll ever find you. And that's the bit that decides whether you end up with a business or an expensive certificate on the wall.

So this isn't another list of courses. It's the part the courses skip, built from two things I've spent a long time on: what people actually buy and write when they're unhappy at work, and what 10,954 coaching websites do when they're trying to win clients.

I'll take it in the order you'd actually need it.

![A career coach going through a client's CV with him at a desk](career-coach-cv.jpg)

## Do you need a qualification to become a career coach?

No, not legally. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so anyone can call themselves a career coach tomorrow. Whether you should get trained is a different question, and in my opinion the answer is usually yes, because it'll make you better at the job.

But be clear about what a qualification does for you when it comes to winning clients. When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That's the sense that you've seen their problem before and know what to do about it. A certificate is the weakest version of that. Having been through the exact thing your client is going through, or having spent years on the other side of it, is the strongest.

So if you spent fifteen years in HR deciding who got promoted, or you rebuilt your own career after being made redundant, that's worth more to a client than any letters after your name. Say it. And if you haven't got that kind of background, that's fine too, but your training on its own won't be the thing that makes people pick you.

## Step 1: Decide who you're going to help

This is the step new career coaches tend to skip, and it's the one that costs them the most. "I help people with their careers" sounds sensible, but it's like a doctor saying "I help people with their bodies". Nobody books an appointment off the back of that.

People come to a career coach for very different reasons. We looked at the books people buy about their working lives and what they write about them, and found at least seven different reasons, each wanting something different. Here they are, with how many books we found on each one and the worry that sits underneath.

| Why they want a career coach | Books we found on it | What they're worried about |
|---|---|---|
| Stuck and can't decide on a direction | 159 | "No one would be interested in employing me" |
| Keep getting rejected at interview | 39 | "What am I doing wrong?" |
| Burnt out at work | 41 | That rest hasn't worked, so nothing will |
| Going through a midlife change | 68 | That it really is too late |
| Passed over for promotion | 34 | That the system is rigged against them |
| Going back after a career break | 25 | That employers will judge the gap |
| Just been made redundant | 20 | Trying again and still failing |

Look at the difference between the top and the bottom of that table. People who feel stuck have 159 books aimed at them. They've done the personality tests, read the lists and tried the advice, and they're tired of all of it. So you'd need something genuinely different to say. People who've just been made redundant have far fewer books aimed at them, and the ones that exist are mostly about finding the next job, not about getting over being let go. That's a much quieter place to be.

But quiet comes with a catch. Some people who've been made redundant are worried about paying the bills, which makes it a harder sell. So it isn't as simple as picking the smallest number. Pick the group where you've got something real to say, where you understand the worry, and where people can afford what you charge.

You don't have to turn everyone else away. You just lead with one group, so that the right person knows straight away you're for them.

## Step 2: Be honest about whether you're coaching or teaching

This one winds me up a bit, so bear with me. A lot of what gets sold as career coaching isn't coaching at all. Rewriting someone's CV is a service. Showing them how to answer interview questions is teaching. Coaching is when you ask the questions and help the person work out their own answers.

All three are useful, and plenty of clients want a mix. Somebody who's been rejected 30 times probably needs some teaching about interviews before any amount of self-discovery will help. That's fine. What isn't fine is calling it all coaching and leaving the client to guess what they're getting.

So decide what you actually do, and say it plainly. "I'll help you work out what you want next, then we'll get your CV and interviews ready for it" tells someone exactly what happens. "Holistic career coaching" tells them nothing.

## Step 3: Write the one sentence that says what you do

Before you build anything, write one sentence that says who you help, what's wrong and what changes. You'll use it on your website, your LinkedIn profile, your business cards and when someone asks what you do at a party. If you can't write it, you're not ready to market yourself yet.

Here's a simple way to do it. Fill in the blanks:

"I help [who] who [what's wrong, in their words] to [what changes]."

Let's say you spent years in HR and you want to help people who've been made redundant. A first go might be:

"I help people who've been made redundant find a new job."

It's not bad, but it's thin. Every recruitment agency says the same thing. Now use their words and their worry:

"I help people who've been made redundant and are starting to feel like a failure to get their confidence back and land the right job, not just the first one."

That's someone a person can picture, with a worry they recognise and a result they want. It took two minutes, and it'll do more for you than a new logo ever will. If you want to check it, [the five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) is a good place to start.

## Step 4: Get your first clients from people who already know you

This is the unglamorous truth. Your first career coaching clients will almost certainly come from people who already know you. Former colleagues, people you've managed, friends of friends who've just been made redundant. They already trust you, so they skip the part where a stranger has to decide whether you're any good.

Tell people what you're doing, using your one sentence. Ask them who they know who's going through it. Offer your first few clients a lower price in return for an honest testimonial, because you're going to need proof. [How to get your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into this in more detail.

What I wouldn't do is spend months building a perfect website and a social media plan before you've worked with anyone. You'll learn more from your first five clients than from any amount of planning, and their words will make your marketing far better.

## Step 5: Build a website that says the same thing

Once you've got a few clients and some testimonials, build a simple website around your one sentence. The research is clear on what makes the difference. We looked at 10,954 live coaching websites and checked each homepage for 10 things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch. Only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. Two thirds showed no proof at all. And 82.3% didn't show a price anywhere.

So if your homepage says who it's for in the first line, shows a couple of real testimonials and gives at least a starting price, you're already ahead of the vast majority of coaches out there. [The career coach website post](/blog/career-coach-website) walks through it top to bottom, with a full example homepage.

## Step 6: Decide how you'll price it

You'll have to decide whether to put your prices on your website, and new coaches often hide them because they're nervous about the number. I understand why, but it usually makes things harder, because people guess, and they don't guess in your favour. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) goes through both options and how to make each one work.

Whatever you charge, sell it as a package with a clear result rather than single sessions. Somebody who's just been made redundant doesn't want "six sessions". They want to get back on their feet and into the right job. [The coaching offer post](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you how to put that into words.

## So what's the order?

Get trained if you want to, because it'll make you better. Then decide who you're for, be honest about what you actually do, write your one sentence, get your first clients from people who know you, and only then build the website and set the price. Courses tend to teach you the first bit and leave you to work out the rest on your own.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to be a career coach?

Not legally, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. Training will make you a better coach, but when it comes to winning clients, relevant experience counts for more than a certificate.

### Can I become a career coach without HR or recruitment experience?

Yes. Your own career change, years of managing people, or having been through redundancy yourself are all worth saying. Pick the type of client whose problem you understand best and lead with that.

### Which type of career coaching should I go into?

The one where you've got something real to say and where clients can afford you. People who feel stuck in their career are a big group, with 159 books aimed at them, so you'd need something different to stand out. Smaller groups, like people who've just been made redundant or are going back after a break, are quieter, but think about whether they can pay.
