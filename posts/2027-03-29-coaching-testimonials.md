If you want coaching testimonials that actually help you get clients, ask at the right moment, send a short message with five specific questions, then shape the answer into a few plain sentences that say what was wrong before and what changed after. Below are the words to use at each step, ready to copy.

![A coach on the phone with a client, smiling and writing in a notebook](coaching-testimonials-phone.jpg)

Here's what usually happens. A coach finishes with a client who's really pleased, thinks "I must ask them for a testimonial", and then doesn't, because it feels awkward. Or they do ask, and they get back "Hayley is amazing, highly recommend!" which is lovely to read and does nothing on a website.

It's worth sorting out. When we checked 10,954 live coaching websites, two thirds of them showed no proof at all, not one testimonial or client result. And when we split the sites into better and poorer ones, nearly 9 in 10 of the better sites showed proof, against fewer than 1 in 5 of the poor ones. A stranger can't try you before they buy, so they look for someone like them who already did.

## When to ask: the moment, not the end

Don't wait until the coaching finishes. The best time to ask is when the client tells you something has changed.

"I actually said no to my boss this week." "We went a whole weekend without a row." "I've booked the first appointment for the new business." That's when the change is clearest in their head. Leave it three months and it's faded into "it was really good".

So when you hear one of those moments, say something like this, there and then:

> "That's brilliant. Would you mind if I sent you a few questions about it later this week? I'd love to be able to show other people what's possible."

Asked like that, it's easy for them to say yes, because you're asking about their win, not about you. Then send the message within a few days, while it's still fresh.

If you've already finished with clients and never asked, message them anyway. In my experience they're happy to help if you make it easy.

## The exact message to send

Keep it short. Here's one you can copy and change to suit you:

> Hi [first name],
>
> I hope you're well. You mentioned last week that [the thing that changed], and I was really pleased for you.
>
> Would you be happy to answer five short questions about it? It's for my website, so other people in the same position can see what working together is like. A few lines for each is plenty, and it doesn't need to be polished. Just write it the way you'd say it.
>
> I'll tidy up the spelling and send you the finished version to check before anything goes anywhere. If you'd rather not, that's completely fine.
>
> Thanks,
> [your name]

Two lines in that message are doing the heavy lifting. "It doesn't need to be polished" takes the pressure off, so you get their real words instead of a stiff paragraph. And "I'll send you the finished version to check" tells them nothing goes public without their say.

## Five questions that get specific answers

If you just ask "can you write me a testimonial?" you'll get vague praise. Ask proper questions and you get their story. Here are the five I'd use:

1. **What was going on for you before we started?** This gets the problem in their own words, which is what a stranger with the same problem needs to read.
2. **What had you already tried?** This shows a reader the person didn't just give up at the first go. They'd tried books, apps or doing it alone, and it hadn't worked.
3. **What nearly stopped you from booking?** This is the best question of the five. Their doubt is your next client's doubt, and their answer shows that doubt was wrong.
4. **What's different now? Give me one example.** The "one example" bit is what gets you something specific instead of "I feel so much better".
5. **What would you say to someone who's thinking about it but isn't sure?** This often gives you the line to put at the top of the testimonial, or near the top of your homepage.

## Turning the answers into a testimonial

Here's a made-up example. The coach and client are invented, but the reply is the kind you'll really get.

The coach is a confidence coach. The client is Graham, 52. This is what he sends back:

> "before we started i was dreading every team meeting honestly, id sit there with stuff to say and just not say it then kick myself after. id read a couple of books and watched loads of youtube stuff didnt help. nearly didnt book cos i thought coaching was a bit american and fluffy if im honest lol. now i spoke up in the quarterly review last month, put forward an idea and its actually going ahead. id say just do it, its not what you think"

There's a really good testimonial in there. Keep his words, cut the repeats and fix the spelling. Here's the finished version:

> "I used to dread every team meeting. I'd sit there with things to say, not say them, then kick myself afterwards. I'd read books and watched loads of YouTube videos, and none of it helped. I nearly didn't book because I thought coaching would be a bit fluffy. Last month I spoke up in the quarterly review and put forward an idea, and it's actually going ahead. My advice is just do it, it's not what you think."
> Graham, 52

The problem, what he tried, his doubt and one specific result are all there, and every word of it is still his.

What you mustn't do is add things. If he didn't say he got promoted, you can't write that he got promoted. If you change the meaning even slightly, it's no longer his testimonial. Send it back to him and ask, "Are you happy for me to use this as it is?" Wait for a yes in writing.

## Getting permission and choosing the name

Always get permission before you put anyone's words on your website, and get it in writing. A reply to your email saying "yes, you can use this" to the exact final wording is usually enough. Keep it. If they ask you to take it down later, take it down.

Then ask how they'd like to be named. Give them options, because plenty of people don't want their full name used, especially if the coaching was about something personal like a relationship, money or drinking:

> "How would you like to be named? You could choose your full name, first name and age, first name and job, or just your initials. A photo is great if you're comfortable with it, but there's no need."

First name and age, like "Graham, 52", is a good middle ground. It sounds like a real person, and the reader can tell whether that person is like them. Initials work, but they're weaker, so keep the words very specific. "J.T." saying exactly what changed is still believable. "J.T." saying "life-changing!" isn't.

Never make up a name, an age or a job to make a testimonial look stronger. If it's not true, don't put it on the page.

## A short word on the rules

Testimonials have to be genuine and not misleading. In the UK, the Competition and Markets Authority and the Advertising Standards Authority both say you can't use fake reviews or testimonials, and you should be able to show a testimonial came from a real client. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission's endorsement guides say the same, and that if someone got something in return for their words, like a free session, you should say so.

Also, don't use one great result to suggest everyone will get it. And if you coach in health, food, fitness or anything near mental health, don't use testimonials that claim you cured or treated something, because coaches don't diagnose or treat. I'm not a lawyer, so check the rules where you live.

## Where to put them on the page

It's tempting to put every testimonial on one page called "Testimonials" or "Kind Words". In my opinion, not many visitors ever click on that page. Proof does its job when it's next to the thing a reader is doubting.

Here's where I'd put them:

- **One short one near the top of your homepage**, close to where you say who you help. One line is enough here, like Graham's "I used to dread every team meeting".
- **One next to your price or your offer**, because that's where people think "is it worth it?"
- **One next to your booking button**, ideally the one that answers the doubt about booking, like Graham's "I nearly didn't book because I thought coaching would be a bit fluffy".
- **A longer story further down**, for people who want to read more before they decide.

[What to put on a coaching homepage](/blog/what-to-put-on-a-coaching-homepage) shows where proof sits on a good homepage.

Video testimonials are great if a client's happy to do one, because people can see and hear they're real. Keep them under a minute, with a short written quote underneath, because not everyone will press play. Google reviews help too if you coach locally, but use them as well as your own testimonials, not instead.

## Three weak testimonials and what the five questions would get you

These three are made-up examples. You can't turn the weak one into the strong one yourself. The strong one is what the same client might say if you asked them the five questions.

**Weak one:** "Becky is amazing! So warm and insightful. Highly recommend!"

It's all about the coach, and a stranger learns nothing about what changed.

**After asking the five questions:** "I'd been putting off leaving my job for two years. After six sessions with Becky I'd handed in my notice and had my first two freelance clients booked." Anna, 38

**Weak one:** "Working with Ian was a transformational experience that helped me step into my power."

It's full of coaching words nobody says down the pub, so it sounds like the coach wrote it.

**After asking the five questions:** "I used to say yes to everything at work and then stay late every night to get it done. Now I say no to about one request a week, and I'm home for tea with my kids." Mel, 45

**Weak one:** "Great coach, really helped me with my confidence." K.

It's too short and too vague, and a single initial makes it look made up.

**After asking the five questions:** "I hadn't been on a date in four years because I'd talk myself out of it every time. I've been on three dates since we finished, and I actually enjoyed the last one." Kieran, 34

In every strong version the "after" is something you could see or count, like handing in the notice, being home for tea or going on three dates. That's what makes a reader think "that could be me".

## FAQ

### How many coaching testimonials do I need on my website?

Three or four good, specific ones is enough to start, placed next to the things people doubt, like your price and your booking button. In my opinion, one specific testimonial does more than ten vague ones.

### Can I edit a client's testimonial?

You can fix spelling, cut repeats and shorten it, but you can't add anything they didn't say or change what they meant. Always send them the finished version and get a written yes before you use it.

### What if a client doesn't want their name used?

Offer first name and age, first name and job, or initials. If you only use initials, make the words as specific as you can, so it's still believable.

### Should I ask for a testimonial if the coaching didn't go well?

No. Only ask clients who've told you something has changed for them. If someone's unhappy, ask them what went wrong instead, and learn from it.
