A good life coach website tells a stranger, within a few seconds, who you help and what changes for them. Then it gives each kind of visitor what they came for: a face they can trust, proof from people like them, what working with you involves and costs, and an easy way to book. If your website only does one of those jobs, you'll lose the people who needed one of the others.

![A grey-haired man in a pale blue shirt looking thoughtfully at his website on a laptop](life-coach-website.jpg)

The trouble is that people don't all arrive the same way. Someone who found you on Google wants something different from someone whose friend told them about you. So the best way I know to check a website is to read it several times, as different people, and notice where each one gets stuck.

Below is a made-up life coach called Wendy, her made-up website and five made-up visitors. None of them are real, but I'd bet you'll recognise the website.

## Wendy's website as it is now

Wendy has been a life coach for three years, and the people who've worked with her love her. Her homepage, from top to bottom, looks like this:

1. Her logo and a menu: Home, About, Services, Blog, Contact.
2. A big photo of a sunset over the sea, with the words "Live your best life" and underneath, "Find your purpose, your passion and your confidence." Then a button saying "Book a free discovery call".
3. "About me": four paragraphs starting with her 15 years in banking, her coaching qualification and her love of yoga.
4. "How I can help": three boxes called Confidence, Relationships and Career.
5. Her three latest blog posts.
6. Two testimonials: "Wendy is amazing!" from S.T. and "Highly recommend" from J.
7. "Sign up to my newsletter".
8. A contact form.

Her Services page says "Packages tailored to you. Contact me for pricing."

## Visitor 1: the stranger from Google

**Alison** is 52. Her youngest went off to university in September, and since then the house has felt very big and very empty. One evening she types "life coach for women after kids leave home" into Google, and Wendy's website comes up.

All she wants to know is whether this is for her. She reads the headline, glances at the photo and scrolls a little.

The trouble is, "Live your best life" could be on any website in the world. It doesn't mention children, or being 50, or that empty feeling she's got. She scrolls to the three boxes and thinks Wendy helps everyone with everything, which usually means nobody in particular. She goes back to Google and clicks the next one.

This is the biggest problem with life coach websites, in my opinion, and it comes from the name. "Life coach" covers almost anything. When we mapped why people hire a coach, we found over a hundred different reasons, and plenty of them are things a life coach would say they help with. When we checked 10,954 live coaching websites, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with.

Look at how different three of those reasons are when you read what people actually write.

**Midlife reinvention.** We found 68 books on it. People looking for help with it write things like "Is it too late for me?" and "How can I change my life at 50?".

**Empty nest.** A smaller group, with 12 books, but the feeling is very specific. One buyer wrote "I want him to live his life. How do I begin to live one of my own?".

**Confidence and self-worth.** A big group, with 236 books. Some of these buyers are young, and they've been fighting it for a long time. One wrote "How do I fight through low self esteem? Ive had it ever since I was 16. I am now 24".

Those are three completely different people. A 24-year-old who's struggled since school doesn't want the same headline as a 52-year-old whose kids have just moved out. If Wendy writes one headline for all three, none of them will feel it was written for them.

So what does Wendy do? When she looks back, nearly all her clients were women around 50 whose children had left home or were about to. That's her group. She doesn't have to turn anyone else away, but the top of her homepage should speak to them. If you want to check your own headline, [the five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) is the quickest way I know.

## Visitor 2: the friend of a friend

**Gary's** sister worked with Wendy last year and won't stop talking about it. Gary's wife has felt flat since she was made redundant, so the sister says "get her to have a look at Wendy". They look at the website together on the sofa.

They've already been told she's good. Now they want to see her, get a feel for what she's like and work out what happens if they get in touch, so they go to the About page and then back to the homepage looking for a photo.

There's no photo of Wendy on the homepage, just the sunset. On the About page there's a small picture at the bottom, after four paragraphs about banking and qualifications. Nothing says what a session is like, how long it lasts or whether it's on video. Gary's wife says "I'll think about it", which, as we all know, often means no.

Referrals are the warmest visitors you'll ever get. Somebody they trust has already told them you're good. What they need is to feel comfortable: your face near the top, a couple of warm lines from you, and a short bit on what happens when they book, so nobody's walking into the unknown.

## Visitor 3: the person from social media

**Lucy** follows Wendy on Facebook. Last week Wendy wrote about the first Monday after your last child leaves home, and how odd it is making one cup of tea instead of four. Lucy felt like Wendy had been watching her, so she clicked the link.

She wants more of the same, from the person who wrote that post. The link took her to the homepage, so that's where she starts.

Straight away, the website sounds like a different person. The post was warm, specific and a bit funny. The website says "Find your purpose, your passion and your confidence", which sounds like a greetings card. There's nothing about that Monday morning feeling anywhere. She goes back to Facebook and carries on scrolling.

This catches a lot of coaches out. Your posts are often better than your website, because you write them quickly, in your own voice, about something real. So make the homepage sound like your posts, and link each post to a page that carries on the same subject, with a next step at the bottom.

## Visitor 4: the person coming back after the freebie

**Denise** signed up to Wendy's newsletter a couple of months ago. She's liked the emails, and now she thinks she might want some help. She goes back to the website.

She's had the free bit, so now she wants to know what the paid bit is, how it works and roughly what it costs. She heads to the Services page.

All it says is "Contact me for pricing." She has no idea whether it's £200 or £2,000. And nothing on the page connects to the emails. The newsletter covers all sorts, from journalling to sleep to travel, so she can't see how the coaching follows on from it.

A newsletter is a weak freebie anyway, because nobody really wants more emails. Something small that helps with the exact problem your coaching solves works better. For Wendy, that could be a short guide called "The first three months after they leave", with each email after it moving a step closer to what working with her looks like. There are plenty more ideas in [this post on lead magnets for coaches](/blog/lead-magnet-ideas-for-coaches).

## Visitor 5: the person ready to buy

**Raj** has made up his mind. Since his divorce he's felt stuck, and he wants a coach. He's down to two names, Wendy is one of them, and he's going to pick tonight.

He wants the practical stuff: the price, how long it lasts, what he gets, whether it's worked for anyone like him, and how to book. He checks Services, then the testimonials, then the button.

Three things put him off, one after another. There's no price, and asking for one feels awkward. When we checked those 10,954 websites, more than 8 in 10 showed no price at all, so Wendy's far from alone, but that's no comfort to Raj. The testimonial "Wendy is amazing!" from S.T. tells him nothing about what changed for S.T. And "Book a free discovery call" opens a contact form saying Wendy will reply within five working days.

The other coach has a price, a package that says what's included and a calendar where he can pick a time tonight. Raj books with them. Somebody who's ready to buy doesn't want persuading, they just want you to make it easy.

## The fixed page order

Wendy's website doesn't need a new design. The problem is the order things are in and the words she's used. Here's her homepage put in an order that gives each visitor what they came for. The wording is made up, like Wendy.

1. **A headline that names who it's for.** "For women whose children have left home, and who are wondering what's next for them." Alison knows straight away she's in the right place.
2. **One line on what changes, a photo of Wendy and a button.** "I'll help you work out what you want the next twenty years to look like, and start doing it." It's her own smiling face, not a sunset. The button says "Book a 20-minute call".
3. **"Does this sound like you?"** A few short lines in her clients' words. "The house feels too quiet." "I spent years looking after everyone and I've forgotten what I like." This is where Lucy hears the voice she heard on Facebook.
4. **How it works.** How many weeks, how often they meet, video or in person, what's included and the price, or at least a starting price. Denise and Raj get their answer.
5. **Proof from people like them.** Testimonials with a first name, their situation and what changed. "My daughter moved to Leeds in August. By Christmas I'd started a pottery course and booked my first holiday on my own. Carol, 54." Always get the client's permission first.
6. **Wendy's own story, kept short.** Her two children left home within a year of each other, and she was lost for a while. That's why she does this. Banking can be one line on the About page.
7. **A free first step.** The short guide, for anyone not ready to book yet.
8. **The booking calendar.** So Raj, or Gary's wife, can pick a time today without waiting for an email.

## FAQ

### What should a life coach put on their website?

Who you help and what changes for them, right at the top. Then a photo of you, a few lines in your clients' own words, how your coaching works and what it costs, specific testimonials, a short version of your story and an easy way to book.

### Should a life coach pick a niche for their website?

You don't have to turn anyone away, but your homepage should speak to one group of people. Life coaching covers so many different problems that a headline written for everyone means nothing to anyone. Look at who your best clients have been and write the top of the page for them.

### Should a life coach show prices on their website?

In my opinion, yes, or at least a starting price. People who are ready to buy want to know you're in their budget before they get in touch, and plenty of them won't ask. If every client's work really is different, say what the price depends on.
