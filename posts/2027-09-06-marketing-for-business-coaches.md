Good marketing for business coaches starts with picking one kind of business owner. Then you build offers that fit where that owner is, describe their problem in the words they use themselves, and keep turning up in the same places with proof that you've helped people like them. That means dropping the discounts, the deal sites and the marketing that talks to every owner at once, and putting the effort into offers and words that make the right owner think "that's me".

![A business coach giving a short talk to four business owners round a table](marketing-for-business-coaches.jpg)

From 2007 to 2019 I did marketing for driving schools. It's a long way from coaching, I know, but a couple of the lessons from those years fit business coaches almost exactly.

## Stop doing these

### Stop discounting and stop using deal sites

A discount teaches people to choose you on price, and once they're choosing on price, they'll keep comparing you with whoever is cheaper.

With driving schools, one of the things that worked best was taking the special offers off the instructor's car. The reason is simple. People looking for a driving school don't know how to choose one. It's always a gamble for them, so they often choose on price. Once they knew the school's price from the car, they could phone another school to get its price, and now they were talking to that school instead.

The biggest waste of money was voucher sites like Wowcher. They were too cheap, the schools ran at a massive loss, and the schools took on too many pupils.

Business owners are the same. They don't really know how to tell one business coach from another, so if the first thing they see is "half price for your first month", price is what they'll compare. And if you fill your diary with cut-price clients, you've got no room left for the ones paying properly.

Picture a made-up coach who runs a "summer special" on LinkedIn at half price. The enquiries come in, but the people asking are mainly checking whether the offer can be extended. That coach has found people shopping for a bargain, and very few of them will still be around at full price.

### Stop talking to everyone

If your marketing is written for any business owner, none of them will think it's about them.

You can see this on coaching websites. When we checked thousands of them, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with. A business owner can't tell in a few seconds whether you're for them, so they leave and look at the next one.

"I help business owners grow" could be written by anybody. Compare it with this made-up line: "I help people running a trades business with five to twenty staff who are still doing all the quoting themselves at night." A plumbing firm owner reads that and thinks, that's me. An accountant reads it and moves on, and that's fine, because they were never going to hire you anyway. If you're not sure which owner to pick, [our guide to business coaching niches](/blog/business-coaching-niches) goes through nine kinds of owner.

### Stop using coaching words owners never say

Owners don't describe their problems the way coaches do, so coaching words make you sound like every other coach.

Words like "clarity" and "abundance" are the usual ones. Clarity sounds good, but it doesn't tell an owner what you'll actually do with them. A business owner doesn't wake up wanting clarity. They wake up wanting to know why they're working every evening and still can't pay themselves properly. In my opinion, the more a word gets used by every coach, the easier it is for an owner to scroll past you.

Swap "Get clarity on your business vision" for something like "Find out which of your jobs actually make you money". The second one is plain and specific, and an owner can picture it.

### Stop making it all about you

An owner reading your page wants to know if it's for them. Your story comes second.

A lot of business coaches open with their own story, their certificates and their values. Some of that is useful later on. But if the top of your page is about you, the owner has to dig to find themselves in it, and plenty won't bother.

Move "My name is ... and I'm passionate about helping businesses thrive" further down. Put the owner, their problem and what changes for them at the top instead. [What business owners check on a business coach website](/blog/business-coach-website) shows how to lay that out.

## Start doing these

### Start building offers for each type of client

Owners at different stages need different things, and an offer built for their stage sells itself far better than a discount ever will.

When I took the special offers off driving schools, the obvious question was what to do instead. I turned lessons into products designed for each type of pupil, so there was something for beginners, something for part-trained pupils and so on. A beginner and a part-trained pupil want different things.

For a business coach, that means stopping selling "coaching" as one block of hours and building an offer for each type of owner you work with. The solo owner who's overwhelmed needs something different from the owner taking on their first staff, and different again from the owner thinking about selling up.

Here's a made-up set of offers for a coach working with small service businesses. One is a single session for the solo owner who wants to sort out their week. Another is a three-month programme for the owner hiring their first two people. The third is a longer arrangement for the owner who wants to step back from the day-to-day. Each one has its own name, its own page and its own price, and each owner can find theirs. [How to build coaching packages](/blog/coaching-packages) goes through building one step by step.

### Start using the owner's own words

When an owner sees their own words on your page, they feel understood before you've even spoken to them.

In our buying research we found 126 books written for small business owners and people working for themselves, which tells you plenty of them go looking for help. The questions they ask online show you exactly how they talk. One person asked:

> "Is it common to feel overwhelmed as solopreneur starting their first business or does it mean I'm not cut out to be an entrepreneur?"

Another simply asked "anyone else is feeling exhausted by December?". And when they say what they want, it's things like "enjoy being your own boss again". There's no mention of a vision or a roadmap anywhere. They're tired, they're doubting themselves and they want to like their business again.

A made-up headline built from those words might read "Exhausted by December? You're not failing, you're doing every job in the business yourself." That's the owner's language played back to them, and it will get more attention than anything a coach would write from scratch.

### Start being clear about the experience behind you

Business owners want to know where you've come from, and a vague answer loses them.

Plenty of owners will want to know whether you've ever run a business. If you have, say what it was. If you were senior in someone else's business, or you specialised in sales or finance, say that instead, in a sentence or two near the top of your page. Don't wait to be asked, and don't dress it up.

A made-up line like "I ran the operations side of a regional transport firm for nine years, so I know what it's like when the wage bill's due and a big customer pays late" tells an owner far more than "15 years of business experience". If you're still working out which route you're coming from, [how to become a business coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-business-coach) covers the five common ones.

## Keep doing these

### Keep being seen in the same places

Owners need to see you again and again before they trust you, so staying visible counts for a lot.

With driving schools, the other thing that worked best was putting the school's name and colours all over the instructor's car, so people saw the same car around town again and again.

For a business coach, that means being seen again and again in the same places your owners already go, with the same name, the same face and the same plain line about who you help. In my opinion, owners often take their time before they hire a coach, so a burst of activity followed by silence does very little. Here are the places worth working, and one thing to do in each:

- **Local networking breakfasts and your chamber of commerce.** Go every month, not once. Sit with different people each time and ask about their business rather than pitching yours.
- **Trade association groups.** If you've picked trades owners, join the groups and events for that trade. Offer to give a short talk on one problem their members all have, like chasing late payments or finding staff.
- **LinkedIn.** Write one useful post a week aimed at the one kind of owner you've picked, using their words. Reply properly to the comments, because that's where the conversations start.
- **Facebook groups for small business owners.** Answer questions in plain language and don't drop links to your offer. When someone wants more, they'll message you.

Pick two of these and stick with them for a year. That will do more for you than trying all four for a month.

### Keep showing proof

In our buying research, seeing proof from other people helps the decision for more than 9 in 10 types of coaching buyer. Business owners are no different.

If you've got testimonials, results or a short story of an owner you've helped, keep them on your page and keep adding to them. Ask each client at the end of your work together what changed for them, in their own words, and get their permission to use it.

"Great coach, really helpful" tells an owner nothing. A line like "I stopped working Sundays and paid myself properly for the first time in three years" tells them exactly what they could get. That second line is made up, but it's the kind of thing to ask your clients for.

### Keep talking to owners directly

Owners hire people they've spoken to and trust, and a real conversation does more than any ad.

Whatever else you change, keep picking up the phone, replying properly to messages and meeting owners for a coffee. Ask them about their business and listen to how they describe it. Every one of those conversations gives you more of the owner's words to use in your marketing, which feeds straight back into everything above.

After every call with an owner, write down two or three phrases they used to describe their problem. After a couple of months you'll have a page of real words, and some of them will end up as headlines.

## FAQ

### What is the best marketing for business coaches?

Pick one kind of business owner, build offers for each stage that owner might be at, and describe their problem in their own words. Then keep showing up where those owners already are, and keep showing proof that you've helped people like them.

### Should a business coach offer discounts?

In my opinion, no. A discount teaches owners to choose you on price, and they'll compare you with the next coach on price too. Build an offer that fits their situation instead, so they're comparing what they get.

### Do business coaches need to run ads?

No, you can get clients without them. If you do run ads, write them for one kind of owner at one stage, using the words that owner uses. A general ad for "business owners" is easy to scroll past.

### How do I know which business owners to aim my marketing at?

Start with the owners your background fits best, and the ones whose problems you understand from the inside. If you've run a trades business, other trades owners are a natural first choice.
