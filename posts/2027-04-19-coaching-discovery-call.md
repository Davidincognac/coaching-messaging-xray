A good coaching discovery call follows a simple order. You spend the first few minutes making the person comfortable, the next twenty or so asking questions and listening, then you explain how you'd help, say the price plainly and agree what happens next. Below is a 30-minute run sheet you can use, block by block, with what you do and what the person on the other end is probably thinking while you do it.

![A man on the phone at his kitchen table with a list of questions in a notebook](coaching-discovery-call-phone.jpg)

I've put the client's side in on purpose. When we checked 10,954 coaching websites, nearly all of them asked a stranger to take a next step, like booking a call. If yours asks for a call, that call is where the sale happens, and in my experience the person on that call is often nervous, a bit embarrassed and expecting to be sold to. If you know what's going on in their head, you'll handle it much better.

I'll use a made-up example to show it. Jess is an invented confidence coach, and Martin is an invented 44-year-old who's booked a call with her.

## Before the call: what to put on your booking page

In my experience, people who book and don't turn up have usually got nervous, forgotten, or weren't sure what they'd signed up for. Your booking page can sort out a lot of that by telling them four things.

1. **What the call is.** "A 30-minute chat on Zoom to talk about what's going on for you and whether I can help."
2. **How long it is.** Say 30 minutes and stick to it. People with busy lives want to know when it ends.
3. **That it isn't a hard sell.** Say it in plain words, like this: "If I think I can help, I'll tell you how and what it costs. If I don't, I'll tell you that too, and maybe point you to someone who can. Either way, there's no pushing."
4. **What to do beforehand.** One question is enough, like "What made you want to book this call today?" It gives you a head start.

Then send a reminder the day before and another an hour before, with the link in both.

Here's what Martin thought when he read Jess's booking page: "Thirty minutes, no hard sell, and she'll tell me if she can't help. Alright, I can do that." If your page doesn't get that reaction, rewrite it until it does.

## 0 to 3 minutes: settle them in

**What the coach does.** Jess says hello, thanks Martin for booking and checks he can hear her properly. Then she tells him what's going to happen:

> "So you know how this works, I'll ask you a few questions about what's going on, then if I think I can help I'll explain how and what it costs. If I can't, I'll say so. We'll be done by half past. Does that sound alright?"

**What Martin is thinking.** "I hope this isn't going to be weird. What if she asks me something I don't want to answer? How long before she tries to sell me something?"

Telling him the plan answers those worries in about twenty seconds. He knows when it ends, that the price is coming and that he's allowed to say no.

## 3 to 12 minutes: find out what's going on

**What the coach does.** Jess asks open questions and mostly listens. These are the ones I'd use:

- "What made you want to book a call now, rather than six months ago?"
- "What's going on at the moment?"
- "Can you give me an example of when that happened recently?"
- "How does that affect the rest of your week?"

She writes down the exact words Martin uses. When he says "I just sit in meetings and say nothing, then I'm furious with myself on the drive home", she writes that down word for word.

**What Martin is thinking.** "I've never said this out loud to anyone. Is she judging me? Does she think this is a bit pathetic?"

Notice what's not on that list. No "what's your biggest challenge?" and no "what keeps you up at 3am?". I can't stand those questions. When a coach asks me one, I know they're reading from a script, and I make my exit, because that's not coaching. Your questions should come from what the person has just told you, so they feel listened to rather than processed.

This is where people either open up or close down, and it depends on you. Don't jump in with advice, just ask the next question. If they go quiet, wait, because they're usually working out how to say what's really bothering them.

## 12 to 18 minutes: what they've tried and what they want

**What the coach does.** Now Jess asks about the past and the future:

- "What have you already tried?"
- "What happened with that?"
- "If nothing changes in the next year, what does that look like?"
- "If this worked, what would be different? What would you notice first?"
- "Is anything likely to get in the way, like time, money or someone else who needs to agree?"

If there's a partner who needs to agree, or money is very tight, you want to know now, not after you've said the price.

**What Martin is thinking.** "I've read the books. I did a course once. None of it stuck. What makes this any different?" And then, when she asks what would be different: "Actually, I'd quite like to put my hand up in a meeting and not feel sick."

When Martin describes what life would look like if it worked, he's picturing it, and that does more selling than anything Jess could say.

This is also the point where you'll find out if it's something you can't help with. If someone describes something that sounds like a mental health problem or a medical issue, tell them kindly that they need a doctor or a therapist first. If they say anything that makes you worried about their safety right now, stop the sales part of the call and gently encourage them to contact their GP or doctor straight away, or emergency services if it's urgent. Coaches don't diagnose or treat anything, and pretending otherwise isn't safe for them or for you.

## 18 to 22 minutes: say back what you heard and how you'd help

**What the coach does.** Jess sums up what Martin told her, using his words, then explains how she works:

> "So from what you've said, you sit in meetings with things to say and don't say them, and you're furious with yourself on the way home. You've read the books and they didn't stick. What you want is to speak up without feeling sick. Have I got that right?"

Then, once he says yes:

> "The way I'd help is twelve weekly calls. We'd start with what's going on in your head before a meeting, then we'd practise what you'd actually say, and each week you'd try one small thing at work and we'd talk about how it went."

**What Martin is thinking.** "She actually listened. That's exactly it." And then straight after, "Right, here comes the price."

Summing up in their words is the strongest thing you'll do on the whole call. Keep the explanation of how you work short, two or three plain sentences is plenty. If you need longer than that, your offer probably isn't clear yet, and [how to put together a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) will help you sort it.

## 22 to 26 minutes: say the price

**What the coach does.** Jess says it plainly, once, and then stops talking:

> "It's twelve weekly calls over three months, and it's £1,200. You can pay that in one go or in three monthly payments of £400."

That £1,200 is a made-up figure for this example, not what I'm telling you to charge. If you're unsure what to charge, read [how to price your coaching](/blog/coaching-pricing) first.

**What Martin is thinking.** "Hmm, that's more than I was hoping. Can I justify that? What would my wife say?"

This is why you leave a silence. He's doing sums in his head, and if you start talking again, offering a discount or explaining why it's worth it, you interrupt him.

More than 8 in 10 coaching websites we checked show no price at all. That means a lot of people arrive on a call with no idea what coaching costs, and the number is a shock whatever it is. Saying it clearly and calmly is the kindest thing you can do, and mumbling it or saying "it depends" only makes them more nervous.

## 26 to 30 minutes: end it, whatever the answer

A call can end three ways, so be ready for all of them.

**If it's a yes.** Don't keep selling. Jess says, "Brilliant. I'll send you an email in the next hour with the payment link and a few dates for our first call." Then she lets him go.

**If it's a no.** Thank them and mean it. "Thanks for being so open with me. I hope it goes well, and if anything changes, you know where I am." Don't argue. If you handle a no kindly, some of those people come back months later, or send a friend your way.

**If it's a maybe.** This is the one you'll get often. Martin says, "It sounds good, I just need to think about it." Jess asks, kindly, "Of course. Is it the money, the timing, or something about how it works? If it's something I can answer now, I'd rather you had the full picture." If he needs to talk to his wife, she offers to send something they can read together. Then she agrees a day to follow up: "Shall I drop you a line on Friday?"

**What Martin is thinking in all three.** "Please don't make this awkward." Whatever he says, your job is to make the end of the call feel easy.

There's more on handling "I need to think about it" in [how to sell coaching without feeling pushy](/blog/how-to-sell-coaching).

## After the call: the follow-up email

Send it the same day, while the call is fresh in both your heads. Here's the one Jess sends Martin after his maybe. Copy it and change it to suit you:

> Subject: Our chat today
>
> Hi Martin,
>
> Thanks for talking to me today. I know it's not easy saying that stuff out loud to someone you've just met.
>
> Here's what I heard: you've got things to say in meetings and you're not saying them, and you'd like to speak up without feeling sick beforehand.
>
> Here's what I'd suggest: twelve weekly calls over three months. Each week we'd look at one meeting, work on what you'd say and how to get past the nerves, and you'd try one small thing at work. It's £1,200, paid in one go or three monthly payments of £400.
>
> If you'd like to go ahead, just reply and I'll send the details. If you've got questions, reply with those instead. And if it's not for you right now, that's completely fine, just let me know and I won't keep emailing.
>
> I'll drop you a line on Friday as we agreed.
>
> Jess

For a yes, keep the first two paragraphs and swap the rest for the payment link and dates. For a no, just thank them and wish them well.

The email uses Martin's own words, so if he forwards it to his wife, she reads what he actually said rather than a sales pitch. It also makes it easy for him to say no, and in my opinion people are more likely to reply when they don't feel trapped.

If you hear nothing after the Friday message, send one more a week or so later, then leave it.

## FAQ

### How long should a coaching discovery call be?

Thirty minutes is plenty. It's long enough to listen properly and short enough that busy people will book it. Say the length on your booking page and finish on time.

### Should I say the price on a discovery call?

Yes. If you can help them, say the price on the call, plainly, and then be quiet while they think. Leaving it for a later email can leave people wondering, and some will lose interest.

### What if someone doesn't turn up?

Send one short, friendly message offering another time. Then look at your booking page. If it doesn't say what the call is, how long it lasts and that it isn't a hard sell, fix that first.

### Should I call it a discovery call?

Call it whatever a stranger would understand. Not everyone knows what "discovery call" means, so something plain like "a 30-minute chat to see if I can help" usually works better.
