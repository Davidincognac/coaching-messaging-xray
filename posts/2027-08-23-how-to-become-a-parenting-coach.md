To become a parenting coach, you need proper coach training with plenty of practice, a clear idea of what a coach does and doesn't do with families, a plan for what happens if a child might be at risk, insurance, and one group of parents you understand well. There's no licence to get, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. You don't need a psychology degree and you don't need to be a perfect parent, but you do need to know where your job stops.

![A tired dad on a laptop at a kitchen table covered in children's drawings](how-to-become-a-parenting-coach.jpg)

Say you want to do this and everyone's got an opinion, your mates, your mum over Sunday dinner, and you at 2am when you can't sleep. Some of what they say is rubbish, but each worry points at something you do need to sort out.

## What your friends will say

### You don't need a psychology degree

Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so there's no exam you legally have to pass. What a degree won't teach you is how to coach, and that's the skill a parent is paying for.

Get real coach training, with lots of practice on real people and someone experienced giving you feedback. Before you pay for any course, ask how much observed practice it includes. Then find a supervisor. That's an experienced coach you can talk to about your clients, in confidence.

Be careful what you call yourself. In the UK, titles like "clinical psychologist", "counselling psychologist" and "practitioner psychologist" are protected by law, and only people registered with the HCPC (the Health and Care Professions Council) can use them. "Coach" isn't protected. Rules are different in each country and US state, so check the rules where you live.

And whatever training you do, you don't diagnose children. If a parent thinks their child might have ADHD or autism, or might be depressed or anxious, that's for their GP, the school or a specialist. You can help the parent cope while they wait, but you never tell them what's wrong.

### Coaching isn't telling people how to raise their kids

For me, coaching and teaching are two different things. A coach listens, asks questions and helps someone work out what they want and what's stopping them. A teacher presents information and helps the person understand it. If you mainly hand out rules for bedtime and screen time, you're teaching, so call it that.

Our research found a parent who'd tried a list called "How to Stop Yelling at Your Kids: 3 Easy Steps!" and still couldn't stop. When the tips don't stick, parents end up blaming themselves rather than the tips. A coach helps the parent work out what sets them off and what they'll do differently on Tuesday at teatime. So a parenting coach helps a parent with their own goals, habits, stress and confidence, and helps them stick to changes they've chosen.

What a parenting coach doesn't do is therapy, so you don't work on deep trauma, depression or a parent's mental illness, and if a parent is struggling day after day they should see their GP or doctor. You don't diagnose children, you don't give medical advice about a baby's sleep or feeding, and you don't give legal advice about custody or contact.

### Health visitors do a different job

In the UK, health visitors are qualified nurses or midwives, usually working within the NHS, who support families with young children, and family support workers often work through councils or charities. In the US, a family's paediatrician covers a lot of the same health questions.

Their job isn't the same as yours, though. A health visitor is there for the child's health and development. If a parent's worry is about a baby's sleep, weight or feeding, that's for the health visitor or GP first, not you. One new parent in our research wrote "I don't sleep at all because he cries inconsolably if I leave him in the crib, I'm exhausted". You can help that parent with the exhaustion, the arguments with their partner and asking for help from family. Safe sleep advice belongs with the health visitor or doctor, and you work alongside them.

## What your family will say

### Nobody needs their coach to be a perfect parent

Your family might point out you're not a perfect parent yourself. They're right, and neither is anyone else. In our research, one parent wrote "I feel like I'm constantly yelling at my kids and I don't want to be that parent. What can I do?" Another wrote "I feel like a failure as a parent". These people are already drowning in guilt. Someone who seems to have it all sorted just makes them feel worse.

One parent of a teenager put it bluntly: "Please don't give me the I didn't raise her right speech. My other three children do [fine]". That's someone who's had enough of being judged, and they want a coach who's on their side.

What you do need is to keep your own family out of your sessions. If a client's teenager reminds you of a row with your own, notice it and park it.

### You can set it up as a proper business

"It's a nice idea, but it's not a real job" is one you'll hear. In the UK you can start as a sole trader by registering with HMRC (the UK tax office). In the US, people usually start as a sole proprietor, but each state has its own rules.

Get professional indemnity insurance, which covers you if a client says your coaching caused them harm or cost them money, and public liability if you meet families in person.

Some of what parents tell you about their children will be health information, which UK data protection law treats with extra care. You may need to pay a small yearly fee to the ICO (the UK's data protection regulator), so check, and keep notes secure.

Write a simple client agreement that says what coaching is, what it isn't, and what you'll do if a child may be at risk. If you're still deciding whether coaching is right for you at all, [how to become a life coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-life-coach) helps with that first, and [how to start a coaching business](/blog/how-to-start-a-coaching-business) goes through the setting up step by step.

## What your own head will say

### Parents do pay, if you help them change things

Some parents won't pay for coaching, and that's fine. You only need enough of the ones who will.

We found 172 books written for parents, plus another 46 just on parenting teenagers and 30 for parents of children with special needs. People buy those books because they want something to change. A book is cheap and a coach isn't, so what does a parent get from you that they can't get from page 40? What they get is someone who helps them actually do it, week after week, with their own child.

A short package aimed at one problem, like getting a teenager talking again, is easier to say yes to than open-ended sessions. [Our guide to coaching packages](/blog/coaching-packages) shows how to put one together.

### There are loads of parenting coaches, but a lot sound the same

There are plenty, and in my opinion a lot of them sound exactly the same. When we checked coaching websites, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with. "I help parents" isn't enough, because a parent of a newborn and a parent of a 15-year-old have nothing in common except the word parent.

The other problem is how coaches talk. Here's some of the coaching language we found aimed at parents of teenagers: "co-regulation involves external support from a trusted caregiver through attuned responses, active listening, and empathetic validation". Now here's how a parent of a teenager actually described it: "I feel like I'm losing my daughter. We used to be so close... I'm heartbroken and she doesn't even notice." Which of those would make you pick up the phone? So pick one group of parents, learn how they talk, and use their words.

- **Parents of teenagers.** We found 46 books just on parenting teenagers. One parent wrote "My teen son never talks to me, what should I do?" Another had tried the usual: "He was grounded and had his phone taken away. What else should be done here?"
- **New parents.** We found 15 books on newborns and baby sleep. The voice here is pure exhaustion: "I feel like crying due to lack of sleep/energy".
- **Single parents.** There's not much written for them. We found just one book on single parenting among the parenting books, and 8 on solo parents among books for single people. Someone in the single dads group asked "Why do I feel so overwhelmed as a single parent when it looks so easy for everyone around me?"
- **Parents of neurodivergent children.** We found 15 books on parenting autistic children and 30 in special-needs parenting overall. One parent wrote "I have an autistic daughter and I want to be the parent who understands her world." If you choose this group, be extra clear that you don't diagnose and don't replace the school, the GP or specialists.

[How to choose a coaching niche](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) goes through picking a group properly.

## Have a plan for when a child might be at risk

One day a parent will tell you something that worries you, so decide now what you'll do. If anything a parent says makes you think a child may be at risk of harm, it goes to the police or children's services, never into a coaching plan. In the UK, that means the local council's children's services, or 999 if a child is in danger right now. In the US, it means child protective services, or 911 in an emergency. Some US states require anyone who suspects child abuse to report it, so check the rules where you live. Put this in your client agreement and say it in your first session, so parents know before they start.

The same goes for the parent. If they talk about harming themselves, or if they're being abused at home, point them to emergency help if they could be in danger now, or to their doctor or a domestic abuse helpline straight away.

You'll also hear about DBS checks. These are criminal record checks in England and Wales. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own versions. As a self-employed person you can apply for a basic check yourself, but the higher levels are only available for certain kinds of work and have to be requested by an organisation. Whether you need one depends on what you do and who with, so check the rules on the [Disclosure and Barring Service pages on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/disclosure-and-barring-service). In the US, background checks vary by state.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a parenting coach?

Not legally. Coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. Proper training with real practice and feedback is still worth doing, and parents may ask what training you've had.

### Can a parenting coach diagnose ADHD or autism in a child?

No. Only qualified health professionals can diagnose those. If a parent thinks their child might have ADHD or autism, they should talk to their GP, health visitor, the school or a specialist.

### What's the difference between a parenting coach and a family therapist?

A therapist works with mental health, trauma and deep family problems, and in the US therapists are licensed by each state. A parenting coach helps a parent with their own goals, habits and confidence, and refers them on when something needs more than coaching.

### Do I need a DBS check to be a parenting coach?

It depends on what you do and who with. If you only coach parents online, it may not apply. If you work with families in person, check the current rules on GOV.UK, or the rules in your state if you're in the US.
