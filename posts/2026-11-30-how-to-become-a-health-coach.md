Plenty of people come to health coaching because something changed their own life. They sorted out their own gut, lost the weight and kept it off, got through the menopause without losing their mind, or came back from burnout. They want to help other people do the same. I think that's a brilliant reason to do it, and it's also one of the best things you'll have going for you.

But I'll be honest with you from the start. Health coaching is one of the busiest areas in the whole of coaching, and the people already in it aren't doing a great job of standing out. When we looked at 10,954 live coaching websites, 622 of them belonged to health, nutrition, wellness or fitness coaches. We checked each homepage for 10 things a stranger needs before they'll get in touch, and not one of those 622 did well enough to count as strong. Not one.

That's not a reason to give up. It's a reason to do it differently from the start. So rather than tell you which course to take, which plenty of other people will do, here are the five questions I'd want answered before I became a health coach.

![A health coach listening to a client at a kitchen table, notebook open](health-coach-kitchen-table.jpg)

## Is health coaching worth getting into?

Yes, if you go in with your eyes open. People spend a lot of money on their health, and plenty of them are fed up with being rushed through a ten-minute appointment. There's a real need for someone who'll listen and help them change how they live.

The catch is that a lot of your future clients have tried things already. Diets, apps, programmes, supplements, a personal trainer, a book their friend swore by. They're not short of advice. They're short of something that's worked for them. So whatever you do, you'll be talking to people who've heard the usual promises before, and you'll need to sound different from the start.

## Question 1: What kind of health coach are you going to be?

"Health coach" isn't one job. When we looked at the books people buy about their health and what they write about it, we found at least eleven different reasons people go looking for help, and they want very different things. Some are crowded and some are much quieter.

**The crowded ones.** Weight and fitness had 151 books in our research, more than any other health subject. And you can hear how tired people are. They write things like "eating less but can't lose" and "tired of suffering to keep it off". Chronic illness and chronic pain had 63 books, and the people in it are worried this will be one more thing that doesn't believe them. Longevity and healthy ageing had 57.

**The middle ones.** Gut health and nutrition had 50 books, from people writing "my stomach is getting worse every day and I don't know the cause". The menopause had 40, from women dealing with "exhaustion, brain fog, rage" and worried their doctor won't take them seriously.

**The quieter ones.** Life after cancer had 33 books, from people stuck in the "what happens after chemo" limbo. Sleep had 32, from people who are "exhausted all day" and wake up just as tired. Men's health had 28, from men who feel tired, low and "old" even though their test results came back normal. Caring for ageing parents had 19, from people for whom caring has cost them their marriage, their home or their sense of who they are.

A crowded subject means plenty of people want help, but they've heard everything before, so you need a real reason to be picked. A quiet subject means fewer people looking, but less competition and often far less help aimed at them. Neither is better on its own. The question is which group you understand best, and where you've got something to say that they haven't already heard.

If you want to go further into how crowded each area is, [the most profitable coaching niches post](/blog/most-profitable-coaching-niches) looks at it across all of coaching, not just health.

## Question 2: What are you allowed to say?

This is the one health coaches need to think about more than any other kind of coach. You're working with people's bodies, and some of them will have things going on that need a doctor, not a coach.

Coaching itself isn't regulated in the UK or the US, so you can call yourself a health coach without a licence. But some titles are protected. Dietitian is one, in both countries, so check what you can and can't call yourself where you live before you put it on a website. And whatever your training, a health coach doesn't diagnose or treat medical conditions. Your job is to help people change how they live, alongside whatever their doctor is doing, not instead of it.

That's not a limit on your business. It's actually a selling point if you say it well. Look at those worries again. People with chronic illness are scared of being dismissed. Women in the menopause worry their doctor won't take them seriously. Men worry they'll be told their tests are normal and there's nothing to fix. A health coach who says "I'll work alongside your doctor, and I'll actually listen" is offering something those people really want.

## Question 3: What will make people trust you?

When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That means the sense that you've seen their problem before and know what to do about it. A certificate is the weakest version of that. Having been through the exact thing they're going through is the strongest.

That's why, in health coaching, your own story is often the main reason someone picks you. If you sorted out your own gut after years of being told it was all in your head, say that. If you went through the menopause and came out the other side, say that. People don't want a health coach who read about their problem. They want one who's lived it or helped plenty of people through it.

Then add proof from your clients. Two thirds of the coaching websites we looked at showed no proof at all. A client saying "I've slept through the night for the first time in two years" is worth more than any qualification on your About page.

## Question 4: How will people find you?

At first, through people who already know you. Your first clients will almost certainly be friends, old colleagues, people from your gym or your school gate, people who saw what changed for you and want the same. [How to get your first coaching client](/blog/first-coaching-client) goes into that.

After that, your website starts to count, and this is where health coaches have a particular problem. When we compared the 622 health coaching sites with coaching sites overall, the biggest difference was how easy they were to understand in five seconds. Health coaching sites were harder to understand, and they were less clear about who they help.

The reason, in my opinion, is that health coaches pick up a lot of professional language in their training and then put it straight on the homepage. "Functional nutrition." "Root-cause approach." "Holistic wellness." It's how you were taught to think about the work, but it's not how the person reading talks about their life. They don't say "I need a root-cause approach". They say "my stomach is getting worse every day and nobody can tell me why". If your headline sounds like the second one, they'll stop and read. [The health coach websites post](/blog/health-coach-websites-scored) goes into this in more detail.

## Question 5: What will you actually sell?

Not sessions. Nobody wants "six health coaching sessions". They want to stop feeling exhausted, or get their stomach back to normal, or lose the weight without suffering for it. So sell a package with a clear result, and say exactly what's in it. [The coaching offer post](/blog/coaching-offer) shows you how to set that out.

There's some good news here. Health coaching websites were better than coaching sites overall at showing a price and at offering visitors something in return for their email address. So the health coaches who are already out there have got some of the basics right. You need to get those right too, and then win on being clear about who you help. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) covers whether to show your price and how to make either choice work.

## What does this look like for three different people?

Here are three made-up people thinking about becoming health coaches, to show how the five questions play out. Your answers will be different, but the thinking is the same.

**The nurse who wants out of the NHS.** She's spent twenty years watching people with long-term conditions get rushed through appointments. She picks chronic illness, because she knows the system and she knows how dismissed people feel in it. She's careful to say she works alongside doctors, not instead of them, and her years on the wards are her proof. Her one sentence: "I help people living with chronic pain who feel like nobody's listening to work out what actually makes their days better."

**The personal trainer who's seen it all.** He's spent ten years training people who lose weight and then put it back on. He knows weight and fitness is the most crowded area of all, so he needs a different angle, and he's got one: what happens after the weight comes off. His one sentence: "I help people who've lost the weight before and put it back on to keep it off this time, without living on salad."

**The woman who sorted out her own gut.** She spent years being told her stomach problems were stress, until she worked out what was going on for herself. She picks gut health, a middle-sized area where people are scared and confused about who to trust. Her own story is her biggest asset. Her one sentence: "I help people whose stomach is getting worse and who've been told it's just stress to work out what's actually setting it off."

Notice that none of them say "holistic wellness". Each one is talking to one type of person, about one problem, in that person's own words.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a health coach?

Not by law, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. Training will make you better and safer at the job, so in my opinion it's worth doing. But check which titles are protected where you live, and remember that clients are more interested in your experience and their results than in your certificate.

### Can a health coach give medical or nutrition advice?

A health coach doesn't diagnose or treat medical conditions. What you can do depends on your training and where you live, so check before you offer anything that sounds like treatment. Working alongside someone's doctor, and saying so on your website, builds trust rather than losing it.

### Is health coaching too crowded to get into?

Some areas are very crowded, like weight and fitness, and others are much quieter, like life after cancer or caring for ageing parents. Crowded doesn't mean impossible. It means you need a clear reason for someone to pick you, said in their words.
