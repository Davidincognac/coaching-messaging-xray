The best business coaching niches are built around one kind of owner at one stage of their business, rather than business owners in general. A sole trader drowning in admin and a founder whose company has just gone past ten staff both need help, but they're stuck on completely different things, they spend their time in different places, and they'd want a very different first conversation with you. Below are nine kinds of business owner, with what keeps each one stuck (in their own words where we have them), where you'll find them and the first conversation I'd have with them.

![A business coach talking with a bakery owner across her counter](business-coaching-niches.jpg)

## Why "I help business owners" doesn't work

I understand why coaches say it. Business coaching already feels like a niche, and narrowing it down again feels like turning money away. The trouble is that a café owner reading "I help business owners grow" doesn't think "that's me". They think it could be anyone, and they're right.

When we checked nearly 11,000 coaching websites, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with. If you're still deciding who to pick, [how to choose a coaching niche](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) takes you through it step by step, and this guide gives you the business owners to choose from.

The quotes below come from what business owners write about their problems online, which we gathered as part of our research. I've copied them exactly, spelling and all. The book counts tell you how many books we found written for each group, which is a rough guide to how crowded it is. Where we didn't find quotes for a group, I've said so rather than make any up.

For each owner I've given you a first conversation rather than a pitch, because the questions you ask show whether you understand their business. If you can ask a café owner a couple of questions that show you know how a café actually makes its money, you've done more selling than any brochure could. When it turns into a proper call, [how to run a coaching discovery call](/blog/coaching-discovery-call) picks it up from there.

## The field guide

### 1. The sole trader drowning in admin

This is the person doing the work, the invoices, the marketing, the emails and the tax return, all on their own. The phrase that keeps coming up in our research is "wearing all the hats". What they're afraid of is that feeling this overwhelmed means they're just not cut out for running a business. We found 21 books for service-based businesses and 10 on small business finance and bookkeeping, so this group already has plenty written for it.

Accountants are the best way in here, because they see the mess at every tax deadline. Early-morning networking breakfasts and local Facebook groups for self-employed people are worth your time too.

For the first conversation, ask them to walk you through last week, day by day. Then ask which job they'd hand over tomorrow if they could. They may never have said that out loud to anyone.

### 2. The café or shop owner

High street businesses depend on people walking through the door. We didn't find quotes from café owners as such, but a new business owner writing about marketing put it as "my new business has no customers or clients". Another said they had "relied on word of mouth referrals for a small business". We found only 7 books for local and high street businesses, and in my opinion that's good news for a coach who understands them.

The easiest way in is to walk in, buy a coffee and come back. Traders' associations and the local chamber of commerce are where the owners talk to each other, and when you do get chatting, ask where their last ten customers came from and what a quiet Tuesday looks like.

### 3. The trades business

This means plumbers, electricians, builders and roofers, where the owner is usually still doing the jobs themselves during the day, writing quotes in the evenings and chasing payments at the weekend. Our research didn't pick out trades as their own group, so I haven't got quotes from them. The nearest is owners trying to grow a team, where one asked "How do I scale my company without hiring too fast?". We found 14 books on hiring and team building and 9 on delegation and getting out of the day-to-day work.

You'll find them at builders' merchants first thing in the morning, through their trade bodies, and through the people who send them work, like estate agents and letting agents. I wouldn't start on LinkedIn with this group.

The question I'd ask first is what would happen if they broke their wrist next week. Who would do the quotes, who would do the jobs and who would chase the money? That one question shows them you understand the whole business rests on them.

### 4. The accountancy or professional firm

These are accountants, solicitors, architects and surveyors, often run by a handful of partners who sell their time by the hour. Again, we didn't find quotes from partners in these firms. The closest are consultants and freelancers, who asked "How do I stop trading my time for money?". We found 11 books on pricing and positioning for experts.

Their professional body's local events are a good place to meet them, along with LinkedIn and the other professionals they pass work to. I'd ask how they set their prices, and who in the firm brings the work in. If one partner brings in all the work, that's what you'd coach them on, getting the others selling too.

### 5. The solo consultant

This is someone who left a job to sell what they know. They wrote "What should I do if I really struggle with finding freelance clients?" and, on the bad days, "Should I quit freelancing and go back to a full time job?". Some of them do several things at once and can't pick, like the person who wrote "I'm a Jack of all trades, master of none. How do I figure out what fields to focus on?". We found 33 books for consultants and freelancers and 25 for portfolio and freelance careers.

LinkedIn is where a lot of them look for work, along with coworking spaces and online groups for freelancers and independent consultants.

Your first question should be who their last three paying clients were and how each one found them. You'll soon know whether the problem is what they're selling, who they're selling it to, or the fact that they never actually ask for the work.

### 6. The freelancer turning into an agency

This person has more work than they can do on their own and is nervous about the next step. They asked "How do I scale my business from a freelancer to an agency owner?" and "How do I scale my business with a small budget?". The worry our research picked up is hiring too soon, or not knowing what to hand over. We found 77 books on business systems and scaling, and 11 on scaling agencies and service businesses.

You'll find them in the same freelance groups as the solo consultant, asking about contractors and how to price bigger projects.

Start by asking what they'd do with the first ten hours a week they got back. If they can't answer that, the problem probably isn't hiring.

### 7. The founder scaling past ten staff

Somewhere around ten people, the founder can't be in every conversation any more. Founders wrote about being "constantly overwhelmed" and wanting to "get rid of that lonely feeling as a startup founder". A leader in our research described handing work over like this: "delegation feels like jumping out of an airplane and hoping the team is the parachute". The worry our research picked up is that admitting they're struggling will look weak to their team or their investors. We found 46 books for founder and executive coaching, 11 of them aimed at founders and CEOs who are scaling.

You'll find them on LinkedIn, at founder meetups, and through the accountants, lawyers and investors around them. This is also where bigger fees are, and [our guide to high-ticket coaching niches](/blog/high-ticket-coaching-niches) goes into that.

Start by asking which decisions still come to them that shouldn't. Then ask who they talk to when things are going badly. If the answer is nobody, that's probably why they want a coach.

### 8. The family business handing over

This is a parent getting ready to step back, a son or daughter taking over, and everyone else in the family with an opinion. We found only 3 books for family-owned businesses and 7 on succession, exit and life after the company, and no buyer quotes at all, so very few people are writing for or coaching this group yet. The legal and tax side belongs with their solicitor and accountant, and the rules are different in the UK and the US, so they should check the rules where they live. What a coach can help with is the conversations nobody in the family wants to start.

You'll find them through accountants and solicitors who handle succession, at local business awards, at the chamber of commerce, and through the younger generation on LinkedIn.

Start by asking each of them, separately, what the business looks like in five years. If the answers don't match, that's what the family needs help talking about.

### 9. Women running their own businesses

This one is about who the owner is rather than the trade. Women in our research wanted to build a business on their own terms without burning out or playing small. We found 4 books on women entrepreneurs and business building, and 7 for women and underrepresented founders and leaders. The quotes we have come from women writing about money and about themselves. One wanted to "become financially independent without doing a 9-to-5 job". Another wrote "I'm a people pleaser and I'm tired of it". I'm guessing here, but in a business I reckon that shows up as discounting, and as saying yes to clients you wish you'd turned down.

Women's business networks, local women in business groups, Facebook groups and LinkedIn are where you'll meet them. I'd ask what they've said yes to this month that they wish they hadn't. Then ask what they'd charge if they knew nobody would argue.

## Picking yours

Read back through the nine and notice which one you understood straight away. That's usually the one you've lived, worked alongside or sold to before, and it's the one where your questions will be sharpest. An owner can tell within a few minutes whether you get their kind of business.

Once you've picked, say it plainly at the top of your homepage, in the words that owner would use. [What business owners check on a business coach website](/blog/business-coach-website) shows you how to do that, with a full example.

## FAQ

### What are the best business coaching niches?

The best ones pair a kind of owner with a stage of business, like a sole trader drowning in admin, a freelancer turning into an agency, or a founder scaling past ten staff.

### Is business coaching too broad to be a niche?

On its own, yes. A café owner, a plumber and a startup founder are stuck on very different things, so "business coach" doesn't tell any of them it's for them. Narrow it to one kind of owner and one problem.

### Do I need to have run a business to be a business coach?

No law says you must, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. But business owners will ask what you've done, so be ready with real experience of their kind of business, even if it's from working inside one rather than owning it.

### Where do I find business owners to coach?

Go where that kind of owner already is. For a café, that's the high street and the traders' association. For a trades business, it's builders' merchants and trade bodies. For founders and consultants, it's LinkedIn and local meetups. Accountants are worth knowing for all of them.
