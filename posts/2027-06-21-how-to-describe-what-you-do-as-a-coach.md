To describe what you do as a coach, say who you help, what they come to you with, what you do about it and what changes for them, in the plain words the person in front of you would use. That's four short parts, and you can say them in one breath. Once you've got it, you can stretch it for a networking round or shrink it for a LinkedIn headline, and it's still the same answer underneath.

![A woman at a garden barbecue explaining what she does to four friends around the table](how-to-describe-what-you-do-as-a-coach.jpg)

## What I say at a barbecue

When someone at a barbecue asks me what I do, this is what I tell them: "CEOs and owners come to me for the truth about their marketing, and that's what I give them, and what they must do next. The outcome is more qualified leads and profit."

Have a look at what's in it. It says who comes to me (CEOs and owners), what they want (the truth about their marketing), what I do about it (give them that truth and tell them what to do next) and what they get at the end (more qualified leads and profit).

Now have a look at what isn't in it. There are no qualifications, no list of services, no clever name for how I work, and no word the person holding a burger would have to ask me about. If they own a business, they know straight away whether that's them. If they don't, they might know someone it fits.

## Why "I'm a life coach" doesn't work

If you freeze when someone asks what you do, it's not your fault. There are two answers you hear from coaches all the time, and neither helps much.

The first is the job title. "I'm a life coach." "I'm a mindset coach." The trouble is that the other person hears the word coach and fills in the rest themselves, usually a gym instructor shouting at someone, or a reality TV life coach. Then they nod, say "oh, nice" and ask where you're going on holiday.

The second is the "I help X do Y" line, the one you'll often see on LinkedIn. "I help women step into their power." "I help leaders reach their full potential." The shape of the sentence is fine. What's wrong is that it's so vague it could belong to any coach, which means the listener can't tell whether it's them. So you need an answer that says what you actually do, in words a normal person would use.

## The formula, one line at a time

Here's the formula. I'll build it up one line at a time, and alongside it I'll build an answer for Callum, a leadership coach I've made up for this example.

### Line one: who you help

Start with the people. Pick someone the listener can picture, rather than "everyone who wants to grow". Callum starts with "new managers".

If you're torn between describing a type of person and describing a problem, [the difference between a niche and an audience](/blog/niche-versus-audience) will help you decide which one to lead with.

### Line two: what they come to you with

This is the problem, and it needs to be in the words your clients use, not the words from your training course. When we gathered what people write about these problems in their own words, the leadership buyers said things like "my teammates don't respect me and don't listen to me" and "How do I run my team smoothly as a newly-promoted manager?". People burning out at work wrote "I worked too hard for too long. I feel like no amount of rest will help".

None of the leadership buyers put it as "reach my full potential". They talked about their team not listening. So Callum adds: "come to me when they've been promoted over their old workmates and the team won't listen to them."

### Line three: what you do about it

Say what you actually do, with a plain verb. Talk them through it, sit down with them, help them plan it, tell them the truth. Callum says: "I help them handle the hard conversations."

Keep it to one thing. If you list everything you could possibly help with, the listener remembers none of it.

### Line four: what changes for them

Finish with something the client would notice in their own life. Pick a change you could see from the outside. "Transformation" doesn't count. Callum ends with: "so they stop dreading Monday mornings."

### Put together

Here's the formula as one sentence you can fill in:

> "[Who you help] come to me when [the problem, in their words]. I [what you do], so [what changes for them]."

And here's Callum's (made up):

> "New managers come to me when they've been promoted over their old workmates and the team won't listen to them. I help them handle the hard conversations, so they stop dreading Monday mornings."

Two tests before you use it. Could a 13-year-old repeat it back to you? And would the person you're talking to know whether it's them, or think of someone it fits? If the answer to both is yes, you've got your answer.

## Testing it in five places

The same answer has to work in very different places. Some give you a whole conversation, some give you one line. Here's how it stretches and shrinks.

| Place | How long | What you keep |
|---|---|---|
| A barbecue | One or two sentences, spoken | All four parts, said casually |
| LinkedIn headline | One line | Who you help and the problem |
| Top of your homepage | A headline and one line under it | The problem first, then who, what and the change |
| Email signature | A few words | Who you help and the problem |
| 30-second networking round | About 30 seconds, spoken | All four parts, a short example and who you'd like to meet |

### 1. At a barbecue

This is the full sentence, said like you'd say anything else at a barbecue. Say it, then stop. Don't follow it with a list of packages, and don't hand over a business card unless they ask for one.

What happens next is the important bit. If they say "my brother's just been made a manager and he hates it", ask about their brother like a normal person would. Don't switch into coach mode and start asking about their biggest challenge. It feels like a script, and people back away.

### 2. Your LinkedIn headline

This is where the answer shrinks the hardest. We scored more than 11,000 coaches' LinkedIn headlines out of 10, and the average was about 2.6. 420 of them said "Executive Coach" and nothing else, and another 364 just said "Coach".

So the headline drops line three and line four and keeps who you help and the problem. Callum's (made up) would be:

> "Leadership coach for new managers whose old workmates won't listen to them"

It's longer than "Executive Coach", and that's fine. A new manager scrolling past will read it and think, hang on, that's me. If you want to go further with your profile, [how to get coaching clients on LinkedIn](/blog/coaching-clients-on-linkedin) takes one apart from top to bottom, and [our guide to social media for coaches](/blog/social-media-marketing-for-coaches) explains why your headline is the bit people see first, next to every comment and post you make.

### 3. The top of your homepage

On a homepage, you get a headline, a line underneath and a button. Someone arriving from Google has a problem and wants to know if they're in the right place, so here I'd turn the formula round and put the problem first.

When we checked nearly 11,000 coaching websites, about half didn't make it clear who they help or what with. That's the job this part of the page has to do before anything else. Callum's (made up) would look like this:

> **Promoted over your old workmates, and the team won't listen?**
> I coach new managers through the hard conversations, so they stop dreading Monday mornings.
> [Book a free 20-minute call]

A stranger can read that in a few seconds and know whether to stay. If you want to check your own page, [the five second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how.

### 4. Your email signature

Your email signature goes out on every email you send, including the ones to your plumber and your kid's school. People read it without meaning to.

Keep who you help and the problem, and leave the rest. Callum's (made up):

> Callum Price
> Coaching for new managers whose team won't listen

Leave out the inspirational quote, the six sets of letters after your name and the three logos. One plain line does more.

### 5. A networking round where you get 30 seconds

This is where the answer stretches. Thirty seconds is only four or five sentences, which is enough for the full sentence, one short example and a clear ask about who you'd like to meet.

Here's Callum's (made up, and any client story you use needs the client's permission):

> "I'm Callum. New managers come to me when they've been promoted over their old workmates and the team won't listen to them. I help them handle the hard conversations, so they stop dreading Monday mornings. Last year I worked with a warehouse supervisor whose old mates kept going over their head. Three months later they were running the team meetings themselves. If you know anyone who's just been promoted and is finding it hard, I'd love an introduction."

The ask at the end is the part people forget. A room full of business owners can't help you if they don't know who to send your way. "Anyone who wants to be happier" gives them nothing to go on. "Anyone who's just been promoted and is finding it hard" makes them think of someone straight away.

## Words that ruin a good answer

Even with the formula, a few things creep in and spoil it. Watch out for these.

The first is using words the listener has to ask about, like somatic, alignment, nervous system regulation or high performance. If the person at the barbecue has to say "what does that mean?", you've lost them, and they're too polite to tell you.

The second is leading with your training. Your qualifications help later, when someone is deciding whether to trust you. At the start, nobody cares what course you took. They want to know whether you can help with their problem.

The third is trying to cover everything. "I do life coaching, career coaching, relationships and a bit of business coaching too." I understand why people do it, because nobody wants to turn work away. But an answer that fits everyone doesn't make anyone think of themselves.

The fourth is talking about yourself instead of them. Look at the four lines again. Three of them are about the client and only one is about you, and that's the right balance.

## FAQ

### How do I describe what I do as a coach in one sentence?

Say who you help, what they come to you with, what you do and what changes for them. For example (made up): "New managers come to me when their team won't listen. I help them handle the hard conversations, so they stop dreading Monday mornings."

### What should I say when someone asks what I do as a life coach?

Skip the title and describe the problem you help with. "I'm a life coach" makes people guess. "People who've just separated come to me when they don't know where to start again" (a made-up example) tells them straight away.

### Should my answer be the same everywhere?

The idea underneath should be the same, but the length changes. A LinkedIn headline or email signature keeps who you help and the problem. A barbecue or a networking round gets all four parts.

### How long should my answer be at a networking event?

If you get 30 seconds, that's four or five sentences. Use the full sentence, add one short example, and finish by saying who you'd like to be introduced to.
