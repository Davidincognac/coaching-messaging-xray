I look at a lot of coaching websites. And relationship coaching websites tend to look the same as each other. A couple laughing on a beach. A headline about love. A grid of four services. A contact form that wants to know everything about you before you've even said hello.

None of that is terrible. But think about who's actually reading it. Somebody who found messages on their partner's phone last night. Somebody whose marriage has gone so quiet they can hear the clock. They're not browsing. They're hurting, they're worried someone will see what they're looking at, and they'll leave the second the page doesn't feel right.

So instead of a list of tips, I'm going to do what I'd do with a client. I'll take one website and go through it from the top, section by section, saying what's wrong and what I'd change. The website's made up. The coach is called Emma, and she's good at what she does. Her website just doesn't show it.

## The coach behind the website

First, what Emma actually does, because her website doesn't say. She works with couples where one of them has had an affair and they've decided to try to stay together. She's been through it herself. She's trained, she's good, and her clients do well.

That's a real group of people. When we looked at the books people buy about relationships and what they write about them, getting over an affair had 21 books. People wrote things like "my wife cheated on me and I don't know what to do" and "partner of 7 years has been cheating for 3 years". And when they'd decided to stay, they asked "what steps are crucial for rebuilding trust after infidelity in a marriage?" Those are Emma's people. Now let's see if her website would find them.

## Section 1: the headline

**What's there now:**

> **Love Deeply. Live Fully.**
> Relationship coaching for individuals and couples.

**What the visitor thinks:** nothing. It could be a dating app, a wedding planner or a candle shop. Someone who found out about an affair three days ago reads "Love Deeply. Live Fully." and feels a bit sick, then goes back to Google.

We looked at 10,954 coaching websites, and only 14.3% could be understood by a stranger within five seconds. Emma's isn't one of them.

**What I'd change it to:**

> **You've found out about the affair. You've both decided to try. Now what?**
> I help couples rebuild trust after infidelity, step by step, with someone who's been where you are.

It's not pretty, and it isn't meant to be. It names exactly where the reader is, in words they'd use, and it tells them straight away that this is for them. Someone who isn't in that situation will leave, and that's fine. Emma doesn't want them. [The five-second test](/blog/five-second-test) shows you how to check your own headline.

## Section 2: the picture

**What's there now:** a stock photo of a young couple laughing on a beach, wind in their hair.

**What the visitor thinks:** that's not us, and it might never be again. When you're in the middle of a crisis, a picture of a perfect couple doesn't give you hope. It shows you what you've lost.

**What I'd change it to:** a real photo of Emma. Not a glamour shot, just her, looking like someone you'd be happy to sit across from. People are hiring a person to talk to about the worst thing that's happened to them. They want to see who that person is.

## Section 3: the services grid

**What's there now:** four boxes. Couples Coaching. Individual Coaching. Relationship Breakthrough Intensive. Workshops.

**What the visitor thinks:** which one am I? Is an "intensive" for me? What's the difference between couples coaching and a breakthrough? When somebody's upset, every decision you ask them to make is another reason to leave.

**What I'd change it to:** one clear offer, explained in plain words.

> **Rebuilding after an affair: 12 weeks, together**
>
> Ten sessions over twelve weeks, for both of you. We'll go through what happened, what each of you needs to feel safe again, and how to rebuild trust in a way you can both see. Between sessions you'll have simple things to try at home, and you can message me if something blows up.
>
> If your partner isn't ready to come yet, you can start on your own.

That last line does a lot of work, and I'll come back to it.

Emma can still do workshops and individual sessions. They just don't need to be on the front page. One clear thing beats four vague ones.

## Section 4: the About bit

**What's there now:** a list of Emma's qualifications, three accreditations and a sentence about being "passionate about helping couples thrive".

**What the visitor thinks:** fine, she's trained. But does she get it?

**What I'd change it to:** two or three sentences of Emma's own story, said simply. Something like: "Eight years ago I found out my husband had been having an affair. We nearly didn't make it. We did, and now I help other couples who've decided to try."

That's all it needs. Keep the qualifications, but put them underneath. When we looked at what people buying coaching respond to, authority came up in 87.9% of the 918 types of buyer we mapped. That's the sense that you've seen their problem before. Having been through it yourself is the strongest version of that there is. If you want more on telling your story well, [the how to become a relationship coach post](/blog/how-to-become-a-relationship-coach) has a full before-and-after About page.

## Section 5: the testimonials

**What's there now:** "Emma saved our marriage! Thank you so much. – J & K"

**What the visitor thinks:** nice, but it could be anyone, and it could be made up.

**What I'd change it to:** stories that name what changed, still with initials or first names, because nobody wants their affair on a stranger's website.

- "Six months ago I couldn't be in the same room as him without checking his phone. Now I go days without even thinking about it." – *S, 44*
- "I thought I'd ruined everything. Emma helped me understand why it happened and how to actually earn back her trust, not just say sorry." – *M, 51*

Notice the second one is from the person who had the affair. That's on purpose. They're often the one searching, and they need to know they won't be judged either.

Two thirds of coaching websites show no proof at all, so Emma's ahead just by having some. But specific beats general every time. Always ask permission, and always check exactly how each person wants to be named.

## Section 6: the contact form

This is where I'd make the biggest change, and it's the one that's special to relationship coaching.

**What's there now:** a form asking for your name, your partner's name, both email addresses, your phone number, how long you've been together, and a big empty box saying "Tell me about your relationship challenges."

**What the visitor thinks:** three things. That's a lot to write to a stranger. Why do you want my partner's email? And what if they see this?

**What I'd change it to:** first name and one email address. That's it. Then three short lines underneath:

> You can start on your own. I won't contact your partner unless you ask me to.
>
> I'll reply within one working day, from an email that just says "Emma", with nothing in the subject line about coaching or relationships.
>
> If you'd rather talk first, book a free 20-minute call, and you choose the time.

## Section 7: the bit nobody thinks about, which is privacy

Following on from the form, this deserves its own section, because I hardly ever see relationship coaches talk about it.

The person reading your website might share a laptop, a phone bill or a bank account with the very person they're worried about. So think about every trace your business leaves behind:

- **Your emails.** Does your reply have "Couples Coaching" in the subject line? Change it.
- **Your newsletter.** Does filling in the form sign them up automatically? Don't. A "Your Relationship Tips This Week" email turning up in a shared inbox can cause real trouble.
- **Their bank statement.** What name shows up when they pay you? If it's "Affair Recovery Coaching Ltd", that's a problem. Use your own name or a plain business name.
- **Pop-ups.** A big pop-up shouting about "saving your marriage" on a shared screen is the last thing they need.

Then say on your website that you've thought about it. "I keep things discreet. Nothing I send you will say what it's about." For someone who's nervous, that one line can be the reason they get in touch.

## Section 8: safety

There's one more thing every relationship coach website needs, and Emma's doesn't have it.

Some of the people who find you won't be safe at home. What they're describing as a "difficult relationship" might be abuse. Coaching isn't the right help for that, and a coach shouldn't try to work with a couple where one of them is being controlled or hurt.

So add a short, calm line, somewhere easy to find: if you don't feel safe at home, coaching isn't the right first step, and here's who can help. Then point to the domestic abuse helpline in your country. It costs you nothing, and it could be the line that helps someone more than anything else you write.

## Section 9: the price

**What's there now:** "Investment available on request."

**What the visitor thinks:** that means expensive. And I'll have to talk to someone before I even know.

In our research, 82.3% of coaching websites didn't show a price, so Emma's far from alone. But for someone already anxious, not knowing the price is one more reason to put it off. Either show it, "12 weeks, from £X", or at least give a range and say what's included. [The coaching pricing post](/blog/coaching-pricing) goes through how to make either choice work.

## What Emma's homepage looks like now

Put together, from the top:

1. A headline that names the affair and the decision to stay.
2. A real photo of Emma.
3. One offer, in plain words, saying you can start alone.
4. Two or three sentences of her own story.
5. Specific testimonials, from both sides.
6. A short contact form, with the privacy lines underneath.
7. A safety line.
8. A price, or at least a range.

Nothing on that list needs a fancy design or an expensive developer. It needs Emma to be clear about who she helps, and to think about what it actually feels like to be the person reading. ## FAQ

### What should a relationship coach put on their homepage?

A headline that says exactly who you help and with what, a real photo of you, one clear offer, a short version of your story, specific testimonials, a simple contact form and a price or price range. If you work with couples, say clearly that one person can start on their own.

### Should a relationship coach website show testimonials if clients want privacy?

Yes. Use initials, first names or ages instead of full names, and focus on what changed rather than who they are. Always get permission, and check exactly how each person wants to be named before you publish anything.

### How do I keep things private for clients on a relationship coaching website?

Keep your contact form short, never sign people up to emails automatically, use plain subject lines and a plain business name on bank statements, and say on your website that you keep things discreet. The person reading may share a device or an account with their partner.
