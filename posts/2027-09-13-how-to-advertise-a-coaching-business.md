To advertise a coaching business, put your ad where your buyer already goes to ask for help, and aim it at one kind of person, using the words that person uses. Then sell them something small and cheap, rather than asking a stranger to book a coaching call. The money from those small sales pays back what the ads cost, and the people who buy become the people you offer coaching to later. Think of it as a chain with five links. If any one link breaks, the ad loses you money.

![A man working on an ad on his laptop with a phone beside him](how-to-advertise-a-coaching-business.jpg)

I've run ads, and I'm running one right now on Facebook, selling a small, low-priced offer.

One thing before we start. If you're brand new and nobody knows you yet, ads probably aren't your first step. Ads need time. They don't reach the right people straight away, so coaches need to think long term. If you need a client this month, [getting coaching clients on Facebook](/blog/coaching-clients-on-facebook) without paying for ads is a better place to start.

## Link one: the platform

The platform is where your ad appears, so Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, Google, YouTube and so on. The right one is wherever your buyer goes when they want help with their problem.

For the coaching areas I work in, Facebook seems to work better for me than other places. I didn't pick it because it was the easiest to use. I picked it because my buyers were asking questions there. It's all about where the person goes to find help.

### How it breaks

It breaks when a coach picks the platform they like, or the one a course told them to use. In my opinion, somebody whose marriage has just ended is unlikely to say so on LinkedIn, where their boss can see it. They might ask about it in a Facebook group, or type it into Google late at night.

### How to check yours

Go and look for your buyers asking for help, in their own words. Search the platform for the problem you help with and see whether real people are asking about it there. If you find people asking questions, that's your platform. If all you find is other coaches, the buyers are somewhere else.

## Link two: the person the ad talks to

Your ad is always talking to somebody. What you need to check is whether it's the person you want as a client.

### How it breaks

I'll tell you what I see. When I scroll, the only coach ads I really get are business coach ads, and they're not even aimed at me properly. They're all aimed at newbies, and I'm experienced. The ad is written for someone starting out, and that isn't me.

That's where it breaks: the ad is talking to someone other than the person reading it, usually because the coach wrote it for "anyone who wants to grow" instead of one kind of person in one situation.

If you're a business coach and your buyers really are new coaches, write to them as new. Here's how a new coach put it in our research: "I am a new life coach. What is the best way to get new clients and make a living coaching?"

### How to check yours

Write one sentence about the person your ad is for. Say where they are right now and what's gone wrong. Then read your ad and ask whether that person would think "this is for me" or "this is for someone a bit like me". If it's the second, it's broken. Our guide on [marketing for business coaches](/blog/marketing-for-business-coaches) looks at this from the business coach's side.

## Link three: the ad itself, in the buyer's words

This is the picture and the words people see. Its job is to make the right person stop and think "that's me".

### How it breaks

It breaks when the ad uses coach words. Phrases like "step into your power" and "transform your life" sound fine to coaches, but buyers don't talk like that.

Look at how people going through a divorce actually describe it. We looked at 81 books on relationships and divorce, and at what the people who bought them wrote. One wrote "What do I do after divorce life becomes blank? I don't understand from where to start". Another asked "How do I get over my failed marriage and move on?" Neither of them asked to be transformed. One wanted to know where to start, and the other wanted to know how to move on.

So here's a made-up ad for a made-up divorce coach, just to show the difference:

> Bad: "Step into your new chapter with my transformational coaching programme."

> Better: "Life after a divorce can feel blank, and plenty of people say they don't know where to start. This short guide gives you the first five things to sort out, in order. £9."

### The rules on what an ad can say

Ad platforms have their own rules. Facebook and Instagram don't allow ads that say or suggest they know something personal about the person seeing them, such as their health (including mental health), their money worries, their religion or their sexuality. So an ad that asks "Are you struggling with anxiety?" or "Drowning in debt?" can be turned down, because it suggests the platform knows that about you. Weight loss ads have extra rules too, including around before and after pictures. Google has its own rules for anything to do with health.

So write about the situation the person is in, and don't label them. For example, write "after a divorce, lots of people don't know where to start", not "are you anxious?"

Then there's the law. In the UK, ads have to follow the advertising code that the Advertising Standards Authority enforces, which means no misleading claims and evidence for what you promise. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission says ads must be truthful and claims must be backed up, and that includes claims about how much money people will make. A coach should never promise to cure or treat anything, because coaches can't diagnose or treat. The platforms change their rules often, so read the current ones yourself before you spend anything, and check the rules where you live.

### How to check yours

Put your ad next to the real words your buyers use, the ones you found when you checked link one. Count how many of their words are in your ad. If the answer is none, rewrite it.

## Link four: the self-liquidating offer

A self-liquidating offer is a small, low-priced product that you sell through the ad. It might be a short guide, a workbook, a recorded workshop or a small online course. The money from those sales pays back what you spent on the ads. So the ads pay for themselves, or close to it, and the people who buy aren't strangers any more. They've paid you a little, they've got your product, and you can offer them coaching later.

Compare that with the usual coach ad, which asks a stranger to book a free call. In my opinion very few strangers will book a call with someone they've never heard of. A free download is a step better, and our [lead magnet ideas for coaches](/blog/lead-magnet-ideas-for-coaches) shows how to do one properly. But when you advertise a free download, you pay for every person who gets it, while a small paid product brings money back in.

### A made-up example

Fiona is a made-up divorce coach, and these are simple numbers just to show how the sums work. She writes a £9 guide called "Where to start after a divorce". She spends £100 on Facebook ads over two weeks, and 12 people buy the guide.

- Ad spend: £100
- Sales: 12 guides at £9 each, which is £108
- What she's got at the end: her £100 back with £8 over, plus 12 people who've paid her and read her work

Those 12 people know her name and have read her way of thinking, and some of them will want more help.

Now say a different week goes badly and only 5 people buy. That's £45 back on £100, so she's lost £55. That's still useful, because it tells her one of the links is broken. Real life also has payment fees and maybe tax to take off.

### How it breaks

It breaks when the product is too expensive for someone who's never heard of you, or so vague nobody knows what they're getting, or when it solves a problem the buyer doesn't care about yet. The guide has to be about the thing they want sorted first. What you think they ought to want can come later.

### How to check yours

Ask whether a stranger would understand what they get in one sentence, and whether the price feels like a small, easy yes. Then check the product leads somewhere. A guide on "where to start after a divorce" leads naturally to coaching on what comes next. A guide on something unrelated to your coaching doesn't.

## Link five: what happens after they buy

This is everything after the payment goes through. That covers the thank-you page, the email with the product in it, the emails after that, and the point where you offer them coaching.

### How it breaks

In my opinion this is where a lot of the money gets wasted. Someone buys, gets a download link and then hears nothing ever again. Or they get the product and the very next email is a hard sell for a big coaching package, before they've even opened what they bought.

### How to check yours

Buy your own product, using a different email address, and see what happens. Did the product turn up straight away? Was it easy to open? Over the next week or two, did you get emails that were useful and written in the buyer's words? And when the coaching offer came, was it clear what it was, who it's for and what changes for them? If the offer itself is woolly, [how to write a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) goes through it with a before and after example.

Give this link time. People who buy a small guide won't all want coaching at once. Some will want it within weeks and some months later. If you judge the whole thing on the first week, you might switch it off before it's had a chance to pay.

## How to find the broken link

When an ad isn't working, it's tempting to change the picture or headline first, because that's easiest. But the break could be anywhere, so check the links in order. If nobody clicks, look at links one to three. If people click but don't buy, look at link four. If they buy but never become clients, look at link five. Change one thing at a time, so you know which change made the difference.

## FAQ

### How much should a coach spend on ads?

Only what you can afford to lose while you test. With a self-liquidating offer, the aim is for sales to pay the ads back, but you won't know if yours does until you try it with a small amount first.

### What is a self-liquidating offer in coaching?

It's a small, low-priced product, like a short guide or workbook, sold through your ad. The money from those sales pays back what the ads cost, and the people who buy become people you can offer coaching to later.

### Which platform is best for advertising coaching?

Wherever your buyers go to ask for help with their problem. For my niches Facebook seems to work better, because that's where my buyers were asking questions. For yours it might be Google or somewhere else.

### Can I mention health problems in a coaching ad?

Be very careful. Facebook and Instagram don't allow ads that suggest they know someone's health, including mental health, and coaches can't diagnose or treat anything. Read the current rules and check the rules where you live.
