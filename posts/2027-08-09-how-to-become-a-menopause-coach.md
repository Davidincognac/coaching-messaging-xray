The hard part of becoming a menopause coach is knowing which problems are yours and which belong to a GP. A menopause coach helps people with the life around the menopause, like work, home, confidence and daily habits, and helps them get more out of their time with the doctor. A menopause coach doesn't diagnose anything, doesn't treat anything and doesn't tell anyone whether to take HRT. You don't need a licence to do it, because coaching isn't regulated in the UK or the US. You do need proper training with plenty of practice, insurance, the basic admin and a group of people you want to help.

![Two women in their fifties walking and talking on a country footpath](how-to-become-a-menopause-coach.jpg)

Of the 12 client requests sorted below, numbers 4, 5 and 7 are copied exactly from what people wrote in our research, and the rest are made-up examples.

## The coach pile

**1. "I've lost my confidence at work."** Coach. This is about how they see themselves and what they do next, and that's what coaching is for.

**2. "Help me talk to my boss about it."** Coach. You can help them work out what to say, what to ask for and how to open the conversation. If it turns into a dispute, it moves to the third pile.

**3. "My partner doesn't get it."** Coach. You can help them plan what to say at home. If the relationship is in real trouble, a couples counsellor may be the better fit.

**4. "I want to be me again".** Coach. That's a real quote, and it's about goals, routines and getting their life back on track, which is coaching work, as long as the medical side is being looked after by their doctor.

**5. "What do you do when your doctors do not listen to your concerns?"** Coach, for the preparing part. This one is a real quote too. You can't tell them what's wrong, but you can help them write down what's been happening, list their questions and decide what they want out of the appointment.

## The doctor or GP pile

**6. "Should I go on HRT?"** Doctor. This is a medical decision about their body and their health history, and only a doctor can make it with them. You can help them get their questions ready.

**7. "Can menopause cause anxiety and panic attacks?"** Doctor. This is another real quote. Working out what's causing a symptom is a medical job, and anxiety and panic can have more than one cause. If someone tells you they feel low day after day, or talks about not wanting to be here, send them to their GP or emergency help straight away. In the UK that's 999 or NHS 111, or Samaritans on 116 123. In the US call or text 988.

**8. "I can't sleep."** Doctor first. Poor sleep can come from lots of things, so they should get it checked. Once the GP has had a look, a coach can help with the habits around it, like the evening routine and how they plan a day after a bad night.

## The someone else pile

**9. "Plan my meals."** A dietitian. In the UK "dietitian" is a protected title, so only someone properly registered can use it. A coach can help someone stick to changes they've decided on, but a meal plan built around their health is a job for a qualified person.

**10. "My employer won't change anything and I think I'm being treated unfairly."** HR or an employment adviser. In the UK, Acas gives free advice on problems at work, and an employment solicitor can advise on rights. In the US it's an employment lawyer. Employment law is different in each country and each US state, so check the rules where you live, and never tell a client what they're entitled to.

**11. "Can I take this supplement with my medication?"** A pharmacist or their GP. One person in our research talked about being recommended essential oils by "someone at a networking event". A coach shouldn't be suggesting supplements or oils to someone on medication. That's a question for a pharmacist.

**12. "I think a lot of how I feel goes back years, to my childhood."** A registered or accredited therapist or counsellor. In the UK that means someone on a professional register such as BACP or UKCP, and in the US a licensed therapist. Check the rules where you live. Coaching looks forward. Old pain that keeps coming back needs someone trained to work with it.

## What the piles tell you a menopause coach is

Look at the coach pile again. Every one of those requests, from confidence at work to getting ready for the GP, is about a conversation, a decision or a habit. None of them need a medical qualification, but all of them need someone who can ask good questions and keep a person moving.

The other two piles are where new coaches can get themselves into trouble. It's tempting to tell a struggling client what worked for you. But whether to go on HRT, or whether something is the menopause at all, isn't yours to answer. You're there to help them get the best out of the people who can answer it.

I'd put this on your website and in your client agreement in plain words. Something like "I'm a coach. I don't diagnose or treat anything, and I'll always point you to your GP for anything medical." In my opinion people trust you more when you say that up front.

## Why "clarity" won't get you clients

I see this everywhere in coaching, and menopause coaching is no different. "Clarity" is not the answer.

You want clarity? Well, there are 360 directions you can take. Clarity doesn't give a process. It's too vague, it blends in with all the rubbish, and it's great for getting you hidden, because everyone uses it like a necessary buzzword.

When we collected the language coaches use in this area, we found lines like "reclaim your resilience, restful sleep, optimal energy and metabolism" and "THRIVE in perimenopause rather than simply endure it". Coaches who work with people in midlife wrote things like "clarity comes from movement, not more thinking". You could swap any of those onto plenty of other coaches' websites and nobody would notice.

Now look at how the people going through it talk. These are copied exactly from our research:

- "How do I tackle perimenopause when all I feel is exhausted?"
- "my brain feels like cotton wool"
- "I don't know who I am anymore"
- "I'd go from 0-100 in a millisecond, feel totally out of control with my emotions and would get so angry my body would shake"

Not one of them asks for clarity. They describe what's happening to them, in ordinary words, and that's what you should do on your website.

So what do you say instead? Say who you help, what situation they're in, and what you'll actually do together. Take that first coach line from our research:

> "reclaim your resilience, restful sleep, optimal energy and metabolism"

Here's a made-up rewrite of it:

> "Your brain feels like cotton wool, you've snapped at someone you love again, and you're dreading Monday's meeting. I help women in perimenopause plan their working week, get ready for their GP appointment and talk to their boss about what they need."

The first one could be anyone. The second one tells the reader you understand what their week looks like, and it tells them what happens if they book.

## Who to help: at work, at home or with health habits

"Women going through the menopause" is a big group, and if you try to talk to all of them at once you'll sound like everybody else. In my opinion you're better off picking one part of their life. Here are three you can choose from, with what we found in our research.

**At work.** This is about confidence, managing a job while feeling exhausted, and having the conversation with a manager. We found 6 books on menopause at work and leadership. You stay on the coaching side, and when it becomes a question of rights, you point them to HR, Acas or an employment lawyer.

**At home.** This is about partners who don't understand, rows that come out of nowhere and feeling like a different person in your own house. It overlaps with relationship coaching, so be clear about where you stop and a couples counsellor starts.

**Health habits.** This is sleep routines, moving more and sticking to changes. We found 8 books on sleep through perimenopause and menopause and 7 on menopause weight gain and metabolism. This is the area closest to health coaching, and [how to become a health coach](/blog/how-to-become-a-health-coach) covers the limits there. You help with the habits. The GP checks the symptoms, and a dietitian handles the meal plans.

On menopause and women's hormones we found 40 books and 21 buyer quotes. Perimenopause and early symptoms on its own had 22 books. Midlife reinvention had 68 books, so if you find you're more drawn to people rebuilding their lives in midlife than to the menopause itself, that might be your area instead. [How to choose a coaching niche](/blog/how-to-choose-a-coaching-niche) will help you decide.

## Setting up so you stay in the coach pile

**Training.** You don't legally need a certificate, but coaching training with lots of practice on real people and someone giving you feedback is what makes you good at the first pile. Learn the basics about the menopause from reliable medical sources too, so you can spot what belongs in the doctor pile. Knowing about it isn't the same as being allowed to advise on it, and a course that tells you otherwise is one to walk away from.

**A referral list.** This is the third pile written down: a GP practice or menopause clinic, a registered dietitian, a registered therapist or counsellor, a pharmacist and an employment adviser. When a request belongs to someone else, you'll have a name ready instead of guessing.

**A client agreement.** This puts the piles in writing before you start. Say what coaching is, that you don't diagnose or treat anything, and that you'll point them elsewhere for anything medical or legal. It stops the client expecting the wrong thing, and it protects you if they later say you should have done more.

**Insurance.** This is for when something goes wrong anyway. Professional indemnity insurance covers you if a client says your coaching or advice cost them money or harmed them. Check exactly what your policy covers, and tell the insurer you work in a health-related area, because some insurers treat that differently.

**Tax.** You'll need to tell the tax office you're working for yourself, which is HMRC in the UK. In the US the rules depend on your state as well as the IRS, so check the rules where you live.

**Make a proper offer.** Decide what someone actually gets, how long it lasts and what it costs. [How to build a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) walks through that.

**Then sort out your website.** We looked at 622 health, nutrition, wellness and fitness coaching websites, and none of them scored strongly. [What to put on a wellness coach website](/blog/wellness-coach-website) shows you how to do better, starting with the words your clients use, not yours.

## FAQ

### Do I need a qualification to become a menopause coach?

Not by law. Anyone in the UK or the US can call themselves a coach. Good coaching training with real practice is still worth doing, and you should learn enough about the menopause to know when someone needs their GP.

### Can a menopause coach give advice about HRT?

No. Whether to take HRT is a medical decision for the person and their doctor. A coach can help someone prepare questions for the appointment, but shouldn't recommend for or against any treatment.

### Is a menopause coach the same as a dietitian?

No. In the UK "dietitian" is a protected title, so only someone who is registered can use it. A coach can help someone stick to habits they've chosen, but meal plans built around someone's health belong with a dietitian.

### Do I need to have been through the menopause myself?

No, but if you have, it can help, as long as you remember everyone's experience is different. Listen to how your clients describe it, not how you remember it.
