The way to sell coaching without feeling pushy is to stop trying to talk people into it and start finding out whether you can actually help them. You ask good questions, you listen properly, you say your price out loud like you mean it, and then you let the person decide. If you can't help them, you tell them. That's it, and none of it needs a script that makes you squirm.

![A coach listening on a video call, chin on hand, while the person on screen talks](how-to-sell-coaching-video-call.jpg)

One thing to be clear on first. The sale itself happens in a conversation, usually a call. Getting people to that conversation in the first place is a separate job, and if nobody's asking to talk to you yet, start with [how to get coaching clients](/blog/how-to-get-coaching-clients). This post is about what you do once they're in front of you.

That might sound too simple, so here's where it comes from. I've sold a lot of things in my life. Double glazing, cars, recruitment by cold call, event tickets and consultancy. Then in the coaching world I sold book publishing, masterminds and international retreats, and I spoke directly to the bulk of the people who bought.

If selling makes you feel a bit sick, you're in good company. We looked at 54 books people bought about selling and what those buyers wrote, and the same feelings kept coming up. One wrote "I get nervous when talking to clients... I almost feel bad asking for a sale". Another just wanted to "close a sale without being pushy". These are people who were worried enough about selling to buy a book on it, and they feel exactly what you feel. It's not your fault selling feels uncomfortable, but nobody can say yes to coaching they were never offered.

## Double glazing: the customer decides

Double glazing has a bad name for pushy selling, and some of that is earned.

The thing double glazing really teaches you is that the customer decides. You can do everything right and they still might not want new windows this year. It's their house and their money. Once you really accept that it's their choice, the pushy feeling goes, because you're not trying to win anything. You're just helping them make a good decision, and "no" is one of the good decisions.

The other thing double glazing taught me is that people buy the same thing for completely different reasons. With windows there are four: maintenance, warmth, security, and making the house look nice. Two neighbours can buy identical windows, one because the old frames are rotting and the other because they're scared of being burgled. If you go on about how warm the house will be to the person who's worried about burglars, you've lost them. So my job was to find out which of the four was top of the list for the person in front of me, and talk about that.

Coaching works the same way. Two people can want the same confidence coaching for completely different reasons. One wants to speak up at work, and the other wants to stop feeling like a fraud at the school gate. Find out which it is before you say a word about your programme.

That changes how you start a sales conversation. If the decision is theirs, your job is to help them see their own situation clearly, and the way you do that is by asking, not telling. It's easy to jump on a call and start explaining your programme in the first five minutes. Don't. Start with three questions:

- "What made you want to talk now, rather than six months ago?"
- "What's it costing you to leave it as it is?"
- "What would you want to be different?"

Then listen, and write down the words they use, because they're far better than yours. You'll find that if you can help, it becomes obvious to them without you pushing. If you can't, you'll both know.

## Cars: say the price like you mean it

Anyone who's sold cars knows the moment. You've had the chat, they've had the test drive, and now it's time to say the number. A bad salesperson mumbles it, or wraps it in so many extras the buyer can't tell what it is, or knocks money off before anyone's even asked.

It's easy to do all three in coaching too, like "it's normally this but I can do a special rate", or "well, it depends", or saying the price so softly the person has to ask again. That tells the buyer you don't believe it's worth it, so why should they?

On a forecourt, the price is written on the windscreen and the salesperson says it the same way every time. You can do the same. Here's a made-up example:

> "It's a six-week programme with a call every week and messages in between, and it's £750."

That £750 is invented for this example, not a figure I'm telling you to charge. The point is that you say it once, in a normal voice, and then you stop talking. Anyone who's stood next to a car with a customer knows the first one to speak after the price usually gives something away. So let them be the one to speak next, even if it takes a while.

And don't start discounting to fill the silence. In that same group of book buyers, one wrote about "having to rely on discounts constantly... it's eating into my margin". Knock money off for one person and you'll do it for the next. If someone really can't afford it, change what they get, like fewer sessions, rather than cutting the price of the same thing.

If you're not sure what to charge in the first place, [how to price your coaching](/blog/coaching-pricing) goes through it properly.

## When they say "I need to think about it"

This one is a classic on the car forecourt, and you'll hear it in coaching all the time. The pushy answer is to argue with it, or to make them feel daft for wanting time. Don't.

The thing to know is that "I need to think about it" is hardly ever the real answer. It's a polite cover for something else. On a forecourt, it can turn out to mean things like:

- "I like the car, but I've seen the same one cheaper down the road and I don't want to say so." In coaching, that's someone who's been looking at another coach and is comparing you.
- "My partner will go mad if I come home having spent this." In coaching, that's someone who needs to talk it through at home before they can say yes.
- "I'm not sure this is the right car for me, but you've been nice and I don't want to say no to your face." In coaching, that's someone who doesn't think it'll work for them and is too polite to tell you.

Each one needs a different answer, and you'll only find out which it is if you ask. Here's a line you can use:

> "That's fine. Can I ask, is there a part of it you're not sure about? I'd rather you told me now than went away with a doubt I could have cleared up."

If they're comparing you with someone else, say plainly what you do differently and let them choose. If they need to talk to someone at home, ask what that person will want to know, and answer it. If they don't think it'll work, thank them for being straight.

What you mustn't do is pretend the offer runs out tonight to force a decision. Selling event tickets is a good way to see the difference between a real deadline and a fake one.

## Event tickets: a real date is fine, a fake countdown isn't

When you sell tickets for an event, there's a real date. The event happens on that day, the seats run out, and if you don't buy you don't go. Telling people that is just giving them the facts.

The problem is when coaches borrow that feeling for things that don't have a real date. I mean things like countdown timers on a sales page, "only three spots left" when there are always three spots left, or a price that goes up at midnight and then slips back down the next week. People can smell it.

Our research backs this up. We mapped 918 types of buyer across 117 reasons people hire a coach, and urgency or scarcity helped the decision for fewer than 1 in 20 of them. What helped instead was proof that it works for people like them, which came up for more than 9 in 10.

So if you run a group that starts on a set date, say the date. If you only take a certain number of one-to-one clients because that's all you can handle, say so once and mean it. Otherwise, drop the timers and put your effort into proof.

## Recruitment cold calls: follow up without chasing

I did recruitment by cold call, and in my opinion that kind of work teaches you not to take rejection personally. When you're calling people who didn't ask to hear from you, you get a lot of no's, and you learn that keeping in touch properly gets you much further than hounding people ever will.

There's a big difference between following up and chasing. Following up is doing what you said you'd do. Chasing is messaging every other day with "just bumping this up".

If you agreed to get back in touch, a short phone call or voicemail is often better than another written message, because people can hear that you're relaxed about it. Here's one you can change to suit you:

> "Hi [first name], it's [your name]. I said I'd call today about the coaching we talked about. No need to ring back if you've decided it's not for you. If you've got a question, or you want to go ahead, just send me a text and we'll sort it out."

In my opinion, people are more likely to get back to you when you make it easy for them to say no. If you hear nothing, one more short message a week or two later is plenty, then leave it. If they've agreed to join your email list, they'll still hear from you without you chasing.

## Masterminds and retreats: when to say no

Selling book publishing, masterminds and international retreats is a different thing from selling windows. People are spending real money, often on something abroad, with a group of strangers. In my opinion, they want to know two things before anything else. Has this worked for people like me? And does the person running it know what they're doing?

That's why, in my opinion, proof does more than promises. A short, specific story from a past client does more than any clever line you could write. If you haven't got good testimonials yet, [how to get coaching testimonials people believe](/blog/coaching-testimonials) shows you exactly how to ask for them. And make sure what you're selling is clear before you sell it. If you can't explain your offer in a couple of plain sentences, the sales call will feel pushy because you'll be making it up as you go. [How to put together a coaching offer](/blog/coaching-offer) helps with that.

Saying no isn't only about who you let in. It's also about protecting the people who are already there. On one of the masterminds I ran, there was a woman who tried to take over. She'd talk about her own problems in depth, go off the subject, and run everybody else out of time. I had several private chats with her about respecting the group's time. A mastermind isn't talk therapy, and everyone else had paid for their share of the room too. If you run groups, you'll meet someone like her sooner or later, and the rest of the group is watching to see whether you deal with it.

With something as expensive as a mastermind or a retreat abroad, you need to know when to say no. When you're selling something this big, the worst thing you can do is talk the wrong person into it. They'll be unhappy, and they'll tell people.

So say no when:

- what they need isn't what you do, or someone else could help them better
- they need a doctor, a therapist or another professional, because coaches don't diagnose or treat anything
- they clearly can't afford it and would be borrowing money they haven't got
- they want you to do it all for them, and you can't

If you only take on people you can help, your clients get results, and they're the ones who tell their friends about you.

## FAQ

### How do I sell coaching without sounding salesy?

Ask questions about their problem first and talk about your coaching second. Say your price plainly, let them decide, and be willing to tell people when you're not the right person to help them.

### Should I offer a discount if someone says it's too expensive?

In my opinion, no. If money's tight, offer a smaller version with fewer sessions, or let them pay in instalments. Cutting the price of the same thing tells them it was never worth the first number.

### How many times should I follow up after a sales call?

Do what you agreed on the call, then one more short message a week or two later if you hear nothing. After that, leave it and keep in touch through your emails if they've signed up.

### Do countdown timers help sell coaching?

Our research says not really. Urgency or scarcity helped the decision for fewer than 1 in 20 types of buyer, while proof helped far more. Use a real start date if you have one, and leave the fake timers alone.
