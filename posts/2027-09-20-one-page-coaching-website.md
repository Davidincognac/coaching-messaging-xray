A one-page coaching website needs eight things: who you help, their problem in their own words, what happens when they work with you, proof, a real photo, a short story, a few honest answers to their questions, and a clear booking button. Four more things depend on who your client is: your price, your qualifications, a free download and a contact form. Three things can go: a blog, a long list of services and a row of social media icons at the top. Every one of those decisions should come from what your client expects to see, not from what you'd like to show them.

![Hands holding a phone showing a simple one-page coaching website](one-page-coaching-website.jpg)

First, something you might not expect to read in a post about websites. A coach doesn't need a website. You can get clients without one, and our guide on [getting coaching clients without a website](/blog/coaching-clients-without-a-website) shows how. But a website can be a great help, and a single page is plenty for a lot of coaches. What goes on it depends on your client, so please don't build it around one coach you admire, or around what you want the page to do for you.

Here are 15 things coaches put on their websites, and what I'd do with each one.

## Keep these

### Who you help

Put this at the very top. When someone opens your page, the first thing they're asking is "is this for me?" If they can't answer that in a few seconds, they're gone. "I help people" doesn't answer it. Something like "I help parents of teenagers who've stopped talking to them" does. That's a made-up example, but you can see the difference straight away.

### The problem, in their words

Your client doesn't think in coaching language. They think in the words they'd use to a friend over a coffee. If your page describes their problem the way they'd describe it, they feel understood before they've read anything about you. If it uses words like "transformation" and "alignment", they feel like they're reading an advert for someone else.

The way to find their words is to listen. Read the questions people post in groups and forums, read the reviews they leave on books about their problem, and write down what your past clients said in their first conversation with you.

### What happens when they work with you

People want to picture what they're actually signing up for. How many sessions? Online or in person? How long does each one last? What do they get between sessions? If the page doesn't say, the client is left guessing, and people who are guessing usually don't book.

Say it plainly. "We meet on Zoom once a fortnight for an hour, for three months, and you can message me in between" tells them more than a paragraph about your approach.

### Proof

Your client wants to know you've helped someone like them before. A few specific testimonials from real clients, with their first name and what changed for them, do more than a wall of five-star quotes saying "amazing coach".

If you haven't got any yet, our post on [getting coaching testimonials people believe](/blog/coaching-testimonials) gives you the exact messages to send.

### A real photo

People are hiring a person, so they want to see who they'll be talking to. A proper photo of you, looking like you do on a call, does that job. A stock photo of someone on a mountain top does the opposite. It tells the visitor nothing about you.

### Your story

Your story belongs on the page, but keep it short. When we looked at thousands of coaching websites, 9 in 10 of the best ones had a story on the page, so it's worth having one. The trick is that it needs to be the part of your story your client cares about, which is usually why you understand their problem and how you came to help people with it. Three or four sentences is enough.

### A few answers to their real questions

Have a short FAQ, but only with questions your clients actually ask. Things like "Is this the same as therapy?", "What if I need to miss a session?" or "Can I do this from another country?" If nobody has ever asked you a question, don't put it on the page just to fill space.

That first question comes up a lot, so answer it honestly. Coaching isn't therapy, and a coach doesn't diagnose or treat anything. Saying that plainly helps the right people feel safe to book. Add a line saying who should see a doctor or therapist instead, and it sends everyone else to the right help.

### A booking button

Make it obvious. Your client expects to be told what to do next. One button, with plain words on it like "Book a free 20-minute chat", is better than three different buttons that all do slightly different things. Put it near the top and again at the bottom, because on a phone people scroll a long way and don't want to scroll back up.

## Keep these if your client expects them

### Price

In my opinion this is the one coaches get wrong more than any other. We found that more than 8 in 10 coaching websites show no price at all.

Some clients expect to see a price before they'll talk to anyone. If you're selling a fixed package to people paying out of their own pocket, like a parent paying for six sessions, they want to know if they can afford it before they book. Hiding it just makes them nervous, and some will assume it's too expensive and leave.

Other clients expect a conversation first. A business owner buying a six-month programme may want to talk about what they need before any number comes up. Even then, give them a starting figure, or a line saying you'll talk about price on the first call. What you don't want is a client left guessing.

### Qualifications and logos

If you coach senior managers or business owners, they often expect to see your training and the kind of companies you've worked with, because that's how they judge people in their world. A parent or someone going through a divorce probably doesn't care about the logo of a coaching school they've never heard of.

If your clients do expect it, one line or one row of logos is plenty. Put it after the proof, not before, because what you've done for people like them comes first.

### A free download

This depends on how ready your client is. Some people find you long before they're ready to talk to anybody. They're still reading and working out what's wrong. For them, a short guide they can take away in exchange for their email is a good next step, and it means you can stay in touch.

Other people come to your page because someone recommended you, and they already want to book. For them, a free download just gets in the way. If your clients are mostly referrals, you might skip it. If they mostly find you through searching or social media, it's worth thinking about.

### A contact form

Some people would rather send a message first than book a call with a stranger. That's often true when the problem is personal or a bit embarrassing, and also with business buyers who like to email before they commit to anything. For them, a short form with name, email and one box for a message works well.

If your clients are happy to book straight in, the booking button does the job and a form is just another thing on the page. And if you do collect names and email addresses, in the UK you'll need a privacy notice explaining what you do with them, and many US states expect one too. Check the rules where you live.

## Cut these

### A blog

On a one-page site, leave it out. Someone on your page has come to decide whether to talk to you, not to read articles. A blog also needs regular posts, and a blog with three posts from two years ago tells the visitor you've stopped bothering.

### A long list of services

Your client wants to know you can help with their one problem. When they see a list of twelve things, such as life coaching, career coaching, confidence, relationships, mindset, nutrition and so on, it doesn't look like you can do everything. It looks to them like you're not a specialist in what they need. One offer is ideal, two at a push, and both should be for the same kind of client.

### Social media icons at the top

Cut them from the top of the page. Every icon is a door out of your website, and the person who clicks it is now on Instagram looking at somebody else's holiday photos. They're unlikely to come back.

The exception is if your clients check you out somewhere else before they book, like business buyers looking at your LinkedIn. Then a small link at the very bottom of the page is fine. It shouldn't be the first thing they see.

## Putting it in order

Once you've decided what stays, put it in an order that follows the questions going through your client's head. On a phone they see a small piece at a time, so each piece needs to lead to the next one.

1. Who you help and their problem, in one or two lines in their words, so they know straight away if it's for them.
2. A booking button, for the people who already know they want to talk.
3. What happens when they work with you: sessions, how long, online or in person, and what changes for them.
4. Proof, with two or three specific testimonials from clients like them.
5. Your photo and short story, because now they're interested they want to know who you are.
6. Price, if your clients expect it, or a line on how price is handled.
7. Qualifications and logos, if your clients expect them, kept to one line or one row.
8. FAQs, with three to five real questions with short answers.
9. The booking button again, with a contact form or a free download next to it, if your clients need them.
10. The footer, with a small social link if it helps, and your privacy notice.

That's your whole page. Notice the coach doesn't appear until step five. That's on purpose, because the client cares about their problem first and you second.

## FAQ

### Is a one-page coaching website enough?

For a lot of coaches, yes. If it tells the right person it's for them, shows what working with you looks like, proves you've helped people like them and gives them an easy way to book, it can do the job. If you only have time to build one page, build this one.

### Should I put my prices on my coaching website?

It depends on your client. People paying for themselves for a fixed package often expect to see a price, and hiding it can put them off. People buying bigger programmes may expect a conversation first, but give them a starting figure or say when price will be discussed.

### How long should a one-page coaching site be?

Long enough to cover the ten sections in the order above. On a phone that's plenty of scrolling, and that's fine as long as every section answers something your client wants to know. If a section doesn't, cut it.

### Do I need a blog on my coaching website?

Not on a one-page site. Visitors to that page are deciding whether to talk to you, and an old or empty blog can make you look like you've stopped working. If writing suits you and your clients, think about it when you build a bigger site.
